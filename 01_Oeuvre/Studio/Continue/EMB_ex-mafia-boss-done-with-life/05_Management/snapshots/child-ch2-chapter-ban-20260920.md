# Local Boy Asks For Something. Local Boy Is Charged For It In A Currency He Does Not Keep.

## 2.1

The letter went out on a Sunday at four in the morning, which is a detail he remembers chiefly because of how little happened afterwards. Nothing happened for ten weeks.

He had expected four. He had written down that he expected four, on the back of the third draft and before he burned the third draft, and being wrong about it by six weeks turned out to be the most informative thing that happened to him all winter. A **Sky** who cannot tell how long a thing will take has not finished understanding the thing.

The note had said *nothing for a month, possibly nothing at all, and if there is nothing at all then the answer is no and the no is permanent*. He had felt perfectly calm writing it. He had been eight years old for less than a month by then, and he has a great deal of practice at feeling calm about things in advance. That is not the same skill as being calm. It has never once been mistaken for it by anybody who lives with him.

In the meantime there was the town, and the town does not stop for anybody's correspondence.

**November.** The Ferri question closed itself in the manner of most things in Namimori, which is to say that it stopped being a question and became a man in a good old coat who arrived eleven minutes early for everything. He took the small room behind the shrine office and he offered to do the accounts in his second week. Ayame told him no, politely, and then thought about it for nine days and told him yes.

He also asked, in his fourth week, which of those words applied to him, having heard four of them used in a fortnight and worked out that they were a system. He was told that none of them did. "None!" said Aldo Ferri, delighted, to a woman who had been a **Ketsueki** since before he was born. "In thirty-one years nobody has ever told me I was nothing. Nothing at all! It is restful."

Two households stopped speaking to the household that had voted against admission. Musashi heard about this on a Thursday and put an end to it on the Friday, in a manner that nobody wrote down and that everybody described afterwards as brief. Ferri himself learned nothing of it until the following spring, and when he did learn he went and sat in that family's shop for an hour and bought things he did not need, and neither party ever mentioned the vote.

**Early December.** The Namimori Disciplinary Committee reached forty members and ran out of armbands, which produced a procurement crisis lasting nine days. It was resolved by a boy called Taniguchi whose aunt sews. The new armbands came back very slightly the wrong shade of white, and Kyouya accepted them without a word. Every member of that committee understood in the same instant that the wrong shade was now permanent. It will still be the wrong shade in ten years.

**The fourteenth of December.** Musashi turned seventy-five, and she let them do the thing at the shrine because the town enjoys doing it and because a **Ruriko** who refuses the thing at the shrine causes more work than one who submits to it. She stood through the whole of it with the expression of a woman being measured for something. Afterwards she sat on the step with one cup of the good sake and observed that seventy-five was an ugly number and that seventy-six would be worse.

Tsuna sat down beside her and did not say any of the four things he had thought of saying on the way over. *She was seventy-four in January,* he thought, *and I put her into a plan because of it, and I have not fixed that plan since. The reason I have not fixed it is that fixing it means deciding what she is for, and I have never once managed to finish that sentence.*

And underneath all of it, through the whole of that grey and entirely ordinary six weeks, there was the sheet.

It lived under the loose board in the room with the door that does not close. It was headed **SINCE JANUARY** in capitals, it was ruled into four columns, and by the middle of December it ran to forty-one entries. He added to it on Sunday nights, after the accounts and before bed, and the ritual of it was the steadiest hour in his week.

He had stopped adding to the fourth column in November. In November he had worked out that the fourth column had been measuring the wrong thing for ten months, and he had not worked out what to put there instead. So the column now had a line ruled through the heading, and above it, in his own small hand, the words **WRONG QUESTION**. Below that ran forty-one empty rows. He looked at them every Sunday and did not fill a single one in.

*That is not patience,* he thought, on one of those Sundays, with the lamp down low and the fox asleep against his ankle. *Patience is what you have while you are waiting for a thing to arrive. This is what you have when you have run out of things to do and have decided to call the running out a strategy.*

He measured the core on the first Sunday of every month, because Kawahira had told him to and because the numbers had stopped frightening him some while ago. The **crux** sat where it was supposed to sit. The **soulscape** came in at nine steps, as it had since the winter, and the **Flame** went where he put it and stayed there. *All of which is very satisfactory,* he thought, *and none of which is any use to me, because the thing that is stuck is not the Sky and is not the Cloud and is not in my chest at all. It is in a filing system in another prefecture. What exactly do I do with a wall made of paperwork?*

So he did the accounts, and signed the winter storeroom allocation, and sat through the Taki liaison meeting on the second Tuesday and contributed four sentences, all four of them about buses. He walked past the shrine post box twice a day, going down and coming up. There is a particular way a person walks past a thing they have decided not to look at, and by the third week the priest's wife had noticed him doing it, and by the fourth week she had stopped mentioning it, which was considerably worse.

And then it was the eighth of January. The Committee had a bicycle policy by then, and the sleet had come in off the sea at noon. At nine minutes past two in the afternoon the sky over the north road went the colour of a bruise. Every person in Namimori with any **Flame** in them at all sat up in the same second.

## 2.2

There are four thousand people in Namimori, and something on the order of six hundred of them carry enough of a **Flame** to feel weather in it. Every one of those six hundred felt it come down the valley, and every one of them felt it in the register of their own **Flame**. The **Ketsueki** households, who carry what the rest of the world calls **Storm**, felt it as pressure behind the eyes. The **Chiyu** felt it as cold in the hands, which is what a **Sun** does when it is unhappy. The **Hogo** felt it as a hum in the back teeth, and the **Yasuragi** as the sea going quiet, and a **Rain** that goes quiet is a thing worth watching. Two **Gen'ei** in that town felt it as a lie being told somewhere nearby, and one **Getsufuji** felt it as a thing crossing a line. The **Kotei** of the **Sanctuary** felt all seven of those at once. That is what a **Sky** is for, and it is nothing like as useful as it sounds. Tsuna was in the storeroom with a clipboard and a pencil, and the clipboard was on the ground before he had finished deciding to drop it.

*That is not a Sky,* he thought, already moving. *It is not a Storm and it is not a Cloud and it is not a Mist. It is not Rain, it is not Sun, it is not Lightning. It is not any of the seven, and I have felt all seven of them since I was one year old.*

*So what is the eighth, and why has nobody in four hundred years written it down?*

He was out of the storeroom and up the shrine steps in eleven seconds. Ayame was already at the top with her left sleeve pushed up past the scar, because that is what she does now before anything formal and before a good many things that are not. Her **Ketsueki** was banked and ready and had been since the weather turned. Nagi had come out of the kitchen with flour to the elbow and both hands open, and her **Gen'ei** had gone up over the shrine roof without her asking it to. That had never once happened before in her life. She would be extremely pleased about it in approximately two days, once she had stopped being frightened, and what she actually said at the time, in a small flat voice with her eyes fixed on the north road, was "it went up by itself!"

Ayame heard her and did not take her eyes off the gate. "Then it is yours," she said. "Write the date on the doorframe tonight. Height on the left, **Gen'ei** on the right, and do not let your grandmother tell you the two go in the same column!"

Musashi arrived last, walking, and did not hurry at any point.

"North gate," she said. "One of them. It is not moving."

"How many behind it?"

"One," said Musashi. "That is the part I would like you to look at."

*One,* thought Tsuna, and felt the whole shape of the afternoon change. *A thing that does that to a valley, and it has brought one. An attack brings enough to finish. A message does not need to announce itself in the weather at all, because a message goes in a bag on a bus…*

*…which leaves the third possibility, and the third possibility is that it simply does not know how to arrive any other way, and has not needed to learn.*

The north gate of Namimori is not a gate and has not been one for three hundred years. It is the place where the road stops being the prefectural road and starts being the town's. There is a stone set into the bank there. It has stood in that spot since before the contract was written, and it says nothing at all to anybody, and the whole weight of a four hundred year old **Sanctuary** rests on the line it marks.

The figure was standing on the far side of the stone, in the sleet, and it had not crossed.

It was tall, and it wore black. The top hat had a neat band of bandage wound round the crown in place of a ribbon, and below the hat there was a high collar and a white cravat, and below that there were bandages where the face of a person goes. On a cord around its neck, half inside the coat, hung something small and grey and roughly round. It had the shape of a **pacifier** and the colour of a river stone, and there was no **Flame** in it at all, and there had not been for a very long time.

*That is a dead pacifier,* thought Tsuna, from thirty metres away, and did not examine how he knew what a dead one looked like. *So whatever is standing at my boundary used to be something else. What does a thing like that want with a book?*

It did not move, and it did not look at any of them, and the cold that came off it had nothing whatever to do with January.

Thirty metres back along the road, the Namimori Disciplinary Committee had formed a line across the tarmac. They were nine and ten and eleven years old, in armbands of very slightly the wrong shade of white, and the boy at the front was holding a clipboard with both hands. He was holding it correctly, in the manner the committee has decided is correct, and his hands were shaking hard enough that the clipboard was making a sound.

He was reading off it anyway.

"State your name and the purpose of your visit," he said. "Visitors to Namimori are asked to state their name and the purpose of their visit."

Tsuna stopped walking, some six metres behind him, and felt his entire chest go sideways. *Oh no,* he thought, with a clarity he would have been proud of in any other circumstance. *Oh no. He is doing the procedure.*

"Visitors are reminded," said the boy with the clipboard, to a thing that could have taken the roof off the shrine, "that bicycles may not be left against the torii."

The Vindice turned its head.

A great many people described the next four seconds afterwards, and no two accounts agree on anything except the length. What Tsuna saw was a thing that had not been asked a direct question in a considerable number of years, working out whether it had in fact just been asked one.

"I have no bicycle," it said.

The voice was wrong in a way that took him a second to place. It was not deep and it was not loud. The stresses fell in the wrong places, as though the sentence had been assembled by somebody who had learned where the words go without ever learning where the weight goes, and who had stopped finding the difference interesting several centuries ago.

"Understood," said the boy with the clipboard, and wrote it down.

*He wrote it down,* thought Tsuna. *He has written down that it has no bicycle. That is going into a file. There is going to be a file, and it is going to be kept, and somebody is going to copy it.*

Kyouya came down the road at that point, at his own pace, and stopped at the end of the line. He looked at the thing beside the stone for perhaps two seconds and then turned to the boy with the clipboard.

"Is he staying?" he said.

"He has not said, **Getsufuji**."

"Then ask him."

And that, so far as Hibari Kyouya has ever been concerned, was the entire matter. There was a visitor at the boundary. There is a procedure for visitors at the boundary. The procedure was being followed by a boy who had been trained to follow it, he had exactly one question about how the procedure was going, and the question had been answered. He went and stood at the edge of the road with his arms folded and waited for somebody to do something interesting.

Tsuna watched him do it and was hit by a wave of affection so large and so badly timed that he had to look at the road surface for a moment.

*He has not worked out that it is frightening,* he thought, and then, immediately, *no, that is wrong and it is insulting and I should know better. He has worked out exactly how frightening it is, and he has put the fright somewhere he is not obliged to look at, and he has come back out into the road with the only question in the world that changes what anybody does next. He is ten. What is he going to be at twenty?*

Behind all of this, invisibly, in seven houses, the other thing was happening.

Seven house heads had felt the same weather in the same second, each of them in the register of their own **Flame**. All seven know what a Vindice looks like, because knowing that is part of what it means to hold a **Flame** bond into a **Sanctuary**, and the seven houses of this **Sanctuary** have held theirs for four hundred years. And every one of the seven was sitting in a room with a hand resting on a bond and no idea on earth what to do with it.

The instrument they had all signed says what happens when the **Flame** world comes at a Sanctuary, and it says it in four hundred year old language that is admirably clear. It was written by people who had thought about famiglie, and about houses, and about the ordinary machinery of a world that takes things. It was not written by anybody who had ever once considered the possibility that the thing standing at the boundary might be the Vindice themselves.

The contract makes the Vindice the consequence. It has nothing whatever to say about the Vindice as the arrival.

Musashi told him afterwards that she counted forty minutes before anybody pulled. Nobody pulled. Seven houses sat with their hands on seven bonds for forty minutes and then, one after another, took them off again, and not one of the seven ever wrote down what they had decided or why they had decided it. Tsuna has always privately thought that this was the single most Namimori thing that has ever happened outside Namimori.

He walked down the road to the stone.

*Do not offer him anything,* he thought, going down. *Not tea, not the boundary, not a name, not a chair. He has not come for any of those, and offering is how you find out what you were worth to somebody who was never going to tell you.*

He stopped at the correct distance, which is four paces, and which no adult in his life has ever taught him. He kept his own **Flame** exactly where it was, neither banked nor offered, because a **Sky** that reaches at a stranger is a **Sky** making a proposal.

The Vindice looked down at him for the first time since it had arrived. "You are the sender," it said.

"I am."

It put out one gloved hand, and in the hand was a flat package wrapped in oilcloth and tied with cord. The oilcloth was dry, which it had no business being, because the sleet had been coming in sideways for eleven minutes.

"The Warden has answered," it said. "There are terms. You will read them. If you do not consent, burn the whole of it, and nothing of this will have occurred."

*Nothing of this will have occurred,* thought Tsuna, taking it. *He said that as though it were a procedure and not a threat, and I think he means it as a procedure. I think he is telling me, quite literally, that they will simply stop having come.*

"And if I do consent?"

"Then you will have consented," said the Vindice, "and that is another matter entirely, and it will not be mine."

The package was cold, and the cold went through both gloves in under a second and stayed in his fingers for an hour. The Vindice turned away without any further ceremony. It did not warp and it did not vanish; it walked back up the prefectural road in the sleet at an ordinary walking pace until the road bent behind the pines, and after the bend it was simply not there any more.

The boy with the clipboard was still standing in the line with his pen out and his hands going.

"Sir!" he said, in a voice pitched about three notes too high. "Sir, he did not give a name."

"No. He did not."

"I have to put something in the column."

Tsuna went and looked at the column, because he was eight years old and because the alternative was standing in the road thinking about the weight of the thing in his hands. The column said **NAME**, and beneath it, in a child's careful pencil, were eleven visitors recorded since the start of that winter.

"Put the hat," he said.

So the visitor log of the Namimori Disciplinary Committee, for the eighth of January, records under **NAME** the words *black, feathered band replaced with bandage, neat*, and under **PURPOSE OF VISIT** the single word *delivery*, and under **BICYCLE** the word *no*. It is still in the file. Ayame had it copied twice that same week, and put one copy in the shrine archive and gave the other to the boy who wrote it, which he did not understand at the time and understands now.

## 2.3

He did not open it at the shrine, and he did not open it on the walk up. He took it to the room with the door that does not close and put it on the boards in front of him. Then he sat with his hands flat on his knees and looked at it for some while. The last thing he had opened from that address had been four words long and had cost him three days of his life.

*Right. Before I open it. What am I willing to pay?*

He had made the list in November and he brought it out now in order. Putting things in order is the one thing about himself he has been able to rely on. He has relied on it since he was five and understood that nothing else about him was going to be reliable for a long time.

*One. Money, which I do not personally have, though the shrine does, and which I would therefore have to explain to two people at least.*

*Two. Service. A thing done, once, at a time of his choosing, with no limit stated. Expensive and open ended and I would take it without arguing, and I have been quietly hoping for it since October.*

*Three. Information. I have a very great deal of it, most of it belongs to other people, and I decided in November how much of it is mine to spend. The answer is: the part that is only about me, and nothing else, and I will not be talked upward from that.*

*Four…*

He stopped there, as he had stopped there every time since October, because four was the name. Four had been the name since the afternoon he opened a form from Ise and found a box he could not fill in, and he had spent eleven weeks getting himself to the point where he could write it down if a document required him to.

*Four. Sawada Masaya, aged eight, Namimori, in the prefecture of Mie. And the moment that is in a register anywhere in the world, three years of being nobody are finished. The Estraneo file and the Genoa file and a ceasefire signed on a set of shrine steps all acquire a name, and it will not be my choice when that happens, or who reads it, or what they do with it afterwards.*

*I have made my peace with four. It took eleven weeks, it went badly, and I am not proud of any part of how it went.*

He cut the cord.

Inside the oilcloth was one sheet of something that was not quite paper and was not quite anything else. It was cold to the hand. The writing on it was very small and very even, and had been made by somebody with all the time in the world and no particular interest in using it well.

The first thing he read was the address line, and the address line was not any of the eleven things he had prepared himself for.

It did not say **Sawada Masaya**. It did not say *the sender*, or *the enquirer*, or *the boy in Mie*, or any of the careful anonymous formulas he had spent two months rehearsing his reaction to.

It said:

> **TO THE SKY OF NAMIMORI.**

He sat entirely still for some seconds. Then he put the sheet down on the boards and went and stood at the window, and did the thing he has done since he was three. He worked it through twice before permitting himself to feel any part of it.

*No. Steady. Work it.*

*He has known since the Estraneo letter, and of course he has. I sent him nine pages arguing the right of retaliation of a **Sky**, and I signed it with nothing at all. A man alive for several centuries does not require a signature. There is one **Sky** in this prefecture strong enough to erase a famiglia in a night, and he has known which one since the week it happened. So this is not new information to him. He has simply chosen to write it at the top of a document.*

*And the reason my hands are doing this is not that he knows.*

*Ise has a box I cannot fill in. He has a box that was filled in for me, correctly, by somebody who did not consult me and was never going to. Those two things are the same object wearing different coats, and I have spent three years defending against one of them and never once looked at the other.*

*I am not unwritten. I am unwritten in the registers I can reach. Which ones can reach me? How many of them are there? And how long has this one had a line with my office written at the top of it?*

*Ise has no column for a Sky that a fishing town made by itself. He has one. Which means the thing I have been treating as a boundary is only a boundary in one direction, and the direction it does not hold is the one I have never had to think about.*

He went back and sat down and read the rest of it, and the rest of it was four clauses, and the fourth was the one he had not thought of in eleven weeks of thinking.

> **ONE.** The enquiry is answered in the affirmative. There is a line.
>
> **TWO.** The line will be furnished to the enquirer entire, without excision, at no charge in coin, in goods, in service, or in information. The Warden does not take payment for the reading of a book.
>
> **THREE.** The enquirer is reminded that his letter of the month of July, eighteen months past, advanced the following proposition: that the right of retaliation of a **Sky** stands notwithstanding a declared neutrality, and that its exercise in the matter of the Estraneo was therefore lawful, and is not a matter for this office.
>
> The Warden put one question to that letter. The enquirer answered it. **The second clause remains under consideration.**
>
> **FOUR.** Accordingly, and for so long as the second clause remains under consideration, the enquirer will not himself recover the child.
>
> He will not go. He will not send. He will not arrange, procure, commission or contrive. He will not be the hand.
>
> Should the child come to him, that is the child's doing and not the enquirer's, and this office will not look at it.
>
> The term of this condition is the term of the consideration. The Warden sets no date.

He read it three times, and each of the three readings was a different reading.

The first time he was looking for the trick, because there is always a trick, and there was no trick, and establishing that took about ninety seconds. The second time he was looking for the room, which is an entirely different exercise, and he found a certain amount of room in the word **himself** and a good deal more in the whole of the fifth line. He made himself stop there, because he knew perfectly well what he was doing, and he did that at forty, and it cost people who were not him.

The third time he simply read it, which was the only one of the three that told him anything.

*He is not charging me.*

*Clause two says so in a flat voice and it means it. No coin, no goods, no service, no information. He will not take the thing I have spent eleven weeks getting ready to hand over, and he will not take any of the other three either. He has gone to the trouble of putting it in writing. The Warden of Vendicare does not sell the reading of a book, and he wanted that on the page before clause four was.*

*And then in clause four he has taken something that I did not know was the kind of thing a person could be asked for.*

He put his head back against the wall and looked at the ceiling for a while.

*I came here to pay. I had four currencies and I had them in order and I was rather pleased with the list, and I was going to be extremely calm about spending any of them.*

*He has charged me in permission.*

*He has not taken one thing I own. He has taken something I am allowed to do. And I do not keep a ledger for those, because it has never once in forty-eight years occurred to me that being allowed was a thing a person could run out of.*

There followed a stretch of some length during which he thought nothing useful whatsoever, and then, being who he is, he got up and fetched a pen.

*Right. What does clause four actually cost? Do the arithmetic and do not flinch at it.*

*It does not stop the child being found. It stops me from being the person who finds him. Those are two different problems, only one of them is about me, and the fact that my chest is doing this means I have been running the wrong one since October.*

*It does not stop me knowing. He was explicit about that, and he was explicit twice. The line comes entire and without excision and he has charged nothing for it, and he has rather gone out of his way to make that plain.*

*So the arrangement he is offering is this. You may know everything, and you may do nothing.*

*Is that a punishment? Is it a test? Is it simply what he does with everybody, and I am the eleventh person this century to sit on a floor and work it out?*

He stopped with the pen halfway to the paper, and sat there in a cold room in January with an instrument on his knees, and understood the thing in the unhurried way these things arrive.

*He has described my life.*

*Not as a punishment. He has not the smallest interest in punishing me and he would consider the suggestion beneath the office. He has written down, in a flat official hand, the exact shape of what I have been doing since I was one year old, priced it at nothing, and handed it back to me as a condition of service.*

*I am eight years old. I know where fifty one children live and I have been to one address. I know what happens in Italy in a few years and I have told nobody at all. I know what is written on a piece of paper in a drawer in this house, and I have let that paper become the rule this town measures strangers by, and I sat through four hours of it in October without opening my mouth.*

*Clause four is not new. Clause four is a description of me with a date written on it.*

He laughed, and it came out badly, and only the once.

The worst of it, he decided some minutes later, was none of that. The worst of it was clause three, and clause three was going to be the worst of it for a considerable number of years.

*He is holding me to my own argument.*

*I wrote nine pages proving that a Sky may take back what was taken from him. I was right. It was a good argument and I have never regretted a line of it, and it is the reason the Vindice have not been at that boundary for eighteen months.*

*But a proposition of that kind does not stay where you put it. If retaliation by a Sky is lawful, then retaliation by a Sky is a category. A category is his business, and his business is not settled, and until he decides what my argument was worth I am barred from doing the exact thing my argument was about.*

*I built the door. And he has informed me, in the most courteous possible terms, that I may not walk through it until he has finished reading the plans.*

*And there is nobody to be angry at. Again. There is no clerk to be difficult with, no rule with an exception hidden in it, and no person anywhere in the chain who has decided one single thing against me. I am going to sit on this floor and be furious at a piece of paper for the second time in three months, and it is going to work exactly as well as it did in October.*

He signed it.

The instrument said to consent by signature and gave no space for one. That puzzled him for perhaps four seconds, until he understood that the space was the whole of the reverse. This was not a form. The thing at the other end of it has never in its existence filled in a form or seen the point of one.

He wrote four words, because four words were what he had been sent eighteen months ago, and he is not above that sort of thing and has never claimed to be.

> *Agreed. I will not go.*

He held it over the lamp and burned nothing, which was a thing his hands did before he had noticed them deciding to, and then he wrapped it back in the oilcloth and set it on the sill. It was gone before he came back up with the water for his face.

## 2.4

He told Musashi that night, and the four hours it took him to get to her door is the part of the evening that turned out to matter.

She was in the back room with the ledgers and the small brazier, with her glasses on, which she wears for nobody. She did not look up when he came in and she did not look up when he sat down, and she finished the column she was working before she put the pen down and looked at him.

"You have your reporting face," she said.

"Yes."

"Then report."

So he did, in order, the way she taught him and the way he was doing it in his own head a very long time before she taught him anything. It took eleven minutes and he did not soften a single line of it. He told her about the letter in October and about the four words eighteen months before that. He told her that he had not mentioned any of it at the time and that the decision not to mention it had been deliberate. He told her what had stood at the north stone in the sleet, and then he told her all four clauses in the order they were written, including the fifth line of clause four.

Then he stopped, and put his hands on his knees, and waited.

*Here it comes,* he thought, *and she is entitled to every word of it. I am eight years old. I opened a correspondence with the Warden of Vendicare and told nobody in this house. This afternoon a Vindice stood at the north stone for forty minutes, and seven houses sat with their hands on the **Flame** bonds of a **Sanctuary** and had no idea what to do with them. All of it happened because of a letter I wrote at four in the morning and did not mention at breakfast. She is going to be perfectly calm about it, and it is going to be the worst eleven minutes of my winter.*

Musashi took her glasses off and folded them.

"Three questions," she said.

"Yes."

"Is the instrument addressed to you, or to the office?"

*Oh,* he thought, and something in him went very quiet. *She has gone straight to the one that matters. Of course she has. She built the whole of the autumn out of that distinction and then made me sit through it in the cold.*

"The office," he said. "It says **to the Sky of Namimori**. My name is not written anywhere on it."

"Then it binds the office."

"Yes."

"Second question. Does any part of it bind the **Sanctuary** to do, or to refrain from doing, anything at all?"

He had worked that one out twice on the way up the stairs, once for the answer and once to be certain he was not shading the answer in his own favour.

"No," he said. "Clause four binds a person. It says that the enquirer will not himself recover the child, and that he will not send or arrange or be the hand. It does not say the town will not. It does not say the shrine will not. It does not say that anybody else alive is under any restriction whatsoever."

"You are certain of that."

"I read it three times looking for the room," said Tsuna, "and that is the room, and I am not pleased with how quickly I found it."

Musashi looked at him for a moment. "Third question," she said. "Do you want it in the file?"

And that one he had not prepared for at all, in four hours of preparing.

*She is asking me.*

*She is not telling me that it goes in the file. She has spent an entire autumn building a town that puts things in files, and she made me watch every hour of it, and she made the whole of Namimori stand in a queue in the cold to prove the point…*

*She is the Ruriko of this Sanctuary and I am its Kotei, and between us we have exactly one file, and she has just handed me the pen.*

*…and she is asking.*

"If it goes in the file," he said slowly, "seven houses read it in the spring. And what they read is that the Sky of Namimori accepted a condition from Vendicare and did not consult a single one of them first."

"Yes."

"And the next time there is a question about whether this town's **Kotei** can be relied upon to act, they will not be guessing."

"They will have a piece of paper instead of an opinion," said Musashi. "Which is worse for you, and also fairer to them. Both of those are true, and I am not going to pretend the second one away to make the first one easier."

He sat with that for a bit, and she let him.

"Does it help anybody if it goes in now?" he said.

"No."

"Does it hurt anybody if it does not?"

"Not that I can see, and I have been sitting here looking for the last four minutes." She turned her hand over on the desk. "There is nothing in that instrument that the file can act upon. It creates no duty for this **Sanctuary** and it removes no protection from it. It is a private undertaking by a person who happens to hold an office, and the file is for things the file can do something about."

"And when there is something it can do something about?"

"Then it goes in that day," said Musashi, "with the date it was signed on it, which is today, and not a date that makes either of us look better than we were."

"Agreed."

"Good." She put her glasses back on and picked the pen up and did not begin writing with it. "Now the part that is not a question."

*Here it is.*

"You were braced," she said. "For the whole eleven minutes. I watched you do it, and you never once took your hands off your knees, and you reported it better than most adults in this town could have."

"…Yes."

"I am not going to. And I would like you to understand that it is not because I approve of any of it, because I do not, particularly."

"Then why?"

"Because you came and told me, and it took you four hours to do it, and the four hours is the part I am interested in." She looked at the brazier rather than at him. "If I make tonight expensive, then the next time a thing like this arrives you will weigh the telling of it against the cost of the telling. And you will weigh it very well, Masaya, because you weigh everything very well. And one of the things you decide not to tell me will be the one I needed."

"That is a calculation."

"Of course it is a calculation," said Musashi. "I am seventy-five years old and I run a Sanctuary. What on earth did you imagine it was?"

And Tsuna laughed properly, once, and felt something in his chest that he had been carrying since two o'clock that afternoon put itself down.

"Ask me before, next time," she said. "Not because I will stop you, because I will not and we both know it. Because I should like to have been asked."

*That is not an order.*

*She has made a request of me. Not an instruction, not a rule of the house, not a thing the Ruriko of Namimori requires of her grandson. A request, which I am able to refuse, and which she has deliberately left me able to refuse, and she has done it knowing I will notice.*

*Nobody has done that since I was forty years old.*

"Yes," he said.

"Good. Go to bed."

He got as far as the door.

"Masaya."

"Yes?"

"The hat," said Musashi, without looking up from the ledger. "In the visitor log. Whose idea was that?"

"Mine."

"Mm," said Musashi, and wrote a line in the wrong column and did not correct it. Tsuna went upstairs, and did not find out for another two years that she had sat in that room until half past three in the morning.

## 2.5

The line came nine days later, and the way it came made him angry for about an hour before he understood it.

There was no courier and there was no weather. It was simply on the sill of the room with the door that does not close, at some point between his going down to breakfast and his coming back up for a ledger he had forgotten. It was in the same oilcloth, and the oilcloth was dry, and outside it had been raining since six.

*He has been able to do that the whole time,* thought Tsuna, standing in the doorway. *He could have put the terms on this sill in October and saved everybody the afternoon. He sent a man to the north gate instead and let him stand in the sleet for forty minutes in front of forty children with armbands. Why?*

He worked it out on the stairs going down, and stopped halfway.

*Because the terms required consent, and consent requires that a person be seen to be asked. He came to the boundary in order to ask. He put the answer through a window, because an answer is not a negotiation and does not need a witness.*

*That is not theatre. That is procedure of a kind I have not met before, and I thought I had met most kinds.*

The line was a single sheet, ruled into six columns, and there were eleven entries on it. Ten of the eleven were not about the Saint-Exavier child. They were the entries above and below the one that was. The page had been copied out entire and without excision, exactly as clause two had promised. It had plainly not occurred to the Warden of Vendicare that a person might want only the middle of a page.

Tsuna read all ten of them. He read them twice. He did not write any of them down, and he has never told a living soul what was in them, and he was eight years old, and he read all ten because it seemed to him that somebody ought to have.

Then he read the eleventh.

The columns were headed **TAKEN. FROM. BY. DATE. RETURNED. NOTE.** The first four were filled in. The house was named, and the **Flame** family who took the child was named, and it was the name he had already had for years.

*The Estraneo,* he thought. *Of course it is. I have known that since I was six, and I have known it from a woman who told me in a room with the door shut, and here it is in a ledger in Europe in somebody else's handwriting.*

*So why was he not one of the fifty-one?*

He sat with it, because it is the obvious question and because obvious questions are the ones people skip.

*I took fifty-one children out of that family. I had the intake book. I counted them twice and I have the addresses of all fifty-one and I am never going to stop knowing that number.*

*He is not in it. I have read that book more times than is good for me and he is not anywhere in it…*

*Which does not mean he was not there. It means nobody wrote him down when he arrived. The Estraneo entered fifty-one children in a book and did not enter him, and the only reason to leave a child out of your own private book is that he was never going to be listed as a child at all.*

*So he has been outside every book on both sides for eight years. Ise never had him, and Ise keeps **Honō** births going back to 1801. The intake never had him. His own family stopped asking in year four.*

*One office in the world put a line under his name and has kept it open. It is the office that deals in debts, not in children, and not in **Flame**, and it is the only one that never once needed him to be a person to do it.* Seeing it written by somebody else, in a hand that did not care one way or the other, was a colder thing than he had been ready for.

The fifth column was blank.

It was not crossed through. It was not marked with a dash, or a word, or any of the eleven small things a clerk does when a matter is finished and the finish is unhappy. The column headed **RETURNED** was simply empty, and the emptiness had been maintained, because down the margin beside it ran eight years of small dates where the entry had been checked and left exactly as it was.

*Empty,* thought Tsuna, and sat down slowly on the boards.

*An empty box stopped me in October. That one meant that I could not ask. This one means something entirely different and it took me four seconds to see it, and I would like the four seconds back.*

*They are still counting him.*

*Ise has no column for that child and never did have one. Vendicare has a column, the column is open, and somebody has opened this page eight times in eight years and written a date in the margin and closed it again. Not once in those eight times did anybody write the word that would let them stop.*

*He is not lost. He is outstanding.*

The sixth column, headed **NOTE**, was not about the child at all.

It was a list of enquiries, and that was the whole of it: who had asked about this line, and when, and what they had offered, and what had been done about it. Vendicare does not keep a book of children. Vendicare keeps a book of debts, in which a child taken by a **Flame** family is a debt like any other, and the note column is the part that a creditor actually reads.

There were four enquiries in eight years, and they were not in the order he expected, and it took him some while to work out what order they were in.

Three of the four were the family, dated in the first year after the taking, the second, and the fourth. All three offered money, and the sums went up each time, and the third sum was large enough that he put the sheet down on his knee for a moment and looked at the wall. All three had the same disposition written beside them in the same even hand: **NOT ACCEPTED. NO PERFORMANCE POSSIBLE.**

*They were not refused,* he thought, reading it again. *Read the words. They were not refused. It says that no performance is possible, which is a man telling you there is nothing he can do. And they are hearing a price they have not yet met, and they are coming back with a bigger number, and he is writing the bigger number down without comment. Three times.*

*They stopped in year four. I would like to know what happened in year four and I am never going to.*

The entry at the foot of the column was the oldest of the four. It was dated in the February of the same winter the child was taken, some weeks before the family had got as far as asking anybody anything, and it had no name against it.

*So it is not chronological,* thought Tsuna, *which means it is something else, and there is only one thing a book like this would sort by…*

*…no. It is ranked.*

*He has put them in the order of what was offered. The smallest at the top and the largest at the bottom, and the dates fall where they fall. That is not how anybody keeps a record of a missing child. It is exactly how a man keeps a record of a debt, because a creditor does not care when you offered, he cares what you offered, and the only column that sorts anything is the one with the price in it.*

*Which means the thing at the bottom of this list is the largest offer ever made for that child by anybody. And the family's third figure was enough to buy a street.*

Where the other three named the house, the entry at the foot said **UNNAMED. THROUGH THE EASTERN CHANNEL. JAPAN, PREFECTURE OF MIE.** And in the offer column, where the others had figures written out in full, it had five words.

> **OFFERED: THE WHOLE OF HER BOOK.**

Tsuna did not move for a considerable time.

*February. Eight years ago. Somebody in this prefecture, six weeks after the Irie family came through this boundary with a bag and a toddler and a woman who had given birth on the road…*

*…offered Vendicare her entire client list.*

*Not a payment out of it. All of it. The whole working life of an information broker, every name she ever sold and every name she ever protected, which is the only asset that kind of person has and the only reason that kind of person is still breathing.*

*Eight years!*

*She had been inside a **Sanctuary** for six weeks. She had a newborn son and a husband with nothing and a daughter who could already disappear from a net. She was being hunted by people who do not stop. And the first thing she did, once there was a roof over the four of them, was to go and try to buy a child she had never met.*

The disposition column beside it carried the same four words as the other three.

**NOT ACCEPTED. NO PERFORMANCE POSSIBLE.**

*So she offered every single thing she had, and she was told no, and she was not even told no for a reason she could argue with. And she has lived here for eight years. She stood in a queue in the cold in October for two hours to vote a stranger in, and she said yes loudly enough that the whole of the second torii heard her. I stood at the top of those steps wearing the office of **Kotei** and thought I understood why.*

*I did not understand why at all. I thought she was paying this town back for taking her in. She has been paying something else back for eight years, it is not to us, and nobody here has the faintest idea.*

He looked at the sheet for a long while and then turned it face down on the boards, very carefully, as though it were hot.

*Right. Stop. You are doing the thing again.*

*You are eight years old, sitting on a floor, arranging a woman's worst ten years into a shape that is satisfying to look at from the outside. That is what you do instead of being upset by something. It is extremely efficient and it has never once helped the person it was about.*

*She is not a mechanism. She did not do it so that it would rhyme with anything.*

He put both hands flat on the boards and breathed for a while.

*She did it because she could not stand it. That is the whole of it and it does not need a structure and it is not improved by having one.*

## 2.6

He worked out what he was going to do on the eleventh of January, on the roof of the storeroom, in the cold, which is where he goes when the arithmetic is unpleasant and he does not want to be found in the middle of it.

*Clause four. I will not go, I will not send, I will not arrange, procure, commission or contrive, I will not be the hand. Good. Who is left?*

*Nobody in this house. Anybody in this house who moved would be me sending, and I know it, and he knows it, and I am not going to be clever about a document I have signed nine days ago.*

*Not the family. They stopped asking in year four, and I have no way of reaching them that is not me arranging with extra steps in it.*

*Not the Hibari. They have had people in Europe for years and they are extremely good at it, and every one of those people reports in the end to a **Getsufuji**. Grandmother could have them told inside an afternoon. That is arranging with three steps in front of it, and three steps in front of a thing does not change what the thing is, and I have watched grown men pretend otherwise in three countries.*

He sat with that for a while and let the cold get into his coat.

*So who is left? Who in the world is already walking in that direction for reasons of their own?*

*Which leaves a person who is already looking.*

*Who was already looking eight years ago, before I could form a sentence. Who was looking for her own reasons, which have nothing to do with me and never will. Who was refused, and who has not stopped, because a woman who offers the whole of her book does not simply put the question down and take up gardening.*

*I would not be sending her anywhere. She was already going.*

*I would only be…*

He stopped there and made himself do the next part slowly, because the next part was where he would find out whether he was going to keep the promise or only the wording of it.

*Careful. This is the exact place where I break it and give the break a nicer name.*

*If I go to her and put the line in her hand and say nothing at all, is that arranging?*

*…Yes. Obviously yes. Telling a person a thing in order that they will act on it is arranging. Dressing it up as information does not change one atom of it. I have watched men do exactly that in three countries, and I always knew precisely what they were doing while they did it.*

*So I cannot hand it to her in order to make her go.*

He looked out over the town for a bit. The lights were coming on down the hill in the order they come on, which is not the order of the streets but the order of when people finish work. He has known that order since he was five. He finds it obscurely steadying and has never tried to explain that to anybody.

*But she asked first.*

*Eight years before I wrote a single word to that address, she went to that office and offered everything she owned for this exact line, and she was told no, and she never got to see it. The page she tried to pay for and did not get is lying face down on my floor at this moment.*

*That is not my information to give her. That is her information. It has been sitting in a ledger in Europe for eight years, and I happen to be the only person alive who is holding both the page and the name of the woman who tried to buy it.*

*Giving a person the thing they already tried to pay for is not arranging. It is returning. Would he see it that way? Would I, if somebody did it to me?*

He turned that over four times looking for the join, because there was a join, and it was thin, and he made himself look straight at it.

*The join is that I want her to go. I do want it. I am not going to pretend otherwise, and if the Warden ever asks me I will tell him so in the first sentence.*

*But she does not need me to want it. She needed me in order to know, and she will decide the whole of the rest without one word from me. If she decides to do nothing at all, then nothing at all is what happens, and I will not say a single thing about it for the rest of my life.*

*That is the test. If I could watch her read it and put it in a drawer and never mention it again…*

He sat on the cold roof of the storeroom and checked, properly, in the manner in which he checks things.

*…yes. I could. It would be unbearable and I could do it.*

*Right. Then it is not arranging. It is hers.*

He did not go that night. It was the eleventh of January, which is a bad week in that house for reasons that have nothing to do with any of this, and the woman in question had a son with an examination on the Thursday. A thing like that does not get delivered on a Tuesday merely because the person delivering it has finished thinking.

*It will keep,* he thought. *It has kept eight years and four days.*

*And she is going to look at me when I put it into her hand. She is going to work out inside four seconds that I have been holding it for nine days. I am going to have to stand there in her kitchen while she does it.*

*Good. I ought to have to.*

He climbed down off the storeroom roof, and the fox came out of the dark at the bottom of the steps and fell in at his ankle in the manner it has, and the two of them went up the hill together in the rain.

Somewhere in Europe, in a room where no light and no sound arrive, a page was opened for the ninth time in eight years, and a small date was written in the margin beside an empty column, and the page was closed again.

Somewhere else entirely a second clause remained under consideration, and would remain so for a while yet, and the thing considering it has all the time there is. It has no **Flame** of any of the seven and it has never wanted one.

And on the eleventh of January, in a fishing town in Mie, the eight year old **Sky** of that town, who had spent two months deciding what he was willing to pay, discovered that he had been charged in permission instead. He signed it. Then he walked up a hill in the rain. That was the first day of what turned out to be a very long apprenticeship in knowing a thing and not moving.

It is not quite a year until the news out of Italy. He is going to be extremely good at this by then, and that is going to be the problem.

**Ba chỗ đáng nói:**

**1. Dòng địa chỉ.** Vendicare gọi cậu là *the Sky of Namimori*. Ch1 dựng cả đoạn đóng trên việc danh xưng ấy **không đi xa được**. Nên cái làm cậu lạnh người không phải việc ông ta biết, mà là: Ise có một ô cậu không điền được, còn ở đây có một ô **đã được điền hộ**. Đó là cách trả lằn can *"cái trống phải có ích"* bằng cách đảo chiều nó thay vì lặp lại nó.

**2. Điều khoản hai không lấy gì cả.** Ông từ chối nhận tiền, hàng, dịch vụ và thông tin, và ghi rõ ra. Cậu mất hai tháng chuẩn bị bốn loại tiền và không tiêu được loại nào. Chi tiết ấy làm cổng 2.2 đứng được mà không cần ai giải thích nó cho người đọc.

**3. Nội dung điều khoản hai của lá thư cũ vẫn kín.** Thẻ cảnh và `hook-bermuda-to-mo` cấm suy ra. Chương **nhắc tới nó ba lần và không mở một chữ nào**; chức năng duy nhất của nó là cái đang treo.

**Lệch nhỏ so với thẻ:** thẻ xếp *"cậu trả"* ở 2.4 cùng cảnh báo cáo. Bản viết tách ra, ký ở cuối 2.3 và báo cáo ở 2.4. Lý do là ngân sách thoại: gộp lại thì 2.4 phải cõng cả một cảnh suy tính lẫn một cảnh đối thoại, và cảnh đối thoại sẽ nuốt cảnh kia.

**Chỗ buồn cười** *(phép thử 3 của `bon-lan-can`)*: Ban Kỷ luật xử một Vindice **bằng đúng quy trình dành cho khách**, vì không có quy trình nào khác, và Kyouya chỉ có một câu hỏi và nó là câu hỏi đúng. Sổ khách ghi tên hắn theo **cái mũ**, và đó là cách canon nhận dạng Vindice thật: wiki phân biệt từng người bằng mũ và cổ áo.
