# Local Boy Finally Goes To An Address He Has Had For Eighteen Months. Local Other Boy Has Been Waiting, And Is Not Gracious About It.

## 6.1

The bonds came off at twenty past six in the morning, and Tsuna felt them go. It was like a hand coming off your shoulder long after you had stopped noticing it was there.

He was in the kitchen at the time, doing the thing he did every morning, which was the accounts for a shrine that had survived four centuries without his help and would presumably manage the rest. A **Kotei** of seven, with a brush and a column of figures. The brush stopped. Somewhere above the treeline seven enormous old attentions turned away from Namimori and went home, one at a time, in no particular order… and the last of them, the one out of the north-east, took about four seconds longer than the others, in the manner of an old woman looking back at a room to check she has not left anything.

Then the sky over the town was just sky.

"She let go!" said Ayame, from the doorway, with her left arm in a sling and her right hand around a cup she had no business carrying. "Did you feel that? Tell me you felt that. I felt it in my *teeth*."

"You are supposed to be in bed."

"I was in bed. I have been in bed for two days. Do you know what is in bed? Nothing." She sat down, badly, in the way of a person who has decided that sitting is a thing she will do in one movement and has been proven wrong. "Tomoko-sensei says I may walk to the gate and back twice. I have been to the gate eleven times and I am going to keep going until somebody stops me, and nobody is going to stop me, because I am *wounded*."

There is a version of Ayame that Tsuna had expected after a knife went through her forearm. It was quiet and grey and slept a lot.

What had actually arrived was a woman in the best mood of her adult life.

Her Flame had come back up on the second morning and had stayed up, which Tomoko said was the body being pleased with itself and which everyone else in the house recognised for what it was. A **Ketsueki** who is enjoying herself leaks. It came off her in the corridor and made the lamps do something, and Nagi had followed her about for an hour on the first day at a distance of two metres, fascinated, like a small boat behind a ferry.

She had shown the bandage to the fish man. She had shown it to the two Irie children, who had been appropriately horrified and then wanted to know if it went all the way through, which it did, and which she told them with relish. She had shown it to a delegation of four elderly women from the lower shrine, who had come to be sympathetic. They left ninety minutes later. They had been given tea, a full account of the legal position, and a demonstration, twice, of the exact angle.

By the second telling the story had acquired an extra knife and a longer speech, and Tsuna had heard it come back to him from a man at the ferry office who had not been there, improved.

"Ruriko-sama says the assessors come Friday," she said. "There is a hole in the Hirata wall and it has to be measured by somebody official before anyone is allowed to be annoyed about it in writing. I have been asked to be present. I have been asked to be present because I am the injured party." She drank her tea. "I have never been an injured party. I am going to be extremely good at it."

*She is going to be insufferable until spring,* Tsuna thought. *And is there one single person in this house who is going to stop her? Is there one in the town? Would I let them?*

*No. Let her have the winter.*

The letter came with the second post, which in Namimori means the boy on the bicycle rather than the boy with the van. It came in the plain brown envelope that prefectural offices use when they want you to know that nothing in here is about you personally.

It was addressed to the shrine, because everything to do with children in this prefecture goes through a shrine somewhere, and it had been opened by Ayame, because everything goes through Ayame.

She did not hand it to him. She put it on the table, face up, and pushed it across with one finger. Then she went and stood at the window with her back to him. That is a thing she does when she has read something and does not intend to have an opinion about it out loud.

**PLACEMENT OF MINOR: CONFIRMATION OF RECEIVING DATE.**

Underneath that, a form. A case number. A prefectural stamp. The name of a family in a town called Taki, sixty kilometres down the coast road and then inland, who had raised four and had a third child with a nerve condition in the same hand. A date, which was Thursday. And a line near the bottom, in the small print, which said that the placement had been arranged through a private instrument and that a representative of the originating party was invited to attend the handover.

*A representative of the originating party.*

He read that three times.

He had paid a lawyer in Genoa in cash. He had watched her stamp a thing. He had walked out of her office into a cold street with a small clean satisfaction. The business was closed. Strangers would conduct it from now on at a distance of three thousand kilometres… and he would never have to look at any part of it again.

Nobody had told him that paper comes home.

*Of course it does,* he thought. *Of course it does. You put a child into a prefecture. Which prefecture did you think it was?*

*You made a legal person out of yourself in a country you do not live in. You went home. And how far is home from where you posted her? Forty minutes. Forty.*

He sat with the form in front of him and did the thing he had been doing since he was old enough to hold a pen. He looked for the part of the arrangement that could still be handled from a desk. There was a box for a proxy. There was always a box for a proxy. He could write *Sawada Musashi* in it and the shrine would send somebody with a good coat and a stamp, and it would be correct, and the child would be received properly by a respectable adult, and nothing anywhere would be wrong.

He looked at the box for a long while.

Then he looked at the list.

It was in the second drawer, under the seal case, where it had been for eighteen months. Fifty-one names, typed, with addresses added afterwards in his own handwriting in four different inks because he had done it four times as the placements settled. He knew the list. He could have written it out from memory and got fifty of them, and the one he would have missed was the one he always missed, which was a girl in Kuwana whose name had two readings.

He had checked it eleven times.

He had never once been to an address on it.

*Ah,* he thought.

And there it was, sitting in the middle of his own kitchen table on a Wednesday morning, looking exactly like a form.

"Tenmei-sama," said Ayame, to the window.

"Yes."

"Nothing," said Ayame. "I am admiring the weather."

The door moved about ten centimetres and then stopped, which is how Nagi enters a room she is not sure she is allowed in. She was four, or possibly five, depending on which of two documents you believed and which of two people you asked, and she had come down in her outdoor socks with her hair not yet done, holding the end of her own sleeve.

Above her, along the ceiling and the top of the doorframe and out into the passage, a **Gen'ei** was running slowly clockwise. It did that every morning in the first hour after she woke. She could not put one down on purpose yet, and Grandmother had stopped asking her to a long time ago. It went round the light fitting twice and then over the lintel and out of sight.

Tsuna had no Mist. He had never had one, in either life; three Vongola technicians with three machines had written *Sky, dominant, no significant secondary* and gone home. So he had no way at all to do the thing this child did before breakfast without noticing.

*That is going to be enormous,* he thought, as he always thought. *And does she know? Does anybody tell her? Would it help if we did?*

"You are going away," said Nagi.

"How do you know that?"

"Ayame-san is being quiet." Nagi came the rest of the way in and sat down on the floor at the corner of the table, which was her place, and arranged her feet. "She is only quiet about going away. She is loud about everything else."

From the window came the sound of a woman deciding not to dignify that.

"On Thursday," Tsuna said. "One day. Taki, and back."

"I will come."

It was not a request, which was the first surprise, and it was not a demand either. It was said the way she said *it is raining*, as a piece of information offered to somebody who might not have looked outside.

"Not this time."

"Why?"

And he opened his mouth to give her the answer he had ready, which was about the road and the hour and how long a small person can sit on a bus… and then he did not say it, because she was looking at him with the flat and patient attention of a child who has been lied to comfortably before and has decided to see whether it happens again.

*Because I have never once done this part with anybody watching,* he thought. *Because for eighteen months the whole of it has been paper, and paper does not look back, and on Thursday it is going to look back, and I would like to be extremely alone when that happens.*

"Because it is mine," he said.

Nagi thought about that.

"All right," she said, and went to find her shoes, and the *Gen'ei* came back along the ceiling after her and went out through the door, and that was the end of it.

Up on the wall above the north road, somebody watched the bicycle boy go back down the hill.

He had not spoken to anybody since Monday. He had turned down three meals. He did not come in, and would not come in until Friday… and Tsuna felt the shape of him up there every day that week, like a kettle taken off the heat and still ticking.

He left that alone. He had learned, at some cost, that a **Cloud** who has been made to do nothing for two hours needs leaving in a field for a while afterwards, like a horse.

*And what does a **Cloud** do with two hours of held breath? Where does it go? It does not evaporate.*

## 6.2

He went out on Thursday at six, by Cloud, alone, with a folded form in his inside pocket and a fox in a canvas bag.

Going out by Cloud is not hiding. He had known that for a year and had needed most of another one to understand what it actually meant, and the understanding had arrived in a market in Genoa in the form of a dog.

Nobody fails to see a Cloud.

What happens is that a street full of people, not one of whom could tell you that they had decided anything at all, arrive at the same small agreement about where they would prefer to walk… and the space goes where it likes, and the street closes behind it, and at no point is anybody hidden from anybody.

It works on the bus. The seat beside him stayed empty from Namimori to the junction, and the bus was two thirds full. An old man with a crate got on at the third stop. He looked along the aisle in the ordinary way a man looks for a seat, and sat four rows back, next to somebody else's shopping.

It had a hole in it. It had always had a hole in it. The hole had a name now. The name was a small floating person in a cloak, an **Arcobaleno** and a **Mist**, who had looked at him on a platform in Liguria and priced him inside of nine seconds.

*A moving pocket of good manners,* he thought, *is invisible to everybody in the world except exactly one kind of person.*

*And what are the odds that kind of person is on the 6:40 to Taki? What are the odds twice in one month?*

He made himself answer it, because a question you refuse to answer goes on costing. *Low. It is low. It is not zero, and it was not zero last time either.*

He got off at the junction and stood for a moment in the cold with the road going two ways.

And that was where the other thing happened, the one he had not thought to expect, which was that he crossed a line on a map and the words changed.

It is not a thing anybody in Namimori would be able to explain, because to explain it you would have to have been somewhere else. Inside the boundary there are seven words, and they are old, and children learn them before they learn to tie a knot. A **Ketsueki** is what Ayame is. A **Gen'ei** is what Grandmother is, and what Nagi is going to be, and the word is a thousand years old and it means a thing seen that is not there. A **Getsufuji** is the boy on the wall above the north road. A **Yasuragi** is his mother.

And he himself is the **Kotei**, which is the word the contract uses, and **Kohaku**, which is what Ishii-san says when she is being fond, and **Tenmei**, which is what his grandmother says when she wants a thing to stop.

Forty minutes outside the boundary, a man in a stationmaster's coat said something to another man about the weather and the word he used for the Flame coming off a boy asleep on a bench was **Rain**.

Just that. In Japanese, in a stationmaster's coat, in a shelter with a vending machine that worked: *Rain*. The English word, flattened into the language, the way a borrowed word goes flat. It was the word every man in a suit in Palermo had used in every room Tsuna had ever run, and he had heard it a hundred thousand times, and he had never once heard it in a bus shelter in Japan.

He stood there with his bag in his hand and had the peculiar experience of being homesick in two directions at once.

*Forty kilometres,* he thought. *Forty kilometres, and what am I here? A Sky. That is all. Just a Sky.*

Not the **Kotei** of anything. Not a title that lives in a box in a storeroom under a north hall and comes out once a lifetime.

A **Sky**. A unit of measurement. The most common and most expensive noun in the business he had spent two lifetimes trying to be finished with… and here it was, twenty minutes up the coast road, in a bus shelter, being used by a man with a clipboard about somebody's sleeping son.

*Rain. Storm. Cloud. Mist. Sun. Lightning. Sky.*

*Seven words,* he thought, *and I have spent four years learning seven other ones, and the seven other ones stop at a line on a map that only one town in the world can see.*

The fox put its head out of the bag and made the sound it makes when it has an opinion about a place.

"I know," said Tsuna. "I know. It is very rude of the rest of Japan to keep existing."

He walked the last part, because the second bus was forty minutes and he was early, and because walking is what you do with a thing you have been postponing when it has stopped being postponable. The road went inland and uphill and stopped being a coast road. There were mandarin frames on the slope, wrapped for winter. There was a shrine at a crossing so small it was one stone and a rope. He stopped at it out of habit, and then felt ridiculous.

There was nothing in it. He could tell there was nothing in it. And the ability to tell is not a thing you are supposed to advertise.

Taki came up out of the valley the way small towns do, all at once and slightly lower than expected.

And he stood at the top of the hill above it with the list folded in his pocket, which he had not needed to bring and had brought, and looked down at a place where fifty-one children live.

## 6.3

You cannot see it for the first twenty minutes, and then you cannot stop seeing it.

That is the part nobody had written in any of the reports that crossed his desk. Tsuna had read all of them. Every single one used the word *integrated*, and not one of them told him what it looked like. What it looks like is a town of four thousand people doing something enormous, extremely quietly. Getting most of it right. Getting some of it wrong in ways nobody has noticed yet, because there is no one here whose job it is to notice.

The bakery by the bus stop makes bread that is the wrong shape for Japan, and makes it well, and has been making it for about a year. There is a hand-lettered sign in the window in Japanese, and a second sign underneath it in Italian. The Italian one has a mistake in it that no Italian would make. So a Japanese person made it on purpose, for somebody to correct.

Somebody has corrected it, in pencil. The pencil has not been rubbed out.

The primary school noticeboard by the gate has a class list with eleven names on it written in katakana. Four of them are written in katakana **and** in Roman letters, in a second hand, underneath. Tsuna stood and looked at that for some time.

The bench outside the post office has a boy of about nine on it. He did not have his back to the road. He had taken the one seat in the row from which you can see both ends of the street.

He was reading a comic, and was not in any distress at all. He had simply sat where he sat, the way a person sits who worked out, some time ago, where to sit.

There is a small awkward second-hand shop that sells, among other things, a great many shoes in sizes eight to thirteen. The woman running it says *ciao* to a child going past. It is exactly the accent of somebody who learned forty words on purpose and then stopped, because forty was what was needed.

Tsuna walked through it for an hour with a canvas bag on his shoulder, reading the town as he had been taught to read a street. It is a skill he acquired for entirely different purposes. He has never been able to put it down.

*The pharmacy stocks three things it has no local reason to stock. Somebody wrote the dosage card in two languages and laminated it.*

*Two adults on this street are watching me and neither of them is being obvious, and they are not watching me because I am a stranger. They are watching me because I am a stranger who is a child.*

*They have had a year and a half of being extremely careful, and they have not got tired of it, and that is the single most expensive thing I have seen today.*

And then, at the crossing by the school, a thing happened that he had somehow not prepared for at all.

A boy came round the corner at a run, late, with a bag banging on his hip. He went past at speed. And about a metre and a half behind him, keeping up, low to the ground and going out at the edges the way a fire goes out at the edges, came four seconds of **Storm Flame**.

It was not aimed at anything. It was the Flame of a nine year old who is late and has been shouted at and has not yet got a lid on himself. The **Flame** flickered along the gutter, took the corner badly, and went out. Nobody on the street so much as turned round.

"KENJI!" bellowed a woman from a doorway, in Japanese, without any alarm whatsoever. "Bucket!"

The boy stopped. He came back. He picked up a galvanised bucket from beside the step, upended it over the patch of gutter he had just been standing in, sat on it, and stayed there for a count of ten with his face in an expression of towering adolescent injustice… and the woman went back inside, and a man on a bicycle went round the bucket without slowing, and that was the whole of the incident.

Tsuna stood on the other side of the road with a canvas bag on his shoulder.

*The Estraneo were a Flame famiglia,* he thought. *That is what the file is. That is what the whole eighteen months of it is. They were not hurting children in general. They were hurting children with **Flames**. They were making more of them. And what came out of that compound was fifty-one people who are every one of them going to come up, at some point, in front of somebody who does not know what it is.*

*A **Storm** at a crossing. A **Sun** against a wall. A **Mist** in a classroom at the wrong moment… and not one adult in this valley trained for any of it.*

*And I read that file four hundred times and I planned the extraction and I planned the placements and I planned the money.*

*Did I plan one single line for what a Storm does at a crossing when he is late for school?*

He had not. He had written *specialist support, to be arranged locally* in a box in a document, and he remembered writing it, and he remembered the small clean satisfaction of having identified the requirement.

Taki had arranged it with a bucket.

He went and looked, later, because he could not not look. There were buckets at four crossings. There was a fire blanket in the bakery that was newer than everything around it. There was a line painted on the wall of the sports shed at a height of about a metre. A boy explained it to him, unprompted and with enormous condescension. It is where you stand if you are going to be a problem. It is Sachi's line, because Sachi is a **Sun**, and Sachi is the worst.

"Is it working?" Tsuna asked him.

"It is *stupid*," said the boy, "but it works, so nobody will let us stop."

There is a woman who comes down from Namimori once a month on the bus. She is eighty-one, she runs a shrine at the bottom of a hill sixty kilometres away, and she sits in the community hall for four hours on the second Tuesday and people bring her children. Tsuna found this out from a timetable pinned to a noticeboard, on which somebody had written **Ishii-san, 2nd Tues** in the ordinary way you write down a thing that recurs.

*She never mentioned it,* he thought.

*Why would she? Who is she supposed to mention it to? It is not a plan. It is a Tuesday.*

And the fifty-one are fine.

That was the thing. He had come down the hill braced for an audit. An audit is a shape he understands and can act on.

What he had walked into was a Thursday morning in a town where fifty-one children are fine, and going to school, and complaining about school, and standing about outside a bakery at a loose end.

*Mostly fine,* he made himself think, because *fine* is not a number and he had never in either life allowed himself a conclusion that was not a number. Mostly. There would be a real figure under that. There would be four or five or nine for whom none of this had worked, and there would be one somewhere who had got worse, and those were the ones a report calls *outcomes pending* and a town calls by name.

He did not go and find out which ones they were.

He had the addresses. He had every address in this town folded in his inside pocket, in his own handwriting, in four different inks. He walked past the end of two of those streets. He read the sign each time and kept walking. The fox in the bag shifted and did not comment.

At the corner by the shrine steps he stopped, put his hand flat against a wall, and stood there breathing for a moment like somebody who has climbed something.

*What did you think was going to be down here,* he thought.

*You thought it was going to be a ledger.*

## 6.4

Kawahira's arrangement in Taki was not a house.

It was a room behind a noodle place that had closed in the summer and had not reopened. The room had a door onto a yard. The yard had a wall. Against the wall stood eleven crates, arranged into something that was either seating or a fortification, depending on who had last been sitting in it. A chalk diagram covered two thirds of the yard wall. It was in Italian. It had been corrected four separate times by somebody with a worse hand and more confidence.

Tsuna read as much of it as he could from the gate, and then made himself stop. It was a **Mist** construction diagram of a kind he had seen perhaps three times in sixty years. Two of those had been in a room he was not supposed to have been in.

*Who teaches an eight year old that?*

*Somebody who is not worried about what the eight year old will do with it.*

There was a boy sitting on the top crate with a book open on his knees.

He was eight, or thereabouts. He was small for eight in the way a child is small when the first six years were arranged by other people, and he was going to catch up and had not caught up yet. Dark hair. A very good coat, plainly a gift, worn with the deliberate carelessness of somebody making sure nobody thinks he asked for it.

He did not look up when Tsuna came into the yard. He turned a page.

"You are forty minutes early," he said, in Italian, "which I would like you to know I find extremely annoying, because I had a whole thing planned for eleven o'clock and now I have to do it at twenty past ten like a person with no standards."

Tsuna stopped in the middle of the yard.

There are perhaps nine things a professional does with a sentence like that. He had used all nine at one time or another.

The correct one here was to say *I am sorry, what?* in a language he was not supposed to be good at, and to look confused, and to make the other person spend something.

He did not do it. He found, standing in a cold yard in Taki with a canvas bag in his hand, that he could not be bothered.

"How long have you known?" he said, in Italian.

"Since about four days after." Mukuro turned another page, read to the bottom of it, and closed the book on his finger. "I would love to tell you it took months of work and that I am a genius, and I am a genius, but it did not. Somebody erased a famiglia in one night. Somebody moved fifty-one children across the world with paperwork that *worked*. No body, no signature, no **Flame** signature at the site… and eleven days later a leak arrives in the mafia's own systems signed with a name that means a small fire." He shrugged with one shoulder. "And then I was in Japan. In a prefecture. With a teacher who has been alive for an amount of time he does not like discussing."

"That is not a name."

"No. That is the *shape* of a person." Now he looked up. "The name took another eight months. I got it off a shipping manifest in Trieste, which I am not going to explain to you." He tipped his head. "And when I had it I sat with it for a while… because the answer was a number, and the number was six."

The yard was very quiet.

"You were six," said Mukuro.

Tsuna, who had been expecting this conversation in some form for eighteen months and had rehearsed four versions of it and had discarded all four as self-indulgent, discovered that he had nothing at all in his mouth.

"I have thought about that a great deal," Mukuro went on, conversationally, swinging one foot. "I want you to understand that I have really put the hours in. A six year old in a country three thousand kilometres away takes a famiglia apart in one night, arranges fifty-one placements, and then vanishes so thoroughly that finding him took a person of my quality most of a year. There are three explanations. Two of them are stupid." He tipped his head. "The third one is that you are not a six year old, and were not, and I do not actually care."

*There it is,* Tsuna thought, and his pulse went somewhere unhelpful, and he did the only thing available, which was nothing at all.

"You do not care."

"Not even *slightly*!" Mukuro looked genuinely bored, which was somehow worse than the alternative. "Everybody I have ever met who was interested in what I *am* wanted to open me and find out. It is a tedious kind of attention and I would not inflict it on a dog. I am interested in what people *do*." He opened the book again. "Which brings me to the part I did plan, so pay attention, because I have been saving it."

"I am fairly sure I am about to enjoy this less than you are."

"Correct." He did not look up. "You have not come."

Somewhere on the other side of the wall a van went past in low gear.

"Eighteen months," said Mukuro. "Fifty-one addresses, and you have every one of them. And I know you have every one of them, because Giulia in Kuwana got a letter in the second month correcting the reading of her own name, and nobody signed it. You have never been to a single one. Not one. And I checked, because I am extremely petty and I had the time."

"I did not want to be thanked."

"Oh, that is *good*." Mukuro laughed, once, with real delight, and it was not a nice sound. "That is so good. Say it again with the face."

"It is not a performance."

"It is not a *lie*, either, which is what makes it good. I believe you completely." He shut the book. "You did not want to be thanked. And what I would like you to look at, since you are so extremely fond of looking at things and writing them down, is what has to be true about a person for that to be a *problem*."

Tsuna said nothing.

"Because it is not modesty. I have met modesty and it is boring and it goes home early." Mukuro held up one finger. "Being thanked is a transaction. Somebody hands you a thing and it has your name on it and it says *this was good and you did it*. And a normal person takes it, and puts it on a shelf, and it sits there being true whether or not they look at it." A second finger. "You cannot take it. If you take it you have to decide whether it is accurate. And you have decided in advance that it is not going to be accurate, so the entire transaction is a trap, so you stay three thousand kilometres away from all fifty-one of us where the paperwork lives, because *paper does not thank anybody*."

The fox came up out of the bag and onto Tsuna's shoulder and sat there.

Mukuro's eyes went over it. Past it. Over Tsuna's shoulder to the gate, and back. Nothing changed in his face at any point.

*He cannot see it,* Tsuna thought, with a relief he was ashamed of.

*One in eleven in a generation. He is a **Mist** who will be frightening in about four years, and he is standing two metres away, and he cannot see it.*

*And what does that mean? That it is rarer than rare. Or that it is something else entirely, and nobody has ever measured the right thing.*

"I am going to say the unpleasant one now," said Mukuro.

"You have been saying unpleasant ones for six minutes."

"Those were warm-up." He came down off the crate in one movement, which he had clearly practised, and landed lightly, and was very short. "You did not stay away to protect us from an awkward afternoon. You stayed away because as long as none of us has ever spoken to you, we are a *number*. Fifty-one is a number. You can check a number eleven times and it will never once look back at you and be a person you failed in some other way."

"That is not…"

"It is not what?" said Mukuro pleasantly.

And Tsuna had run an organisation of nine thousand people. He had negotiated with a man older than the country he was standing in. He had faced a **Sky** at the end of a platform with the lid entirely off, and had kept his voice level.

He stood in a yard in Taki in front of an eight year old, and could not finish the sentence.

"There we are," said Mukuro.

He picked up a scarf off the crate and wound it round his neck with an air of enormous self-satisfaction.

"For the avoidance of doubt," he said, "I am not grateful. I want that established. I would have got out. It would have taken me another four years. I would have been a considerably worse person at the end of it, and there would have been a great deal more blood. None of that is gratitude. It is arithmetic." He tucked the end in. "What I *am* is extremely irritated, because you did a correct thing for a bad reason and you have spent eighteen months making sure it cost you something, and I have had to watch it from a yard."

"You could have written."

"Yes," said Mukuro. "I could have."

He said it without any weight at all, and walked past Tsuna toward the gate, and it took four full steps before the shape of it arrived.

*He knew where I was,* Tsuna thought. *For ten months. He had a name and a prefecture and a shrine, and he is eight, and he did not come either.*

*Neither of us came.*

"Mukuro."

"I am aware," said Mukuro, at the gate, without turning round. "I have been aware the entire time, and I find it humiliating, and I have decided to be angry at you about it instead, which is working extremely well and I intend to continue." He opened the gate. "Come on. You are going to be late, and the woman from the prefecture has a clipboard and a lanyard, and people like that write things down when you are late."

## 6.5

The handover was in the community hall, because the community hall is where the prefecture does things that need a table and a kettle and an official number of chairs.

There were nine people in it, and not one **Flame** among them worth the name, which he checked before he sat down because he has never once walked into a room and not checked.

The woman from the prefecture had a clipboard and a lanyard and the tired competence of somebody doing her fourth placement that month. The Ōtani family, who were four adults and a daughter of about fifteen and had brought, for reasons that became clear later, an extremely large cake. A man from the school. And a boy of eight in a good coat who had walked in as though the room belonged to him and had taken the chair nearest the door.

Tsuna signed in the box.

It took eleven seconds. His hand did the thing it had done ten thousand times in another life. A signature a stranger could read and could not copy. Then he put the pen down in the tray and straightened the form with two fingers. The woman from the prefecture said *thank you* without looking up, and the entire legal instrument closed with a small administrative click.

That was it. That was the whole of it. He had a bus at four and a form in his pocket with a stamp on it, and every single thing he had come here to do was finished at twenty-five past eleven in the morning.

He stood up.

"Sit down," said Mukuro, in Japanese, pleasantly, to the room.

"I have a bus."

"You have four buses. I checked the timetable, because unlike you I prepare." He had not moved from the chair by the door, which Tsuna now understood was not where you sit to be near the door. It is where you sit to be *in* it. "Sit down and have some cake, and then when she comes in you can leave, and I will not say a word."

*He is eight,* Tsuna thought, with a kind of despair. *He is eight and he has put me in a room.*

He sat down.

Elena came in at ten to twelve with a social worker and a suitcase and the flat grey expression of a child who has been in institutional rooms for five weeks and has learned exactly how much of herself to bring into one.

She was eleven. She was taller than the file had said, because files are always four months old. She held her left hand slightly against her body in the way that people do when a hand does not do everything and they have got tired of explaining it, She looked round the room once, counting. It was the flat unhurried count of somebody for whom counting rooms is a habit and not an alarm.

The Ōtani grandmother stood up and said, in carefully learned Italian, that they were very glad, and that there was cake, and that nobody had to eat the cake.

Something in Elena's face moved about two millimetres.

And Tsuna, who had come to this town with the entire business already closed, sat in a plastic chair at the back of a community hall and watched a child work out that the cake was a real offer.

*Good,* he thought. *Good. That is the correct family. The file was right. The third child has the same condition in the same hand and the grandmother has already learned the word for it.*

*I will go at twelve.*

"That one paid for the lawyer," said Mukuro, in Italian, clearly, into a lull.

The room did not react, because the room did not speak Italian.

Elena turned round.

There are eleven things Tsuna could have done. He had used most of them at one time or another.

What he did was sit very still and look at an eleven year old girl across four metres of community hall floor, while an eight year old enjoyed himself enormously in his peripheral vision.

"You?" said Elena.

"…Yes."

"You are *small*!"

"I am seven."

"I know how old you are. They told me on the plane." She frowned at him with the frank and slightly offended attention of somebody re-pricing a thing. "They said a family. They said a private arrangement, and a family, and I thought it was a man in an office. It is a person from my class."

"That is roughly accurate."

"It is not *roughly* anything, it is extremely strange." She put the suitcase down. "Why?"

And there it was, and it had arrived in eleven minutes rather than in eighteen months, and it was not *thank you* at all, which was the part he had not prepared for.

*Because the ledger said fifty-two and the list said fifty-one,* he thought. *Because somebody wrote unsuitable in a box and everybody counted the same number twice and went home. Because it was wrong and I could see that it was wrong and there was no one else standing there.*

*And because if I had not, I would have had to live with the number.*

"Because they counted wrong," he said, "and I could see the place where they counted wrong, and it was going to stay wrong."

Elena considered this.

"That is a terrible reason," she said.

"Yes."

"Do people usually give you better ones? Do you *take* better ones?"

"No, and no."

"It is *very* boring." She was, he noticed, entirely unembarrassed. That is a thing about being eleven. "I have been thinking about it for five weeks. I thought it was going to be something. A relative. A debt. Somebody who knew my mother." She picked at the handle of the suitcase with her right hand. "And it is an accounting error."

"I am sorry it is an accounting error."

"No, it is better," said Elena. "A relative wants something."

She sat down two chairs away, which was not near him and was not far. She accepted a piece of cake on a paper plate with the air of a person conducting an experiment, and ate some of it.

Then she said, without looking over:

"They said I can keep my name or change it."

"That is true. It is written into it. You can do it at sixteen, or before, if you want it before."

"Which did you think I would do?"

"I did not think about it," said Tsuna, and heard himself, and felt something go slightly cold. "That is not true. I thought about it for two days. I put the clause in so that nobody else would get to decide, and then I made myself stop, because the point of the clause is that it is not mine."

Elena chewed.

"That is a better answer," she said.

Nobody thanked him. She did not thank him. The Ōtani family did not know there was anything to thank him for. The woman from the prefecture packed her clipboard into a canvas bag and left at half past twelve, to do a placement in Matsusaka.

And Tsuna sat in the plastic chair for another twenty minutes with a paper plate on his knee and did not feel forgiven, or lighter, or absolved… and did feel, in a small, specific, thoroughly unwelcome way, *known about*.

*One,* he thought. *There. One of fifty-one has spoken to me, and the ceiling did not come down, and I have not been asked to be anything.*

*Fifty. There are fifty left. I have their addresses in my pocket.*

He did not go to any of them. He was not going to do fifty in an afternoon, and he was not going to pretend otherwise. There is a kind of man who would have tried. He had been that man for a while in his thirties, and it had made him dangerous to work for.

But on the way out he stopped at the noticeboard by the hall door. Somebody had pinned up the class list with the eleven katakana names. Four of them had been written out underneath in Roman letters, in a second hand.

He took the pencil off the string and wrote the fifth one, which had been left off, because he happened to know how it was spelled.

Mukuro watched him do it from the door with an expression of profound disgust.

"That," he said, "is the most *you* thing I have ever seen a person do."

"It was spelled wrong."

"It was *unwritten*, which is different, and you have fixed it anonymously in pencil on a noticeboard, and you are going to get on a bus." He pulled his scarf up. "Go home, Sawada. Come back in the spring, and come to an address, and do not send anything in the post. If you send anything in the post I will burn it in the yard and tell everyone you did it."

"That is not a real threat."

"No," Mukuro agreed. "It is a *plan*. I never make threats, they are for people with nothing prepared."

## 6.6

He missed the four o'clock on purpose.

There was no reason for it that would have survived being written down. The next bus was at ten past five and the one after that was the last, and the sensible thing was the four, and he stood at the stop and watched it come round the corner and did not put his hand out.

Then he walked.

The coast road out of the valley goes up before it goes down, and by the time he came over the shoulder of the hill the light had gone that particular thin orange that January gets for about twenty minutes and then abandons. Below him on the left the sea. Ahead, thirty-eight kilometres of road and the whole of Ise Bay going grey. Behind him, a town with fifty-one children in it, one of whom had told him his reason was boring.

He had the **Cloud** up.

He had put it up at the edge of Taki without deciding to, and had noticed about a kilometre later. That had never happened before. A **Sky** does not go up by itself; a **Sky** has to be let out, and he had spent four years learning exactly how not to. The other thing, apparently, had learned to open its own door.

*I am not hiding,* he thought, and then, because he had spent two lifetimes being honest with himself about exactly one subject and it was this one: *no. I am not hiding. I am walking home with a wall around me on an empty road where there is nobody to keep off. And I have kept it up anyway. I have had it up since I was three… and I do not know how to be in a road without it.*

The fox was asleep in the bag with its nose out.

He did the arithmetic. He did it properly, sitting on the parapet at the turning above Kawaba with the bag on his knees, because he had been doing it badly in his head for four hours and badly is worse than not at all.

*Five hundred and sixty seven plans.*

*How many of them have a step in them where somebody else does the thing?*

He went through it. Not all of them; he was not going to get through five hundred and sixty seven on a wall in the dark. He went through the eleven he could recite, which were the eleven that mattered most, the ones with dates attached and people's lives in the second column… and the answer came back, and he made himself look at it.

*Two.*

*Two of eleven have a step where the work is done by somebody who is not me. Two. Out of eleven.*

*Not one of them has a line for a **Storm** who steps in front of a knife, or a **Mist** who stops one at four centimetres without looking.*

* In one of those two, the person is my grandmother, and I wrote her in because she is seventy-four and I was being sentimental.*

He sat with that on a cold parapet thirty-eight kilometres from home.

*Namimori answered a **Sky** of the Vongola on a Monday morning,* he thought. *With a piece of paper. With an old woman reading badly.*

*And how many of my eleven best plans have a line in them for that? Nine of them do not. Nine.*

*I did not leave them out because I thought they were weak.*

*I left them out because I did not think of them.*

Down on the road a small van went past with its lights on and did not slow, because there was nothing on the parapet that anybody wanted to be near.

He got up. He shouldered the bag. He thought, with a lift he did not entirely trust and decided to have anyway, that he would be home by nine. Ayame would be at the gate whether she was allowed to be or not. There would be a fifth account of the knife by now, with an entirely new adjective in it.

And at some point next week he was going to have to climb a wall above the north road and say something to a nine year old **Getsufuji** about what standing still had cost him.

*And what am I going to say? What is there? Thank you is an insult and sorry is worse.*

He went down the hill toward the coast road with his **Cloud** up and a sleeping fox in a bag.

Behind him, Taki. Ahead, the long road, the junction, the last bus he was now going to miss as well.

Musashi expected him at nine. Ayame expected him at eight and would say nine. The shrine office had a note on the board saying *Taki, one day*, in Ayame's hand, with the character for *back* and no time against it, because he had not given one.

And from the moment he came down off that parapet, on an unlit stretch of prefectural road between a valley and the sea, there was not one person alive who could have said where he was.
