# Local Fox Develops Opinions About The Mail. Local Ex-Mafia Boss Develops Opinions About Himself.

## 3.1

The mail came at seven, and by five past seven it was reorganised by something that did not officially exist.

Tsuna sat on the step with his knees up and watched it happen. The bundle arrived in Ayame's hands, tied with the same waxed string the post office used since before he was born in either life. She set it on the low table inside the door, bowed to the room in general, and left. The room in general was empty except for him.

Then the string came undone.

It came undone slowly, which was the part that still made him want to laugh. Whatever was doing it had no thumbs… and never let that stop it. The knot turned over on itself, sulked, gave up, and slid apart. The stack fanned, and envelopes began to travel.

The heavy one from the prefectural office went to the bottom. The one from the shrine association in Mie went second from the bottom, which was a demotion from last month.

They were all the same letter underneath, and had been for a year. *Kohaku-sama, we have heard a thing. Kohaku-sama, is it true that Namimori has a Kotei again. Kohaku-sama, the Sanctuary contract, does it stand, does it stand for us, Tenmei-sama, please.*

Four hundred years of a Honō society gone quietly feral in the dark. Then one Sky woke up in a small town, and every shrine on this coast started writing letters about the Sanctuary. Two identical envelopes from two different men who both wanted the same piece of land near the river were placed side by side, squared off, and then, after a pause, swapped.

"Those two are the same letter, word for word," Tsuna said. The envelopes swapped back.

"You only did that because I said something out loud."

They swapped again, and this time one of them was nudged a full finger's width ahead of the other, which was either a ruling on the land dispute or an insult. He could not tell… he stopped being able to tell around the fourth month.

Something small moved along the edge of the table. It was the length of his hand and the colour of wet straw, and it had a black smudge over one eye that was less a marking than a habit. It went to the good envelope. It sat on the good envelope. It looked at him.

"I can see you sitting on it," he said. It kept sitting on it.

"That isn't a legal procedure, and you know perfectly well it isn't."

Then it waited. It had learned somewhere in the last year that waiting worked on him, and he resented how right it was.

"Is that a ruling?" he asked. "Are you *ruling* on this? You don't have jurisdiction, and you don't have a *body*."

The tail went over the corner of the envelope, tidily, the way a clerk squares a document before sliding it across a desk.

He picked up the envelope it wanted him to pick up.

It was from Kaneko-san, who ran the paper shop by the second torii and who wrote to the house roughly once a season about nothing at all. She had beautiful handwriting and no business with any of them. She wrote because she liked writing. That was, as far as Tsuna could tell, the entire basis of the correspondence, and it was also the entire basis on which the fox had decided she outranked the prefectural office.

*Of course she does,* he thought. *She's the only one who isn't asking for anything.*

He read three lines about a cat that had moved into the shop and would not move out. The cat had opinions about the display window. The cat, last Thursday, sat on a customer.

He laughed, once, out loud, in an empty room at ten past seven in the morning.

"She's writing to us about a *cat*," he said. "Do you understand how much of my week is land disputes? Do you have the faintest idea?"

Something small radiated satisfaction from the middle of the table.

"Don't look at me like that," he said. "Don't you dare be smug about a paper shop."

It was extremely smug.

This was the year he lived. It was made almost entirely of mornings like this one, and he had not decided yet whether that was a small life or an enormous one.

He knew what it was, at least the way a man knows the name of his own disease. He knew within about four minutes of opening the tube, which was the fastest he ever understood anything in either lifetime. And he spent the year since then understanding it worse and worse, which is what happens with things that stay.

A *kudagitsune*. A pipe fox, a Flame in a tube. The kind of thing that lived in a length of bamboo in the sleeve of a mountain diviner, gathered what could be gathered, and whispered it back. The kind of thing nobody else could see.

Nobody else could see it. He tested that early and thoroughly, in the way of a man who spent decades not taking anyone's word for anything, and the results were consistent and unsettling. Grandmother's eyes went over it. Indra's eyes went over it, and Indra noticed the lint on a stranger's collar at forty paces. Ayame set a tray down on top of it once and did not feel the difference.

So he was a seven year old boy who talked to the air, in a house full of people who had every reason to worry about him. What those people did about it, consistently, for a year, was move the teapot.

Nagi did that. She was doing it now, from the doorway, without breaking stride. The pot went from the near end of the table to the far end, out of range of the invisible thing that once put a paw in it. She carried on into the kitchen as though rerouting a teapot around a *yōkai* were an ordinary item in a morning.

"Thank you," he said.

"Mm," said Nagi.

She did not ask, and she never once had. In the first weeks he braced for it every single day… rehearsed three different answers, ranked by how much of the truth each one cost. And then he slowly understood that the question was not coming. Not because she was incurious. Because she lived in this house too, and in this house you learned early that there were a lot of things it was kinder not to make someone explain.

He was not sure whether that was the most generous thing anyone had ever done for him or the saddest.

*Both,* the morning suggested. *It can be both.*

The fox got off the envelope and went to the window. Outside, the cold came down overnight and set the garden hard, and the *sakaki* by the wall had that black wet look that old leaves get in the first real frost. The fox watched the tree the way it always watched the tree, which was to say the way a person watches a door they are not going through.

"Leave the tree alone," Tsuna said. "We have had this conversation eleven times." It did not turn around.

"I mean it, and you know exactly how much I mean it." It flicked an ear and stayed exactly where it was, and he let it, because in a year he never once won that particular argument… and he started to suspect he was not supposed to.

Indra came through at the turn of the corridor with her hair still up from practice and a cup in each hand. She looked at the table. She looked at the fanned, resorted, editorially reorganised stack of the household's correspondence, in an order that no living person had put it in.

"Somebody has been efficient this morning," she said, without slowing down.

"It's a system, and it has been running quite happily for about a year."

"Whose?"

"It is a *collaborative* system," said Tsuna, "and I would rather not go into the details before breakfast."

Indra made a sound that was not agreement and was not doubt. She had been making that sound at him for a year and a half now, ever since the day she put her spear down in front of a six year old. He had never once managed to get underneath it.

She set one of the cups by his knee on the way past, which was the sound's translation, roughly.

He gathered the mail into the order the fox had made. He did not correct any of it.

It could not read Flame, that was the strange part, but it could read a *man*. Hand it a letter from a Ruriko of forty years' standing and it would tell you what she was frightened of, and never once tell you she was a Mist. Whatever the tube sent him, it was not a sensor.

*Which is fine,* he thought. *I've got two Mists, a Rain and a Storm in this house, and between the four of them they couldn't read a letter if it was on fire.*

That was unfair to Indra, who read a room the way an inverse Rain reads one, which is to say completely and about forty minutes late. It was unfair to Grandmother, who was a Ruriko and had been one since before the war and who missed nothing. It was extremely fair to Nagi, who was going to be the most dangerous Mist on this island in about fifteen years and who currently could not be trusted with a shopping list. The prefectural office would keep until after breakfast. The men who wanted the land could have their answer in the afternoon, and by then the fox would have a view. The infuriating thing, the thing he stopped mentioning out loud, was that the view would be correct.

At the door he stopped.

"Hey," he said. The ear came around.

He opened his mouth and found nothing in it. There was a word that went in the gap, and he did not have it… three hundred and some mornings, and still no word. The shape of the absence was so familiar by now that he built his sentences to go around it without slowing down.

"Nothing, forget I said anything," he said. "Come on, or you will miss the rice." It came.

## 3.2

The room he worked in had been a storeroom, and before that a room for drying something, and before that nobody remembered. Grandmother had given it to him without ceremony the winter he turned six, which was the same winter she stopped pretending not to know what he was doing in it.

There was a low desk. There was a box of paper. There was a stack of ledgers with nothing written on their spines, because a label is a gift to whoever finds it.

And there was the method.

The method was this. He read a thing, then set it down. He read it again, with the first reading deliberately out of his head, which was a skill and had taken years. Then he wrote what he thought it meant on a separate sheet, and then he read it a third time against what he had written, and only then did he act.

Three passes. Every time. On everything.

Grandmother watched him do it once, from the doorway, when he was five. She said nothing about it for two days. Then, over breakfast, apropos of nothing, she remarked that a man who checks the rice three times is not checking the *rice*.

He thought about that for a week… and then went on doing it.

It was, he knew perfectly well, a ridiculous way to run a life. In his first one it had been a ridiculous way to run a famiglia. Reborn told him so with a boot, on six separate occasions, and once made the point with a chair.

He had gone on doing it anyway. Somewhere in his twenties he worked out the arithmetic that governed him… and after that there was no arguing with it.

The arithmetic was simple: his intuition was the fastest thing he owned. His intuition was also, in this life, missing most of an arm. What was left came through thin and late and sometimes not at all, and a man who has lost the sense he used to steer by does not get to steer by feel. He gets to count instead.

So he counted. Three passes. Every time.

The letter from the shrine association in Mie was nine pages long, which was six more pages than it needed. He was two paragraphs into the first pass when the fox took it off him.

"Hey."

It went up the sleeve of his *haori* with the pages folded under it, which should not have been physically possible. It came out at the collar, sat on his shoulder, and began to read.

He could feel it reading. That was a thing he had never got used to and did not expect to. It came in as a pressure at the back of his thoughts, unhurried, thorough, like someone going through a shelf in another room.

"Give that back, I was two paragraphs into it," he said. It did not give it back.

"I have a *method*, and you are currently sitting on the second pass of it." The pressure did not care about his method.

And Tsuna sat there in the cold room with a length of warm weight against his neck, and he had a thought he had been circling for about a month and had not let himself land on, which was this.

It read faster than he did.

Not a little faster. Substantially. It had gone through nine pages in the time it took him to get through two. He had watched it do this with the mail every morning for a year, and never once caught it wrong. Not on a name. Not on a number. Not on the thing under the sentence that the sentence was built to hide.

Which meant what, exactly? That the three passes were not protecting him from anything. That they had never been protecting him from anything, not for a year, and that all they were doing now was costing him a morning.

"All right," he said. The fox stopped reading.

"I said all right, so go on and tell me what is actually in it."

There was a pause. In the pause he had time to notice that his hands had gone still on the desk, flat, the way they went when he was about to do something he had not cleared with himself.

*This is stupid,* he thought. *This is objectively stupid. What do I actually know about it? That it reads. That it has never been wrong. Is that a record, or is that just a run?*

*And how would I know the difference, if I never let it be wrong?* Then it told him.

It did not use words, because it did not have any and never had. What it had was better and worse than words. It put the shape of the nine pages into him whole, the way a stone tells a hand its weight long before it tells anyone its name. The association wanted three things, said it wanted one, and the one it said was a courtesy wrapped around the third. There was a man's name on page six who should not have been on page six. There was a date in the fourth paragraph that did not match the date on the seal.

And under all of it, quiet and entire, the actual request. *Please tell us where we stand with Namimori now, because everything has changed and nobody will say so out loud.*

Tsuna sat with it.

He knew exactly what he was supposed to do next. He was supposed to unfold the pages and check it himself. Page six, the fourth paragraph, the seal. Two minutes, and he had done it ten thousand times. His hand was already halfway there.

He put the hand back down.

"Right," he said. "Then that is what the letter says, and I'll write to that."

He took a fresh sheet and wrote the reply straight through. And he did not read the letter, not once, not at the end, not even to check the spelling of the name on page six.

It took eleven minutes, where it usually took him an hour and ten. He signed it, sealed it, and set it on the corner of the desk for Ayame. The whole time there was a small dense knot of Flame sitting on his collarbone, saying nothing at all. He could not have told you which of them was more unnerved.

Because here was the thing he was not going to say out loud, in this room, to this animal, at seven years old, in the dark end of the year.

He knew what it was made of.

He had known since the fourth minute. A box weapon is not a companion that a Flame happens to power. A box weapon *is* the Flame, given a shape and a name and a stubborn little opinion of its own, and it comes out of the man who made it the way a word comes out of a mouth. Whatever else this thing was now, whatever it had climbed into on the way down, it had started as a piece of his Flame.

So when he handed it a letter and did not check the letter, he was not doing what it looked like.

He was trusting his own reading, and he had just gone the long way round to get there. He did not say that, and he did not say any of it.

He straightened the ledgers instead, and then rolled his shoulder so that the weight on it had to resettle. It resettled with the extremely put upon air of something that had been perfectly comfortable where it was.

"Don't," he said. Smug.

"You are not even slightly subtle, and you have never tried to be." Smugger, if that was a word.

And then, because the room was cold and quiet and his guard was down by exactly the amount that eleven unchecked minutes cost him, he felt it.

Something in his chest moved a fraction of an inch toward the middle.

It was not painful. That was what he noticed first, and what he would think about for the rest of the day. It was not painful, and it was not sudden, and it did not feel like anything arriving. It felt like a room being slightly less far from another room. It felt like a door that had been across the house being, now, only across the hall.

He sat very still, and out on the shoulder the fox went still too.

"What was that," he said. "And don't sit there pretending you didn't do it."

It did not answer. It was not, it had made extremely clear over the course of a year, the sort of thing you got answers out of.

But it did not move away either, and after a while he stopped waiting for either one.

## 3.3

He did not go looking that day. He waited two days, which was cowardice dressed as method, and he knew it was cowardice at the time, and he waited anyway.

On the third night he went in.

The way in was easier than it used to be, and he noticed that before he noticed anything else, which should have told him something. There was a time when going down into his own soulscape was like getting into cold water. Now it was like stepping off a low step in the dark: one moment the room, the next the other place, and the only sign of transition a small drop in his stomach.

The other place had weather. It always had. Tonight it was the tail end of a rain, and the ground gave under him, and the air smelled the way the air behind the shrine smelled in the hour after a storm.

And there was the tree.

It was not a *sakaki*, exactly. It had the shape of the one by the wall the way a word in a dream has the shape of a word. It went up further than it should, and its roots went sideways further than they should. Somewhere in the third or fourth year of this life he had stopped trying to describe it to himself and started simply calling it *the tree*, which is what everyone does eventually with the largest thing in their landscape.

In the branch system of that tree, threaded through it like light through a lattice, was his Flame.

Not a metaphor for his Flame. The Flame. A Sky core, out of its own crux, held in the neutral core of a *sakaki* standing in that garden since before the Sanctuary contract was written.

All of it. Every year of it. Sixty some years of Sky, getting heavier and hotter and more particular the entire time… because that is what his kind of Flame does. By the end of the first life, people stopped standing close to him at functions without meaning it as an insult. All of that, poured into a network of wood, because the alternative was a small child's ribcage, and the ribcage would have lost.

That was the whole disease in one line. Sixty years of Sky, a body that could hold about four, and a *sakaki* doing the rest.

*Your body did that,* Kawahira had said, in a tone Tsuna disliked intensely. *Your enemies did not do it. Your bad luck did not do it. Your body looked at what came down with you and made a decision.*

He walked out under it and looked up, and he did what he came to do, which was to count.

He had a method for this too. You could not measure a soulscape with a ruler, but you could measure a soulscape against the last one. He did that four times a year from the moment he was old enough to hold the shape of it in his head. Where the light sat densest. How far out along the branches it ran. How much of it answered when he called and how quickly.

He called.

It answered fast.

It did not answer politely, and it did not answer the way it used to, which had been the way a house answers when you shout up the stairs and then wait. This came the moment he opened his mouth.

He stood there in his own head in the wet dark, and the whole structure turned toward him like a field of grass turning in wind. It came so fast and so completely that he lost his footing on ground that was not ground.

"All right," he said out loud, in a place with nobody in it. "All right. All right."

He did it twice more to be sure. Then a fourth time, because twice more is not a sample, and he was the kind of person who knew that and could not leave it alone.

Four for four, which is a record and not a run. Then he made himself do the other measurement, the honest one, the one he had been not doing for three days.

He found the near end of it. The place where the tree's system came closest to the crux, which in this landscape was also the place where the thing in his chest was closest to the thing in the world.

The crux. Everyone else's Flame sat in theirs and stayed there and never once had to be discussed. His had been evicted from its crux by a body doing sums in an emergency, and every measurement he had taken since had been a measurement of that one distance. He had a mark for it. He had put the mark there at four years old with the absolute seriousness of a small child, and the absolute seriousness of a man who had run an organisation for thirty years. Those were, he had learned, the same kind of seriousness.

The mark was nine paces behind him.

He turned around and looked at it and did the arithmetic, and the arithmetic was not complicated, because the whole point of a mark is that it does not move.

Nine paces.

Last autumn: eleven.

The autumn before that it had been eleven, and the spring before that it had been eleven, and it had been eleven for as long as he had been counting, because that was the distance his body had chosen and bodies do not renegotiate.

"Oh," he said.

He sat down on the wet ground of his own soul and put his hands over his mouth.

The drifting had stopped in the winter he was six. That was the fact he had been living on for a year. The tube came, and the thing in the tube came, and about eleven days after that he went a full week without his mind pulling loose from his body and down the Flame like a boat off a mooring. Then a month. Then a season.

So he had filed it under *solved*, and he had gone back to work.

He had been wrong about what he was looking at. He had seen a symptom stop and called it an ending, and it was not an ending, it was the first two paces of something that had never once stopped since. Every morning. Every stupid argument about the mail. Every hour of a year, that Flame had been sitting on his shoulder doing a job so slowly that he could not see it happening from inside his own life.

Two paces. In a *year*. He said it out loud, to see whether it sounded better in the air than it did in his head. It did not.

He worked out the number before he could stop himself, the way he always did. Two paces a year, against a distance he had never measured properly, because he had never once thought he would *need* to. Decades. Two lifetimes of afternoons.

And it would not be enough even then, and he knew that too, and that was the part that made him tired rather than frightened.

Because Kawahira had been very clear, in that flat old voice, sitting on a rock with his sandals off. *You may get it close. You will not get it to the crux. Not in that body, and not in the one after it, and probably not in any body that grows the ordinary way. A Sky like yours goes on getting denser until the day it stops, and a boy is a boy. At some point, Sawada Tsunayoshi, you are going to have to build something.*

He had not asked *build what*. He had known better. There are questions you only ask when you have the strength to hear an answer, and at six years old, on a cold rock, with his feet not reaching the ground, he had not had it.

*Do I have it now?* he thought. He did not.

He sat with that, and then he sat with the other thing, which had been waiting patiently underneath the whole time and which was, he understood, the actual reason he had waited three days.

His body had pushed it out.

No enemy had done it. The fall had not done it. Neither had the reincarnation, or the transmigration, or whichever of the two it had actually been. His intuition had insisted on both, which was a problem filed for another year. His own body, doing the only arithmetic available to it, had looked at what came down with him and had shoved it into a tree. A child who holds that much does not stay a child. Does not stay anything.

It saved his life… and something was undoing it.

Gently. Two paces a year. In doses so small that a man who counted everything had not caught it for four seasons. A thing the size of his hand had been carrying his own Flame back across the garden one mouthful at a time, And every single one of those mouthfuls was a reversal of the decision that kept him alive.

*Does it know?* he thought. *Does it know what it's doing?* And then, worse, and he felt the cold of it go down the back of his neck in a place that did not have a neck.

*Do I know how fast it should go?*

Because that was the thing. He wanted it faster. He had wanted it faster since the first morning of this life. Every time a conversation went past him that the old him would have caught. Every time he had to count instead of simply *knowing*. Every time he stood in a room and reached for the shape of it and got thin air where his arm should be. He would have taken the whole Sky back tonight. Standing here, right now, he would have said yes.

And his body, which had never been consulted and had never been wrong, would have said no. So he made himself do the rest of it properly, because a measurement you only take once is a rumour.

Density, out along the second and third branch systems of the Flame: up. Reach, out to the far ends where it had always gone thin and unreliable: up, and not by a little. Response time: he had already done that one three times and he was not going to enjoy it a fourth.

Colour. That one he did last, always, because it was the only measurement that had never moved and he liked having something that did not move. Sky. Still Sky. Still the particular late orange his had gone in the last decade of the first life, when people had started calling it Kohaku to his face instead of behind his back, and when the Ninth had looked at it once across a table and gone quiet.

And the thing he had never in his life been able to measure, which was how much of it there was. Sixty years of Sky does not come with a number on it. You cannot weigh a fire. All you can do is stand in front of it and notice whether you flinch.

He stood in front of it.

A Sky is not supposed to frighten its own owner. That is in no manual anywhere. A Sky is the warm one, the one everybody else stands near, the one that the Elements of a Sky come home to.

He flinched. *Good,* he thought, unsteadily. *Still too big for me. That part of the Flame is still working.*

*How fast is it going?* he thought. *And who decided? Because it wasn't me, and it wasn't my body, and that leaves exactly one candidate, and it weighs about as much as a bread roll.*

He stayed down there a while longer.

When he came up, the room was the room, and the brazier had gone to ash, and there was a small weight in the crook of his arm that had definitely not been there when he lay down.

"You are a great deal heavier than you look," he said, and his voice came out wrecked.

The weight did not move.

"I worked it out while you were asleep, or whatever it is you do at night." It did not move.

"Decades," he said. "You know that, don't you? You are doing something that takes decades, and you are doing it by *post*."

Nothing. A slow ear.

He put his hand over it. Under the fur there was a heartbeat going at the speed small things go, four or five for every one of his. And it was warm, and it was the warmth of a Flame he *recognised*, and that recognition was the oldest and least examined thing in his entire life.

"Hey," he said. "Do you actually know who you are?" The heartbeat went on at its ridiculous speed.

"Because I have been sitting down here for three days finding out that I don't."

The fur moved under his hand. It turned itself over, put its chin down on his wrist, and stayed.

It did not have an answer. It did not have one for him, and it did not have one for itself either… neither of them was going to get an answer out of the other tonight.

So at some point in the next hour he stopped waiting for one. So he lay there in the dark, in a cold room, with his hand on a warm thing that was exactly as lost as he was.

It was not a solution… but it was better than the three days.

## 3.4

The old man had sent it.

That was the fact that lived under all of this. Tsuna could go a week without touching it. Then some morning the fox would do something impossible with a knot, and the whole thing would come up out of the floor of him at once.

Because a *kudagitsune* is not a thing you find. It is a thing that is *given*, by somebody who has one to give. And there was exactly one person in this world who walked up the road to this house in a hat and sandals, and then walked away again without explaining himself.

Seven days after the wedding. A length of bamboo on the step, in the rain, with his name on it in a hand older than the alphabet it was written in.

*This should help with your problem.*

Nine characters. Not one of them a promise.

He had not seen Kawahira since. Nobody in this house knew to find that strange, and Tsuna found it strangest of all, because you do not do what that man did and then simply stop coming.

The negotiation ran three days.

Everyone in the household believed it ran one. Grandmother thought it was an afternoon with a strange guest. Indra thought it was a piece of business. The town thought whatever the town thought, which was usually closer than either.

Three days. The first two were about the Estraneo, and he could have walked away after the second with everything he came for.

He went back on the third day for himself. That was the only time in either life he could remember doing that.

The old man was by the river, on the same flat rock, with his feet in water cold enough to hurt.

"You came back," he said, without turning around.

"You knew I would come back, because you said so yourself on the first day."

"I *hoped*, which is a different animal, and I get the two of them mixed up less often than I used to." A pause, and the water going over. "Sit down, Decimo."

"Don't call me that, because I haven't been that for a very long time."

"Ah." Kawahira looked at him sideways, with an expression Tsuna could not read at six and could not read now. "Is that where we are? Already?"

Tsuna sat down. His feet did not reach the water. He remembered being furious about that with a purity of fury only available to small bodies, and he remembered making himself say it anyway.

"I need a Guardian."

"You need six."

"I'm aware of how many I need!"

"Are you? Because you said it extremely calmly for a person who is aware."

"I need *one*." He kept his voice level. He was proud of that at the time, and he was embarrassed about being proud of it now. "My Flame is in a tree, and you saw it yourself. It answers me but it does not hold, and every time it comes loose I go with it, and one of these times I am not coming back up. I cannot anchor that Flame alone, and I have been trying for four years."

"And you want the anchor to be me."

"I want the anchor to be somebody who can't be leaned on. Everyone else in my life can be got at. You can't."

"You want to harmonise with me."

"I want to harmonise with *someone*, because the Flame will not sit. It answers and it drifts and it answers and it drifts… and a Sky is supposed to be the thing that other people are anchored *to*, and mine is in a tree."

The old man laughed. It was not a nice sound. It was the laugh of somebody hearing a very young person describe them accurately.

"You have not thought about this," Kawahira said.

"I have thought about nothing else for four years, which is most of this life."

"You have thought about what it does for *you*. That is not the same activity." He took his feet out of the water and set them on the rock, and folded his hands. "Do you know what harmonisation is from the inside, for the Guardian? I do not mean the diagrams, boy, I mean from the *inside*."

"Then tell me, because in all that time nobody ever has."

"It is a choice that eats every later choice." He said it without drama, which was what made it land. "It is not obedience, and nobody has ever been able to make me obey anything. It is a *direction*, and you would become the thing that everything else in me is measured against, and I would not be able to stop measuring… and I have spent a very long time arranging my affairs so that nothing is a direction any more."

"Why would a man arrange his whole life that way?"

"Because I am waiting to be finished." The water went over the stones. "You know some of what I am, but you do not know how long. I have carried a job since before this island had a name, and there is one road out of it, and it is very far away… and the only thing I have needed, for a considerable number of centuries, is to be allowed to keep walking toward it without being *held*."

And Tsuna sat there, seven days before a bamboo tube arrived on his step, and understood exactly what he was asking for.

"So it costs you the end," he said.

"It postpones the end, which is not quite the same insult."

"Postpones it by how much?"

"By *you*." Kawahira shrugged. "That is what harmonisation is, in the end. A Guardian does not get to put the weight down while the Sky is still standing, however long you happen to live… and you are seven kinds of stubborn, so I would budget generously."

"That's not a price. That's a sentence."

"They are frequently the same document."

"Then *say no*, because harmonisation is a choice, and that is the only rule the Flame has that nobody anywhere can bend." Tsuna heard his own voice crack on it, high and thin and six years old, and hated it. "You are allowed to, and I came here to ask you rather than to collect. If you say no then I will go home and find another way, and it will be worse, and I will do it anyway, and you will never hear a word about it."

"And why should any of that matter in the slightest to you?"

"Because I *asked*!" He threw a stone at the river, badly. "Because you're the only person I've met in either life who nobody's ever asked. They come and they want and they've decided before they get here, every one of them, for as long as there have been people to come… and not one of them ever sat down and said, *and what about you?*"

The water went over the stones.

"Hm," Kawahira said, in a different register than before. "You are going to be extremely exhausting."

Somewhere upstream a heron got something and made a noise about it.

"You'd be my first," Tsuna said, to the water. "You know that? Sixty years and six Guardians and a Vongola ring… and in this life I'm seven and I've got nobody. No Storm. No Rain. Nothing on either side. Just a tree."

"And you are offering me a position that binds for life."

"Yes."

"And in the same breath, telling me to refuse it." Tsuna looked at his knees.

"Then don't do it," he said. "I mean that, and I am not going to be the thing that holds your door shut. People have held doors shut for me my whole life, both of them, and I am not doing it to somebody who has been waiting since before there was an alphabet."

There was a silence then. It went on long enough that Tsuna started counting it, because he counted everything, and it reached eleven.

"Hm," Kawahira said again. "A thought about the last person who sat on this rock and tried to hire me. He did not do that."

"He didn't do what, exactly? Ask you what you wanted?"

"Offer me the exit." The old man looked out at the far bank. "He was, by every measure that anyone in your world uses, a better negotiator than you are. He prepared for nine months, and he knew what I was, which took him considerable effort… and he came with three arguments, a threat, and a very good grasp of my situation."

"And what happened to him in the end?"

"I said no to him, and he went away, and he died an ordinary death about four hundred years ago, and his name was excellent." Kawahira said it without any particular weight. "Your offer was worse than his in every respect but one."

"Worse in every respect but which one?"

"You meant it."

And then he said yes.

Tsuna had not understood, on the rock, why that was the thing. He was six, and exhausted, and mostly relieved. He took the yes and went home. And then he was violently sick behind the shrine wall, from the sheer descending weight of having got what he asked for.

He was seven now, in a cold room in the dark end of the year, and he had had a year to look at it. He thought he understood it a little better, and he did not like what he understood.

Because the old man wore a job for several thousand years, and the job was the Tri-Ni-Sette.

That was the whole of it, and it was never a curse or a punishment. It was a *post*. He was given a function, and he filled it with such total competence for such an unimaginable span that there was very nearly nothing left in there that was not the function. He shopped. He ate ramen. He wore a hat. And under the hat was a man who had been so long at his desk that the only thing he could still want was closing time.

A seven year old with somebody else's sixty years in his head had looked at that and felt his stomach drop… and had not known why until about four months ago, on a Tuesday, sorting letters.

*Because I'm doing it to myself.*

Nobody made him. That was the joke, and it was a good one, and he did not find it funny. No curse, no post, no unpayable stretch of centuries. He came down into a house with a grandmother in it, and a garden, and a town that fed him too much at festivals. And what did he do with that? He unpacked his sixty years of Sky, set out his list of five hundred and sixty seven things that needed fixing, sat down at the desk, and started work.

Seven years old. At a desk. Voluntarily.

He told himself it was gratitude, and then that it was debt, and then that it was efficiency. All three were true and none of them were the reason. The reason was simpler and more embarrassing. He knew how to be Vongola Decimo. He had thirty years of practice at being Vongola Decimo. He did not know how to be a boy in a house in Namimori, he never had, not in that life either… and the desk was the only room in the building where he knew where everything was.

*So: a habit, then. He needed several thousand years of it, and I got there in seven with nobody making me.*

And on the windowsill, in the last of the light, a small thing the colour of wet straw sat and watched the black tree and did not go to it.

Tsuna looked at it for a while.

It was given a job too. It came down out of a life with a name already attached, with a shape and a weight and a very clear idea of who it was supposed to be waiting for, and it landed in the wrong animal entirely. And every single day for a year, it declined.

It did not act like a lion, and it did not try. Not once in three hundred mornings did it attempt to be what it used to be. It sat on the mail and made rulings about stationery and put its chin on his wrist, and it refused, with perfect small stubbornness, to pretend the old name fitted.

*You're better at this than I am,* he thought. The fox flicked an ear.

"I didn't say anything," said Tsuna.

It went on looking at the tree.

## 3.5

The reply from the Vindice came in the last week of the year, in an envelope the fox would not touch.

That was new. It had opinions about everything, it had a ranking system with at least nine tiers that he had never fully reverse engineered, and it had never once refused a letter. This one came in at the bottom of the bundle. The fox looked at it, from a distance of about a foot, with both ears flat and every hair on its spine standing.

"Yes," said Tsuna. "I know. Go and sit somewhere else."

It did not go and sit somewhere else. It went and sat on his foot, which was worse.

The envelope was black, and not black the way paper is black. It had gone through somewhere on the way here. He picked it up and his hand told him, before anything else did, that this object had spent part of its journey in a place where time was a suggestion.

Inside was a single sheet, and on the sheet were four words in a hand like wire. *Explain the second clause.*

Tsuna sat down on the floor of the corridor with the letter in both hands and laughed until his ribs hurt.

"*Four words!*" he said, to an empty corridor. "He sat with it for a season and sent me *four words*!"

"He read it," he said. "He actually read it. He read the whole thing, that's a season, he sat with it for a whole season and then he wrote *four words*."

The fox on his foot was unimpressed.

"You don't understand. He could have not written anything. That's what he does. He's done it to emperors." He wiped his eyes with the back of his wrist. "He wrote back. *Explain the second clause.* Do you know what that means?"

It meant the second clause had worked.

He had written that letter in the spring, and it had taken him eleven days and three complete redrafts, and the thing that had made it hard was not the offer. The offer had been easy, because he had already made it once, to a man on a rock, and it had worked then for a reason he had not planned and could not have planned.

The hard part had been that he could only make it once.

Bermuda von Veckenschtein and Kawahira had nothing in common. Different eras, different species arguably, different grudges, no shared history that Tsuna had ever been able to find. One of them ran the Vindice… one of them ran the Tri-Ni-Sette, and ran it since before the Arcobaleno were a list of names rather than an idea. A Sky was supposed to find both of those facts terrifying. And in the one respect that mattered they were the same man. Both were given a job that would not end, and both were at it so long that the job ate the person… and neither could be bought with anything a living man usually has to offer.

You cannot bribe someone who will outlast the currency. You cannot threaten someone who wants it to stop. You cannot appeal to someone's future when they have run out of the capacity to imagine one.

There was exactly one thing left that such a person still wanted. It was the same thing in both cases. Tsuna had it, and that was the whole of his position, and he was not going to write down what it was even in his own head in a corridor.

A Sky is not supposed to be able to offer that. No Sky in the history of either organisation had ever been able to offer that. He could only offer it because of what the Flame in that tree was attached to, and the number of living people who knew what that Flame was attached to was two, and one of them wore a hat.

He was not going to think about the details of it in a corridor. He kept rules about where he let himself think about the list. The corridor was on the wrong side of that line. And the fox on his foot had a thorough, unfair habit of picking things up off the surface of him.

"Not here," he said. The ears came up.

"I said not in the corridor, and I meant it."

They stayed up, but it got off his foot, which in the treaty language they had developed over the year was a concession.

He worked on the answer for three days.

The first day he wrote nine pages and burned them, which was a habit he was not proud of and brought down with him, and the second day he sat and did nothing for six hours and then wrote one paragraph that was worth the six hours. On the third day the fox came back into the room, which it had not done since the envelope arrived, and sat on the far corner of the desk with its back half turned, like a man attending a funeral for someone he did not much like.

"You still don't want to be in this room, do you," Tsuna said. It did not move.

"Do you know what he *is*? Is that it?" He set the brush down. "You do, don't you. You know what's on the other end of this."

The tail moved once, flat against the wood.

"Then you also know he isn't going to hurt me," said Tsuna. "He is going to do something a great deal worse than that. He is going to *consider* it."

By the second day he had something. By the third he had something good, and he knew it was good in the flat certain way that had nothing to do with intuition and everything to do with thirty years of knowing when a document would hold. He wrote it out clean. He read it twice, not three times, and did not remark on this to anyone.

And then he sat in the cold room with the finished page in front of him, and the ink drying, and he did not put the seal on it.

It was the middle of the afternoon. There was a stack to his left of eleven things that came after this one. The eleven things were in order, because they were always in order, and the top one was a matter in Mie that would take the rest of the day. The bottom one was a five year project he had opened when he was four.

The fox sat on the corner of the desk and waited, and the light came in low and yellow across the paper the way it does in the last week of December.

"I want to go outside," Tsuna said.

His own voice surprised him. He had not decided to say it. It had come up out of him the way something comes up when a door has been stuck for years and somebody leans on it at the wrong angle by accident.

The fox's ears went around.

"Not for anything in particular," he said. "Not to see anyone, and there is nothing out there that I need." He heard himself getting faster and could not stop it. "It's not on the list. It doesn't *do* anything. I just want to go down to the river and look at it, and the water's going to look exactly like it looked last week, and I want to go and look at it anyway."

He stopped. The house made its afternoon noises. Somebody was cutting something in the kitchen. Out past the wall a bicycle went by with a bad chain.

"Huh," said Tsuna.

He had said a want out loud. He could not immediately remember the last time he did that. Then he could: another life, about nine years old, and it was about a video game.

After that there was a gap of roughly fifty five years.

He put the seal on the letter. He set it on the corner for Ayame. He stood up, and his legs had that light wrong feeling you get standing up too fast, except he had not stood up fast.

Grandmother was in the front room with the accounts and her glasses down her nose.

"I'm going to the river," he said.

She did not look up. "Take the grey coat. The good one, not the one you like."

"I like the good one. The good one is the one with the pocket."

"You like the one with the pocket." She turned a page. "Be back for the bath."

He got as far as the passage before she spoke again.

"Tsunayoshi."

He stopped. Here it was. Some part of him that had been braced since the word left his mouth in the workroom went ahead and finished bracing, and he turned around with his face already arranged.

"The river's high," said Grandmother, over the top of her glasses. "Stay off the stones by the weir. Your mother went in there twice and she was *twelve*."

"…Yes, Grandmother."

"Good." She went back to the accounts. Then, without looking up, and in exactly the same tone she used for the accounts: "About time."

"About time for *what*, exactly? What is that supposed to mean?"

"Mm," said Grandmother, who had evidently learned it off Nagi, or else Nagi had learned it off her, and he was never going to find out which.

And that was it.

That was the entire event. He stood in the doorway in his socks with his heart going like something small and stupid, and the sum total of the world's response to Sawada Tsunayoshi wanting a thing for no reason was a note about a coat.

*What did you expect?* he thought, going down the passage. *What exactly did you think was going to happen?*

He did not have a clean answer. Somewhere under the floor of him there had been a shape. It went something like this. *If I ask for a thing that isn't useful, there will be a cost, and I will not see the bill until later.* He had never examined it. It had come down with him. In the first life it had been true often enough that he had stopped testing it, and then he had grown up and taken over a famiglia and simply built his entire personality on top of it and called that being responsible.

The river was the colour rivers are in late December, and the far bank had frost on it in the shadows, and there were two boys further up throwing things at the water with no system.

He sat on the wall.

The cold came straight up through his coat, and his feet did not reach anything, which was the permanent condition of this life and which he mostly stopped resenting. Down at the bend, a heron stood in the shallows doing what herons do, which is nothing, for hours, magnificently.

"It is just a river," he said. "It has been a river the entire time I have known about it."

The weight in his coat pocket, in the pocket of the good grey coat which did in fact have a pocket, shifted and settled.

"I don't even know if I like rivers."

He said it lightly, and then he heard it, and the lightness went out of it as it hung there in the cold.

Because he did not know. That was not a joke. He knew that Vongola Decimo had liked rivers, had liked them specifically in winter, had stood on a bridge in Sicily at four in the morning more times than his Guardians had ever been told about. He had that the way he had the man's handwriting and the man's posture and the man's way of counting four seconds before answering a question.

But that was a thing he was *carrying*. It had come down in the luggage with the sixty years and the five hundred and sixty seven items and the habit of three passes on everything.

What did *Sawada Tsunayoshi* like? Not the dead man. The one with the seven year old hands, and the grandmother who ran an argument about coats. Who was that one? What were his opinions? Had anybody ever asked him? And if they had, would he have had a single thing to say?

He sat on a cold wall above a river he might or might not like and felt the ground go out from under a thing he had never thought to check was standing on anything.

"That is a genuinely terrifying thing to notice," he said, conversationally, to the pocket. The pocket said nothing.

"You would know better than anyone, if you were talking." Nothing.

"That's not a rhetorical question, I'm asking, do you know? Do you know what you like? Or do you just know what he liked?"

And the thing in his pocket put its head out over the edge of the wool.

It had spent a year making rulings about stationery. It had spent a year refusing to sit on the correct envelopes, and declining, with total consistency, to behave like anything that had ever been a lion.

It looked at the river.

For a while. In no hurry. With the specific absorbed attention of a creature that had never seen this before and was finding out, right now, in real time, whether it was interested.

Tsuna watched it look.

"Right," he said, eventually. His voice had gone strange. "Yeah. That's the answer, isn't it."

Upstream, the two boys got hold of something enormous. They carried it between them to the edge, counted wrong, and threw.

"*Yes!*" one of them screamed.

The splash went up white in the cold light, enormous, a proper one! Both of them ran from it, shrieking, and the sound came down the water and broke on the wall, and the fox startled so hard it went straight back down into the pocket like a coin.

Tsuna laughed until he nearly went off the wall into the frost.

## 3.6

It happened at the end of the day, which was where everything in this house happened, in the hour when the lamps were on and nobody was working and the building made the noises that old buildings make when they cool.

He was on the floor of his room with his back against the wall. The fox was on his knee. Outside, the black tree stood in the last of the light with its year of frost on it, holding what it held.

"I've been thinking about your name," he said.

The ears came around. Everything came around. In a year he had never once seen it give him its whole attention like that, and the completeness of it was so immediate and so undefended that he almost stopped there.

"Not that one," he said quickly. "Not the old one." The attention did not go away. If anything it got worse.

"I've been calling you *you* for a year. I know. I know, I'm *sorry*, I know exactly how long it's been. Three hundred and seventy one days. I count everything, it's a whole thing, we've discussed it." He rubbed the back of his neck. "I couldn't. Every time I went for it, the only thing in the drawer was the one that belongs to a dead Sky, and it's a good name, and you're not him, and I wasn't going to make you wear it."

The fox stayed absolutely still.

"And I finally worked out why it kept happening," Tsuna said, "and it's got nothing to do with you at all, which I think is going to be embarrassing for both of us."

He looked at the ceiling.

"It's because I've been doing the same thing to me. I've been going around in a name that belongs to a dead man. And he's a fine man! He did all right in the end. But I've got all his paperwork in my head and all his habits in my hands, and I've been running around this town at seven years old *being him*. Because he's the only person I know how to be." He let out a breath. "So of course I couldn't find you a new one. I didn't think you were allowed new ones. I'd never checked whether I was."

The lamp did its small noise. Out in the corridor the floor ticked as the house let go of the day's heat.

"So," he said. "*Tomoshibi*." The fox did not move.

"A lamp," he said. "A little one. The kind somebody puts out at the road so that whoever's walking can see where the road is. Not a big light. It's not going to light anything up, that's the whole point of it, it's not for seeing by, it's for knowing the way." He was talking too fast again. "And it's a fox thing. It is. That's *kitsunebi*, that's Fire and not Flame and don't let anyone in this family tell you the two are the same argument. The lights on the mountain at the wedding, the ones people see and know something's passing, that's yours. That's the family. And it's a good omen, actually, in three prefectures, so you can't argue with the paperwork."

A pause.

"And I'm not going to say the other reason," he said. "It's too obvious. You can find it yourself, you read everything anyway."

He did not say it.

He was seven years old, and he had been a great many things. One of them had been a man who could tell exactly when a room full of people had understood him. That man had known what to do next, too, and it was always the same thing. Shut up.

*Tomo.*

The last line of an old story. Somebody came home, and seven days later a piece of bamboo turned up on the step, and two days after that, in a garden in Namimori, a boy met an old friend.

"Tomoshibi," he said again.

And the thing on his knee sat with it.

He had thought, in the three days he had been carrying this around, that there would be a *moment*. He had built several. In some of them it climbed up his front and put its head under his chin. In one of them, which he was not proud of, there was a light.

What actually happened was that it sat there for a while, and then it got up, and it walked off his knee and across the mats and sat down by the door with its back to him.

Tsuna let it go.

He sat against the wall and looked at the small shape by the door and understood, with no difficulty at all, what he was looking at, because he had done the same thing about eleven times this week.

You do not get told who you are and then feel better. That is not the shape of it. Somebody hands you a name and you have to go and sit with it somewhere else, and try it against the inside of yourself, and find all the places it does not fit, and there are always places. It is not a solution. It is a place to stand while you work out the rest.

*It's going to sit there for a week,* he thought. *It's going to sit by that door for a week and think about it, and I'm going to have to not ask.*

He discovered that he was entirely willing to not ask. It turned out to be one of the easiest things anyone had ever required of him.

"You can take as long as you want with it," he said. The ear moved.

"I mean it. I've got thirty years of practice at wearing one that isn't mine, I'm not going to time you."

The house cooled. Down the corridor somebody put something heavy down and said something to somebody else, and Nagi laughed, which she did about once a week and which he could pick out of any amount of noise.

After a while, the small shape by the door got up.

It did not come back to his knee. It went to the window, and up onto the sill, and it sat there in the cold draught looking out at the black tree where sixty years of somebody else's Sky hung in the branches like frost.

Then it turned around and looked at him instead, and Tsuna looked back.

"Anything yet?" he said. "Any progress at all on the large question?"

Nothing. A slow ear, in the cold draught, above a garden going hard with frost.

"Yeah," he said. "Me neither. We can be no use to each other about it together."

Neither of them knew. That was the entire content of it, and it went both ways, and it went perfectly cleanly in both directions, which was not a thing he had ever had with another living creature in either life.

Two of them, in a cold room, in the last week of the year. One Sky and one small piece of a Sky. One that used to be a Sky's lion and was not, and one that used to be a man with an empire and was not. Both of them a year into finding out, and neither of them any closer to done, and the tree outside holding the rest of that Flame patiently, giving it back two paces at a time, for as long as it took.

"Tomoshibi," he said. "Come here. It's freezing."

It came.
