# Local Town Takes In A Stuntman At Midnight. Local Octopus Is Not Covered By Any Custom.

The spring after the road would be remembered in Namimori as a quiet one, which says a great deal about what Namimori had come to consider quiet.

The scar on Tsuna's left forearm closed in February and went from red to pink by April. Eleven stitches' worth of it. He would catch himself rubbing it when he read, and stop, and then catch himself again a page later. *Ridiculous. I had worse from paper cuts in the Vongola archive.* He had not, and he knew it, and he kept rubbing it anyway.

Hibari Seiran was never late again. Not to dinner, not to the shrine, not to the small unscheduled moments when she would appear in a doorway and look at him for the length of a breath and then go and do something else. Nobody in the Sawada house asked about the nine days she had spent in Kyoto. She had said *Kyoto* and then shut her mouth on the word, and in a house where three separate adults could read a closed mouth fluently, that was the end of it.

Kyouya's patrol would reach thirty-one children by the end of February, and a roster with them. A real one. Written on a sheet of squared paper in a hand so furious it went through to the table. It was pinned to the noticeboard outside the fish shop, where the fishmonger would read it every morning with his reading glasses pushed up onto his forehead and say *well, that's Tuesday sorted* to nobody in particular.

Nobody had asked Kyouya to make a roster. That was rather the point of it.

Sugita Gen'ichi came up the shrine steps more often now, and some of those times he did not come for Tsuna at all. He came to sit on the veranda with Musashi and drink her very bad tea and ask her questions about Fire in the tone of a man taking a history from a patient who might be lying. She answered every one. He wrote nothing down in front of her. The certificate from November was still in his files, uncrossed, saying exactly what it had always said, and neither of them mentioned it.

*A doctor who knows the paper is wrong and keeps it anyway.* Tsuna turned that over a good many times that spring. *In my old life I would have called that a liability.* He was fairly sure he would have been wrong about that too.

And so it went. Summer came in wet and left dry. Nagi learned to put her *Gen'ei* back where she found it, mostly.

It was, all things considered, the quietest six months of either of his lives. He did not trust a single day of it.

* * *

The thing about being immortal, Skull had found, was that it did very little for your feet.

He had been running for four days. Not all of it on foot, which was a mercy, because his feet were extremely small and the country was extremely large. There had been a freight train out of Osaka, and before that a ferry, and before that a long and unpleasant stretch of the East China Sea that he preferred not to think about. But the last eleven kilometres had been road. Coast road, uphill, in a leather suit that had not been designed for Japanese September, and a helmet that had not been designed for anything except keeping people from looking at his face.

It was doing that job beautifully. It was doing nothing else.

*Just over the next rise,* he told himself, for the ninth time. *And if it isn't?* *That's what the line said. A town that nobody walks into uninvited.*

The line had been four words and a place name, in a ledger that was not supposed to be read by the likes of him, in an office in Palermo. He had been in that office for an entirely different reason. *Ceasefire. Sanctuary. East Asian compact. Namimori.* He had read it once, in the half minute before the clerk came back from the toilet. He had been carrying it around behind his eyes ever since, like a stone in a boot.

A Sanctuary. A place where nobody could take you anywhere you did not want to go.

He had not believed in them, honestly. Nobody in the Famiglia did. They were a thing old men mentioned when they were drunk enough to get sentimental about the war. But the line had been in a ledger, and ledgers did not get sentimental, and when he had finally said *no* to the Carcassa and meant it, the ledger had been the only thing he could think of.

The road topped out. And there it was.

It was small. That was his first thought, and it was a disappointed one. He had been imagining walls, or at least a gate. There was a harbour with a lot of little boats in it, and a main street going up a hill, and a great many roof tiles. At the top of the hill a shrine with red gates so bright they looked wet. The whole thing was the size of a large Sicilian village and considerably tidier.

The second thought was that somebody was already looking at him.

Several somebodies. He counted six before he stopped counting. A man on a bicycle, who had stopped. A woman sweeping a step, who had not stopped sweeping but had turned the broom slightly so that she could see him past it. An old man with a newspaper on a bench. And a small boy of eight or nine in a white shirt, at the corner by the fish shop, who looked at Skull once, very thoroughly, from helmet to boots, and then took a pencil out of his top pocket and wrote something down.

"Excuse me," said Skull, in his best Japanese, which was good, because he had worked a season in a stunt show in Chiba when he was nineteen. "Is this Namimori?"

The boy with the pencil looked at him with the flat, entirely unimpressed expression of a customs officer who has seen worse.

"Name," he said.

"What?"

"Name, then house, then how you arrived. Bicycles count."

"I don't have a bicycle!"

"Then you walked." The boy wrote it down. "Name."

Skull opened his mouth to say something withering about the security arrangements of a town that deputised primary school children. At that moment his own Flame did the thing it always did when he was frightened and too tired to hold it, and came up off him in a long, sulky purple ripple, about a hand's width deep, like steam off a horse.

Every single person in sight looked at the Flame. Then every single person in sight looked away, calmly, as if a cloud had gone over the sun.

The woman with the broom went back to sweeping. The old man turned a page. The man with the bicycle got back on it and rode off in the opposite direction, not quickly, not slowly, as if steering round a puddle. The boy with the pencil put the pencil back in his pocket.

"*Getsufuji*," he said, to somebody behind him, in the tone of a person reporting rain. "Small one. Tired."

"Got it," said a second boy, from somewhere Skull could not see.

And that, apparently, was that.

Nobody tried to stop him. Nobody tried to help him either. Skull walked up the main street of Namimori with his Cloud Flame steaming gently off his shoulders. The whole town parted around him like water round a rock, perfectly courteous, perfectly incurious, not one of them quite looking at him and not one of them quite looking away. A woman carrying a tray of dumplings stepped into a doorway to let him pass, waited, stepped out again. Two teenagers on the pavement moved to the kerb without breaking their conversation. A shopkeeper was pulling his shutters down for the evening, saw him coming, and pulled them down more slowly, as if to say *no hurry, I'm not closing because of you*.

It was the strangest thing that had ever happened to him, and Skull had once been fired out of a cannon into a lake in front of a paying audience of four thousand.

*Are they scared of me?* He did not think so. *Then why won't anybody ask who I am?* People who were scared of him did not look like that. He knew what people looked like when they were scared of him; he had been paid, for three years, precisely because they did. *Are they being polite?* Closer. But politeness had a flavour, and this had none.

It was, he realised somewhere around the third intersection, how you treated weather.

You did not argue with it. You did not take it personally. You went the other way round.

At the top of the main street the road forked, and there was a patch of rough grass with a persimmon tree in the middle of it and a low stone wall around three sides. A very old woman was standing at the gap in the wall, as though she had been waiting there for some time, holding a flask.

"In there," she said, and nodded at the grass.

"Why?"

"Because you've been running, and you're a Getsufuji, and somebody's been making you stand still." She said it with perfect certainty and without much interest. "You'll feel better after an hour in a field. Everybody knows that."

"I'm not a *horse*."

"No, dear." She held out the flask. "Barley tea. Go on."

Skull stood at the gap in the wall with his fists clenched, and his Flame steaming, and the persimmons glowing orange in the last of the light, and the absolutely shameful realisation arriving in stages that she was right. That he did want to go and sit under that tree. That he had wanted to for about four days.

He took the flask. He went and sat under the tree.

*This is insane …* he thought, as the Flame settled down off his shoulders and lay quiet for the first time since Palermo. *This entire town is insane.*

He drank the barley tea. It was very good.

Somewhere below him in the harbour, something large and armoured came up out of the black water beside the fish market, laid one enormous tentacle across the concrete, and waited, politely, for someone to tell it what the custom was.

There was no custom. Namimori had one for every kind of Fire a person could be born with, and several for the kinds they could marry into. It did not have one for a four-metre octopus in plate armour.

The fishermen looked at each other. After some time, one of them went and got a bucket.

* * *

Musashi had received a great many strange guests in her kitchen over the years, and she had a rule about them, which was that everybody got tea first and a question second.

The Arcobaleno got tea first. It sat on a cushion at her low table in its leather suit and its enormous helmet, and held the cup in both hands. It did not drink, because it could not drink without taking the helmet off, and it very obviously was not going to take the helmet off.

*Shy,* she thought. Was he frightened? She had seen frightened, and it did not sit like that. This was a person who had put a wall between his face and the world a long time ago and had simply never found a reason to take it down.

She knew what he was, of course. Most Honō of her generation knew what a baby with a coloured pacifier meant, even if half of them pretended it was a story. She had never expected to have one in her kitchen. She had expected it rather less than she had expected the Varia, and she had not expected the Varia at all.

Ayame knelt at the end of the table with a notebook, in her capacity as the person in the Sawada house who wrote things down. Indra had gone to fetch Kyouya back from wherever Kyouya was, which would take as long as Kyouya decided it would take. And in the corner, on the floor, with his back against the rice chest and a picture book open on his knees, sat Masaya.

He was not reading it. Musashi had not expected him to. He was seven years old, and three weeks from eight. He had been sent to bed an hour ago, and he had come back downstairs in his pyjamas with the book as a sort of passport, and sat down in the corner without a word. She had looked at him. He had looked back at her. She had let him stay.

It was a family arrangement, and it was nobody else's business. Who else's would it be?

"So," she said, when the tea had been accepted and not drunk for long enough. "Tell me why the Carcassa want you back."

The helmet came up sharply. "I didn't say it was the Carcassa."

"No," Musashi agreed. "You didn't."

A pause. Then the small shoulders in the leather suit dropped about a centimetre, which she took to mean *all right, fine*.

"They want me to do something," said the Arcobaleno. "I said no."

"What thing?"

"I said no." He put the cup down. "That's the part that matters, isn't it? I said no, and they said I didn't have the option, and I said I'd find one, and I came here. They're about a day behind me. Maybe less. They'll have come the fast way." He paused. "I came the stupid way. Freight trains. There's an octopus."

"There is," said Musashi. "The fishermen are very worried about it."

"He won't hurt anybody!"

"They're not worried it will hurt anybody. They're worried they don't know what to call it." She refilled her own cup. "What's your position with the Carcassa, exactly?"

"Strategist." He said it with a certain defiant pride, and then, because he was apparently honest in the way that people who are very bad at lying are honest, "They call it that. I plan the attacks. Mostly the attacks are me doing something loud and stupid at the front while everybody else does the actual thing."

Musashi sat with that for a moment, and then she took down the book.

It was not a large book. It was bound in blue cloth that had gone grey at the corners, and had been rebound twice in her lifetime. It held every word of the compact that Hibari Kimiko had written four hundred years ago with half the Honō of East Asia standing round her in a field. Musashi did not need to read it. She had known it by heart since she was twelve. She opened it anyway, because some things should be read off the page, in front of the person they were about to happen to.

"There are two ways into this town," she said. "You can visit. Visitors keep what they came with, and they leave when their business is done, and while they're here they're under the town's protection and the town's rules. Nobody can take a visitor out of here against his will. Nobody can touch him." She turned a page. "But a visitor is still somebody else's man. If the Carcassa come to the boundary and ask for their strategist, the town will not hand him over, but it will not pretend he isn't theirs either. It'll just sit there. Both of you, one on each side of the line. For as long as it takes."

The helmet did not move.

"And the other way?"

"The other way is residence." She put her finger on the line. "*A resident of the Sanctuary acts in no capacity but as a resident. Whoever would dwell here sets down every office he holds.*" She looked up. "Every office. Not the ones you dislike. All of them. You stop being their strategist. You stop being anybody's anything. You become a person who lives in Namimori, and that's all you're allowed to be, in here, for as long as you stay."

Ayame's pencil had stopped.

Musashi watched the Arcobaleno take that in. She had watched perhaps a dozen people take it in, over the years, and they always did it the same way. First the relief, because a door had opened. Then the arithmetic.

*Every office.* She could see him turning it over. The Carcassa were not a good Famiglia; Musashi knew that much of them, and she suspected he knew rather more. But a position was a position. It was a name people called you by. It was the reason a room went quiet when you walked into it. A man could want very badly to be free of the people who had given him a name and still not want to find out who he was without it.

"I don't have to decide now," said the Arcobaleno at last. It was not quite a question.

"No … you can visit tonight, and decide tomorrow." She shut the book. "But if they're a day behind you, and they came the fast way, then tomorrow may be after they arrive, and then you'll be deciding at the boundary with them watching. Some people find that easier. Most don't."

He did not answer.

In the corner, Masaya turned a page of his picture book. He had not looked up once. Musashi, who had been reading that child's silences for seven years with the help of a *pseudo-bond* and every scrap of Fire she owned, could not read this one at all, and she found that she did not like it.

The kitchen door slid open, and Indra came in with Kyouya behind her, and Kyouya took one look at the small leather figure at the table and went absolutely still.

It was not the stillness of a boy meeting a stranger. Musashi had seen the Getsufuji of her own town meet perhaps a hundred strangers, and he had never once gone still for any of them. He had gone still like this exactly once before, the first time he had met her grandson.

*Oh,* she thought. *Oh, dear. Another one.*

"*Getsufuji*," said Kyouya, very quietly, to nobody.

The Arcobaleno's Flame came up off his shoulders in a single startled purple ripple, and went down again.

"Kyouya," said Indra, in a voice like a hand on the back of a neck. "He's a guest."

Kyouya looked at his mother. Then he looked at the Arcobaleno. Then, for some reason, he looked at Masaya in the corner, for rather longer.

"The north road," he said. "They'll come by the north road. It's the only one wide enough for cars after the bridge." He turned round. "I'm moving the roster."

And he was gone, and the door slid shut behind him with a small precise click that Musashi had learned, over the past year, meant *thirty-one children are about to be woken up*.

"Is he …" said the Arcobaleno faintly. "Is he always like that?"

"Yes," said Musashi, Indra and Ayame, together.

* * *

The boundary stone on the north road was older than the shrine.

It stood at the place where the road came out of the cutting and the land opened up towards the bridge, a single upright slab of grey granite about the height of a grown man, with the characters on its face worn nearly smooth. Four hundred years of weather. Tsuna had run his fingers over them once, in daylight, and felt the old seal underneath the stone like a pulse under skin, faint and slow and utterly patient. At night he did not need to touch it. The thing that stone marked the edge of was his, and it had been his since he was a year old and had thrown it up over the town like a sheet over a bed, and he could feel the edge of it now from forty metres away, humming.

*Amakakeru Tsubasa.* His own dome. His own contract, sealed shut and hidden under a *Gen'ei* so fine that nobody alive, Honō or otherwise, could have told you there was a *Kotei* under it at all.

It was a very odd thing, to stand on the edge of your own work in your pyjamas and be nobody at all.

He was nobody at all. He had been nobody at all for nearly a year, officially. There was a certificate in a doctor's file, with a stamp on it, that said so. It said so to every Honō registry in the country. It would say so to anybody from the Carcassa who had the wit to ask, and what it said was that there was nothing here worth looking at. A seven year old in a borrowed coat, standing next to his grandmother at a stone, because his grandmother had brought him, because apparently he could not be left alone in a house at night.

Which was, in fairness, exactly what Musashi had told Indra. Tsuna had very much enjoyed watching Indra decide not to argue with it.

The Disciplinary Committee had got there first. Of course it had.

Thirty-one children stood in a long ragged line across the road, about ten metres inside the stone. Most of them wore coats over their pyjamas, and one of them a coat over a kendo uniform, which suggested a very late practice or a very early one. Nobody had a torch. Kyouya did not believe in torches. They stood in the dark in silence, and every so often one of the younger ones yawned, and was elbowed.

Kyouya stood at the stone itself, the one *Getsufuji* Namimori had never once tried to put in a field. He had been there when they arrived, and he had not turned round.

The Arcobaleno stood about a metre from Kyouya. That had not been anybody's plan either. He had walked up the road with Musashi and Tsuna and the rest. He had seen Kyouya at the stone, and stopped. Then he had walked forward, with the very deliberate unconcern of a person who is not going to let a nine year old decide where he stands, and he had planted himself exactly one metre from that nine year old and folded his arms.

Kyouya had let him.

*Two Clouds on one line,* Tsuna thought. *At midnight. With the Carcassa coming. Wonderful. Marvellous. What could possibly go wrong?*

The cold came off the river and got down his collar. On his shoulder, where nobody else in the world could feel it, a very small warm weight shifted, and curled, and put its nose under his ear.

The cars came at twenty to one.

Two of them, black, not new, with Osaka plates, coming over the bridge with their headlights on full and then, at some point on the far side, very sensibly, dipping them. They stopped about thirty metres short of the stone. Three men got out. Tsuna watched them look at the road and see the line of children across it, and he watched them understand, one after another, that the children were not there by accident.

*Good,* he thought. *Now you're thinking.*

They walked up to the stone and stopped a pace short of it. Three men in dark coats, one old, two not, and not one of them with a hand anywhere near a pocket. The old one had grey hair cut very short and a face that had been in a lot of weather. He looked at Kyouya. He looked at the thirty-one. He looked at Musashi, and Tsuna saw him recognise, from nothing but the way she stood, that she was the person he should be speaking to.

He did not look at Tsuna at all. Why would he? There was nothing to see.

"Signora," said the old man, in careful English. "Good evening. We are sorry to come so late." He inclined his head towards the leather figure beside Kyouya. "We have come for our strategist."

"Good evening," said Musashi, in English. "He's a guest of this town."

"Yes." The old man did not seem surprised. "We read the line in the Palermo ledger, as he did. We are not fools, Signora. We know what this stone is." He paused. "We only ask that he come out and speak to us. He is ours. He has been ours for years. There is a matter of a contract, and of a debt, and of a job he agreed to and then refused."

"I didn't agree to it!" said the Arcobaleno. "I agreed to the *first* thing! The first thing was completely different! You changed the …"

"*Skull.*"

The name dropped between them like a stone into a well. Tsuna felt the small leather figure flinch.

*There it is,* he thought. He had known that name, of course. He had known it since the moment the helmet came up the main street with a fringe of sulky purple steaming off it. He had known it in another life, in another country, attached to a braggart with a heart like a wet kitten, who had complained loudly about every single thing anybody ever asked of him and then, more often than not, gone and done it. *Hello, Skull.* He had kept his face perfectly blank for four hours. It had cost him more than he was prepared to admit.

"You are not a prisoner," the old man was saying, gently. "Nobody here wishes you harm. But you are the strategist of the Carcassa Famiglia, and a strategist does not walk away from a job because he dislikes it. You know this. Come out. Speak to the Don yourself. If you have a grievance, he will hear it."

"He won't …" said Skull. "He never does."

"Then that is between you and him. Not between you and a village in Mie." The old man looked at Musashi again. "Signora, forgive me. I know your rules. A guest keeps what he came with. He came here as our man. He is still our man. You cannot make him otherwise, and we cannot make him come out, and so we will all stand here, I think, for as long as it takes." He smiled, not unkindly. "We have brought coffee."

And that, Tsuna thought, was checkmate, or near enough. Because the old man was right. That was exactly what the compact said. A visitor was protected, but a visitor was still somebody else's man. The Carcassa could stand at that stone for a month if they chose, and Namimori could not lift a finger to shift them, and every day Skull stood on the other side of it would be another day of being theirs.

There was one way to cut it. Every adult on this road knew what it was. Musashi had read it out in her kitchen four hours ago.

And nobody could say it for him.

Tsuna could feel it. He could feel the whole shape of the thing, the way he had felt a thousand negotiations in his first life: where the weight was, where the give was, which sentence would tip it. He could see what Skull needed to hear. He had four ways of saying it, a guess about which one might reach a frightened man in a helmet, and no way at all of knowing whether it would.

He could not say any of them here, in front of three men from the Carcassa with the Palermo ledger in their heads, who had looked at him once and seen nothing, and who needed to go on seeing nothing. The *Kotei* of Namimori did not exist tonight. There was only a doctor's certificate, and Musashi's grandson, in a borrowed coat, up past his bedtime.

*So be the grandson,* he told himself. *You were a child once … presumably you asked questions.*

He looked at Skull. At the helmet, specifically. Purple and white and scuffed down one side, with a long old scrape across the visor that had been polished over so many times it had gone soft at the edges. It did not look like anything a Famiglia would issue. It looked like something a person had owned for a very long time.

He tugged at Musashi's sleeve. She looked down.

"Grandmother," said Tsuna, in the high clear voice of a seven year old who has been awake too long and has just thought of something important, "if he stops working for them, does he have to give them back the helmet?"

For a second the whole north road was quiet.

Then one of the younger men from the Carcassa made a small sound that was very nearly a laugh, and turned it into a cough. Musashi looked at Tsuna for about as long as it took to decide not to ask him anything, ever, about this.

"I don't know, Masaya," she said. "You'd have to ask him."

Tsuna looked at Skull, and waited, with every appearance of a child who genuinely wants to know.

The helmet turned. Slowly. Tsuna felt, rather than saw, the eyes behind the visor come down and find him.

"It's mine," said Skull. His voice had gone odd. "The helmet's mine. I had it before. I had it when I was …" He stopped. "I had it before them. It's not theirs. Nothing on me is theirs." A pause, and then, much quieter, as if he were hearing himself say it and did not quite believe it, "Nothing on me is theirs except the job."

*Yes,* Tsuna thought. *That's it. That's the whole thing. Now you know what you'd be keeping.*

He had not said a single word he could not have said at seven. He looked back down at his feet, and yawned, for effect, and let Musashi's hand come down on top of his head.

Skull turned to face the old man across the stone. He was so small that he had to tip the whole helmet back to do it. His arms were still folded. His Flame had gone very quiet on his shoulders, and there was something in the way he stood that Tsuna had seen before, on the faces of men in his first life, in the second before they did the thing they had been dreading for years.

"I quit," said Skull.

The old man's face did not change.

"Say it properly," he said, quite gently. "If you mean it. Say it properly, so there is no mistake."

"I, Skull," said the Arcobaleno, loudly, in a voice that cracked on the second word and carried straight on regardless, "resign as strategist of the Carcassa Famiglia. As of now. All of it. Every title you gave me and every job I said yes to. I don't want any of it. I'm not yours. I'm not your strategist. I'm not your anything!" He stopped for breath. "And I'm keeping the helmet!"

There was a silence on the north road.

Then Musashi stepped up to the stone and laid her hand flat on the top of it.

"Then hear me," she said. Her voice was not loud. It did not have to be. "I am Sawada Musashi, *Ruriko*, priest of this shrine and keeper of this compact. A person has come to the boundary of Namimori and set down every office he held. Namimori receives him. From this hour he is a resident of this town, and nothing else, and he stands under its protection for as long as he chooses to stay."

And the dome took him.

Tsuna felt it happen. It went through him like a struck bell, straight from the stone, down into the ground, out along the long dark roads of Fire under the town, and back again. It was one enormous slow pulse of his own Fire that nobody on that road could have pointed to, not even him. It found the small cold knot of *Getsufuji* standing at the stone and it closed round it, not tightly, just completely, the way a bowl of water closes round a dropped stone. And the knot, which had been clenched for four days and possibly for six years, unclenched.

Skull made a sound. Just a little one.

Across the stone, the three men from the Carcassa went very still.

They had felt nothing. Tsuna was sure of that; the dome was hidden, and it stayed hidden. What they had seen was Skull. Four days of fright had been steaming off that small leather back in a sulky purple fringe, and now, in the space of one sentence from an old woman, it lay down flat and went quiet, like a dog finally let in out of the rain. The small shoulders dropped. The arms came unfolded. He stood at the stone like a person standing in his own doorway.

The younger of the two young ones took a step back without meaning to. The other looked at Skull, and then at the stone, and then at the old woman's hand still resting on top of it, as if the granite might explain itself. The old man did not move at all. But his eyes went up and to the left, towards the shrine on the hill, and then to the right, towards the sea, and then back to Musashi. Tsuna watched him look for whoever had just done that. He watched him find an old woman, thirty-one children, a sulking *Getsufuji* and a boy in a borrowed coat, and come up empty.

*Good,* Tsuna thought. His heart was going very fast. *Look all you like.*

The old man let out a long breath.

"So …" he said. "That is what the ledger meant."

"That's what the ledger meant," said Musashi.

He looked at Skull for some time. Then he did something Tsuna had not expected. He bowed, briefly, the old European bow that begins at the neck and not the waist.

"Goodbye, strategist," he said. "Goodbye, Skull." And to Musashi, "We will go home and tell the Don what we saw. He will not be pleased. But he is not a fool either." He paused, and his eyes went once more, uneasily, to the dark hill behind her. "Nobody who stood here tonight is a fool."

The three of them walked back to the cars without hurrying. The engines started. The headlights came on, dipped, and went back over the bridge, and after a while the sound of them was gone into the noise of the river.

Thirty-one children stood in the road in silence, and then one of the smallest ones said, "Can we go to bed now?" and Kyouya said "*No*," and then, after visible consideration, "Yes," and they went.

Skull stood at the stone for a long time after that, looking at nothing, with his arms still folded. Nobody moved him. Namimori did not move Getsufuji who were standing still; everybody knew that.

Kyouya, who had not moved either, and who was still exactly one metre away, turned his head at last and looked at him.

"You're loud," said Kyouya.

"You're *nine*," said Skull.

And that, Tsuna judged, was probably going to have to count as a peace treaty.

* * *

Ettore Bassi had been driving for the Carcassa for twenty-two years, and in twenty-two years he had learned exactly one thing worth knowing about bad news, which was that you did not deliver it tired.

So he pulled into a service station on the Ise road at a quarter past two, bought three cans of hot coffee from a machine that thanked him politely in a recorded voice, and sat in the car with the engine running and thought.

The boys were quiet in the back. Nico kept looking back over his shoulder. He had done it all the way over the bridge, as a man keeps checking a door he knows he has locked.

"You saw him," said Nico at last. "Skull. When she said it."

"I saw him."

"What happened to him?"

Ettore drank some of his coffee. It was too sweet, and it was very hot, and it did nothing whatsoever for the cold place under his breastbone that had opened when a man he had known for years went quiet in front of him like a switched-off engine.

"The compact took him," he said. "That's what happened."

"Took him how? I didn't feel a thing."

"Neither did I. That's the part I don't like."

Ettore had been asking himself the next question for eleven kilometres, which was *who*, and he had got no further than he had at the stone. He had looked for them. He had looked properly, the way he had been trained to look forty years ago in a courtyard in Catania by a man who could pick out a Flame user in a crowd of five hundred by the way the pigeons moved. He had looked at the shrine on the hill, because that was the obvious place. He had looked at the old woman, because she had spoken the words. He had looked at the dark sea and the dark sky and the thirty-one children standing across the road. And there had been nothing at all, anywhere, and he could not have put his finger on anybody if the Don had held a gun to his head.

"I don't know …" he said.

Nico looked at him in the mirror. Nico was twenty-four, and in Nico's short experience Ettore Bassi always knew.

"It was an old compact," said Ettore. "Four hundred years. Every Famiglia in Palermo has heard of the East Asian compacts, and every one of them thinks they're dead. Paper. You keep the line in the ledger for politeness, like a grave nobody visits." He turned the can round in his hands. "That wasn't paper. That was alive. Somebody is holding it. Now. Tonight. Somebody strong enough to hold a thing that the whole of East Asia signed, and quiet enough that I couldn't find him standing forty metres away."

The car was silent.

"The old woman?" said Paolo, from the other side of the back seat.

"She's a *Mist*, Paolo. A very good one. She's not that." Ettore shook his head. "Whatever that is, it's not standing where anybody can see it. And if the Don asks me who it is, I have nothing to tell him."

He did not like the shape of that sentence. He tried it out in his head a few times anyway, as a man rehearses a limp before he walks into a doctor's office. *Don, the strategist is gone. He resigned at a boundary stone in Mie and the compact took him. There is something holding that compact that I could not find. I would not send men there again, Don. Not without knowing who.*

*Who holds a thing like that, and hides it?* It would do. It would have to.

Paolo laughed, suddenly, in the back seat.

"What?"

"The kid …" said Paolo. "The little one. With the grandmother." He put on a high voice. "*Does he have to give back the helmet?*"

Nico snorted. Even Ettore felt the corner of his mouth go.

"God, the look on Skull's face," said Paolo. "Well. The look on his helmet."

"He'd have quit anyway," said Nico. "He was going to. You could see it."

"Maybe." Ettore shrugged. He thought about the boy for a second. Small, brown haired, in a coat too big for him, half asleep, yawning at the end of it. Somebody's grandson. There had been nothing to him at all; Ettore had looked, out of habit, the way he looked at everyone, and there had been nothing there, not a flicker, the way there was nothing in a lamp that has not been plugged in. *A civilian child,* he had thought, *brought along because they couldn't find a babysitter,* and then he had forgotten about him entirely until Paolo did the voice.

"Kids say things," he said. "Drink your coffee. We've got nine hours."

He put the car in gear.

Behind them, far back over the dark hills, the town of Namimori slept with its lamps out and its harbour dark, looking like nothing at all. Ettore Bassi drove away from it as steadily as he could, and did not look in the mirror once all the way to Osaka.

* * *

The fishermen came up to the shrine at seven in the morning, in a body, in their good jackets.

There were eight of them. They stood in a row in the forecourt in front of Musashi, with their caps in their hands. The eldest of them, who had been mending nets in Namimori harbour for fifty-one years and had seen three typhoons, a whale and a submarine, explained with enormous dignity that there was an octopus.

"There is," Musashi agreed.

"In armour," said the eldest fisherman.

"Yes."

"It won't eat the fish in the market. We tried … it just holds them. It holds them very carefully and it gives them back." He paused. "We need to know what the custom is."

Tsuna, who was sitting on the veranda steps in his school uniform with a rice ball, very nearly choked.

It was a serious question. That was the thing about Namimori that no outsider ever understood. There were customs for the *Ketsueki*, and the *Yasuragi*, and the *Gen'ei* and all the rest, and every one of them had been worked out over four hundred years by people who had lived next door to a Fire and needed to know whether to knock. That was what a custom was here. It was how you lived beside a Fire you could not fully understand without either of you getting hurt. And now there was something new in the harbour, and eight grown men in their good jackets had walked up forty steps at seven in the morning because they were not prepared to guess.

*I once spent three days arguing with the Cavallone about the protocol for a visiting Rain Guardian's hat,* Tsuna thought, chewing. *This is better. This is much better.*

Musashi considered the matter with the gravity it deserved.

"It came with a guest," she said at last. "The guest is a resident now. So it's a resident's animal, and it lives in the harbour because that's where it fits. Treat it as you'd treat a resident's dog that happens to be very large. Don't feed it from your hand. Don't chase it. If it's in the way of the boats, ask it to move, and it will."

"*Ask* it," said one of the younger fishermen, doubtfully.

"Politely," said Musashi. "As you'd ask anyone."

The eldest fisherman thought about it for some time, and then nodded once, slowly, as a man nods when a thing has been settled for the next hundred years, and put his cap back on. They filed back down the steps. Halfway down, Tsuna heard one of them say *a dog, then*, and another one say *a very large dog*, and a third say *does anyone know what an octopus likes*, and then they were out of earshot.

It was just eight men going down some steps at seven in the morning. And a custom that had not existed yesterday, settling into the town as if it had always been there.

Indra came out and sat down on the step beside him with her tea.

She did not say anything for a while. That was normal. Tsuna ate his rice ball. Down on the forecourt a small leather figure in a helmet had come out of the guest room. He was standing very still in the middle of the gravel, looking at the gates, as if he were trying to decide whether he was allowed to walk through them.

"The last time this town took someone in who had a name worth knowing," said Indra, "was in my husband's great-grandfather's day."

Tsuna waited.

"A Mist from a house in Kyushu. Quite a senior one. He set down his office at the north stone, the same as last night." She drank some tea. "Kyoto sent two old men up those steps within the month. They sat in that kitchen for three days and drank terrible tea and asked, very politely, who was holding the compact these days. Because it had been getting weak for a long time, and everybody knew it, and then all of a sudden it had taken a man in." She turned the cup in her hands. "My husband's great-grandmother told them it was being held the way it had always been held. And they went away."

That was all. She did not say what that meant. She did not say anything at all about what anybody in Kyoto might think, or do, or want, now or later. She told him what had happened once, a long time ago, and then she stopped, and looked down at the forecourt, and drank her tea.

*It was being held the way it had always been held.* Tsuna turned that over. *With a borrowed god in a box, and every Hibari in the town pouring themselves into it.* He knew that. He had felt the old seal under the stone. That was what those two old men from Kyoto had been sent to find out about, and that was the answer they had gone away with.

The answer was different now.

He did not say so. Indra did not ask him to. They sat together on the step in the thin autumn sunshine and looked at the small figure on the gravel.

Skull had turned round. He was looking up at the veranda now, at the two of them, and then past them, into the house, where Musashi was clattering about in the kitchen. His arms were not folded. Tsuna realised it was the first time he had seen them unfolded.

"Hey …" said Skull. "Old lady."

Musashi appeared in the doorway with a ladle.

"What," said Skull, "am I supposed to *do* now?"

It came out very loud. It always did, Tsuna suspected. But it was not a demand. Underneath it there was something Tsuna had heard before, in his first life, in a great many rooms, in the voices of men who had just put down the only thing they had ever been for, and found their hands empty.

Musashi looked at him over the ladle for a moment.

"Whatever you like," she said. "You live here now."

Skull stood on the gravel and opened his mouth, and shut it again.

"I don't …" he said at last, much more quietly. "I don't actually know what I like."

"No," said Musashi. "Most people don't, at first. Come and have some breakfast. That's usually a start."

She went back inside. After a moment, Skull followed her. He went up the veranda steps past Tsuna and Indra without looking at either of them, a small leather figure with a scuffed helmet that was his and had always been his. He paused in the doorway, just for a second, and put one hand flat on the frame.

Then he went in.

Down in the harbour, very carefully, the octopus gave the fishermen back another fish.
