# Local Man Makes A Career Decision On An Unlit Road. Local Town Makes One Back.

## 7.1

The man had been in Taki since eleven in the morning, and he had not been looking for Tsuna at all.

That is the part that took longest to be angry about, afterwards. He was not a hunter. He was a mid-grade Vongola functionary with a good coat and a travel allowance, sent out from Genoa in the ordinary way, to do the ordinary thing that the organisation does with a set of documents that look slightly wrong.

A private instrument. Cash. A Genovese lawyer who had declined to explain her client. Fifty-two children moved out of Italy in a single administrative gesture eighteen months ago, and then one more, alone, on a separate file, four months later.

Somebody in an office in Genoa had looked at that and written *irregular* in a margin. And the margin had gone up, and then across, and then out.

So he came to Mie. And on the second day he went to the prefectural placement office in Taki, and asked, politely, with the correct paper, whether he might see the receiving file for a minor placed that morning.

They gave it to him. Of course they did. It was a legitimate request from a man with the right stamp, in a country where the right stamp is the whole of the argument… and the file was open on a desk in front of him at twenty past two in the afternoon, and at the bottom of the second page there was a signature.

A signature a stranger could read and could not copy.

**Sawada Masaya. Age seven.**

*I gave it to them,* Tsuna thought, later, on the stones, when he had got as far as being able to think in sentences again. *I stood in front of the best Sky in Italy with his lid off and I would not say that name. And then I wrote it on a form.*

*Because it was a form.*

The man sat in the placement office for four minutes without moving.

He was not a clever man. He was not a slow one either, and he had spent eleven years in an organisation where the surname **Sawada** means exactly one thing. It means the External Advisor. It means Iemitsu. It means a line that has been kept quietly and carefully out of every succession conversation for three decades… in a decade when there is a succession conversation going on in every room in Italy.

And a child of seven had signed a legal instrument.

*Which means he is not a dependent,* the man thought. *Which means somebody let him.*

He asked one more question on his way out, about buses, and was answered helpfully, because it was Taki and people there answer questions. Then he went and stood at the end of the coast road in the dark with a cigarette he did not smoke, and did the arithmetic that a certain kind of man does about once in his life.

Here is what that arithmetic looked like.

There are eleven men in Italy who could plausibly be Vongola Decimo, and four of them are Nono's own sons, and one of those four had been slowing down badly all autumn. A man who removes a competitor does not get thanked. A man who removes a competitor **and** puts a crack in Iemitsu's position gets a room with a window.

And the competitor was seven years old and on a road by himself.

*He is a child,* thought the man, who had two of his own.

Then he thought about the room with the window, and about a brother-in-law who had been promoted over him twice, and about what a man is allowed to be at thirty-five in an organisation that measures everything and forgives nothing.

*And whose child is he, though?* he thought, working round to it. *Not the Advisor's household. Not raised in it, not fed by it, not spoken for by it. A boy nobody in Italy has claimed in seven years.*

*So who exactly is going to come and ask?*

That was the question that decided it, and it is worth writing down, because it was wrong in a manner the man did not have the information to see. He asked who would come and ask. He got the answer **nobody**, and the answer was arrived at carefully, from a file, by a man who was reasonably good at his job.

It was the most expensive mistake made by any Italian that decade.

Tsuna had come down off the parapet at seven o'clock and walked on without hurrying. At ten past seven, on the long straight above Kawaba, with his **Cloud** up and a canvas bag on his shoulder, he stopped.

Not because he saw anything. There was nothing to see; the road was unlit for three kilometres and the sea was somewhere off to the left making the sound it makes.

He stopped because a Flame he did not know had come up ahead of him at about two hundred metres, and gone out again immediately, and the going-out was the part that mattered. Anybody can be startled into showing. Only a professional puts it away that fast.

***Storm**,* he thought. *Low grade. Trained, but not trained recently. And not a **Sky** anywhere near him, which means he is alone, which means he is somebody's errand.*

*And he did not put it up at me. He put it up to see, and then he was annoyed with himself.*

The fox came out of the bag and went flat along his collarbone and did not make a sound.

Tsuna stood in the road and, for about four seconds, considered the entire thing from the outside.

*I am seven. I have walked twenty kilometres. My Sky is in a tree in a garden thirty-eight kilometres from here and I cannot reach it and would not if I could. What I have is a **Cloud** I have never once used on a person.*

*I could go back. There is a farm track two hundred metres behind me and a wall I can get over and he does not know I have seen him.*

*And then he is still here tomorrow, and the day after, and he is a hundred metres from a town with fifty-one children in it who nobody has counted properly except me.*

*No.*

He put his hand into the bag and moved two things from the bottom to the top, which took a second and a half, and he did it without looking.

Then he walked down the hill toward the man, at the speed of a tired child, because there was one thing he had that the man did not, and it was not Flame and it was not reach.

It was that he had done this before, and the man was going to do it for the first time.

## 7.2

There is a way of writing this kind of thing that makes it exciting, and it is a lie, and Tsuna would have said so.

So here are the two ends of it.

**The first end.** At eleven minutes past seven, on the coast road above Kawaba, a man of about thirty-five stepped out of a field gate behind a child and said, in accented English, that he was very sorry.

**The second end.** At nineteen minutes past seven, the same man went over the wall on the seaward side of the road at a speed that had nothing voluntary in it. He hit the slope and kept going, down through the scrub, toward the water, making a great deal of noise the whole way.

He did not come back up. He was not dead either… and both of those facts were going to matter a very great deal to several people who were not on that road.

Eight minutes.

What happened in between is not worth describing, because almost none of it was a fight. A fight is two people who both think they can win. This was four seconds of that. The remaining seven minutes and fifty-six seconds were one person correcting another person's assumptions.

Three things are worth keeping.

**One.** The man went for the throat, immediately, without any of the preliminaries that a man uses on an adult, because you do not need preliminaries for a child. And that was his entire error, and it was the only one he was allowed. A seven year old body is a seven year old body. It cannot take a grip and it cannot absorb anything. So Tsuna did not let him have one, and had not intended to from the moment he came down the hill, and the arrangement of the two things he had moved to the top of the bag was about that and nothing else.

**Two.** He used the Cloud once. Not on the man. On the two metres between them.

A **Cloud** is not a weapon and had never been one. What it is, is a quality of not wanting anybody within about two arms' length. Every person alive reads it. Nearly every person alive obeys it without ever knowing they have. Tsuna had spent four years using it to be left alone on a bus.

He put all of the **Cloud** out, flat, at a range of one metre, into a man who was already committed.

The man's whole body did what a body does when it walks into a doorway it thought was open. He did not fall. He *checked*, for about a third of a second. His weight went wrong, and he lost the line of his own attack… and a third of a second is a currency Tsuna had spent sixty years learning to count.

**Three.** It cost him. It cost him at the point where the man's knife came round and found the outside of his left forearm and went along it, not across, and took a long shallow channel out of him from below the elbow almost to the wrist.

He did not notice for another four seconds. That is normal, and he knew it was normal, and he had told other people it was normal in a life he was supposed to be finished with.

At seventeen minutes past seven the man understood, fully and finally, that he was not going to be able to do this, and that the thing in the road in front of him was not what the file said it was.

And at that point he did the only intelligent thing he had done all evening, which was to stop.

"What are you," he said, in English, and it came out of him without any of the control he had walked onto the road with.

Tsuna, who was bleeding into his own sleeve and had taken the bag off his shoulder about a minute ago without remembering doing it, thought about the answer for slightly too long.

*Say nothing. He goes back to Italy with nothing and reports nothing, because a man does not report that he was beaten by an infant.*

*Say something, and he has a story, and stories get believed eventually.*

"I'm seven," he said.

"You are not."

"I'm seven, and you came out of a gate behind a child, and you are going to have to live in the same head as that for the rest of your life." He kept his voice level, and it took nearly everything. "Go home."

And the man looked at him for a second and a half.

Then he went over the wall.

It was not courage that took him over it, and it was not cowardice either. It was the arithmetic arriving in the other direction, and the number coming out with *get off this road* written at the bottom. He went down the slope through the scrub, badly, making a great deal of noise, toward the water and the dark and the long way round to a town with a station in it.

Tsuna stood in the road and listened to him go, and did not follow, and could not have.

Then his legs stopped agreeing with him. He sat down on the stones at the edge of the road, in the dark, thirty-eight kilometres from home… with his left arm open along the outside, and his own blood going warm and then immediately cold inside the sleeve.

He checked his pulse before he checked the arm.

That was the order he had been taught, in a room in Italy, by a man who had been dead for thirty years, and his hands did it without consulting him at all: two fingers, throat, count to fifteen, double it. *Ninety-two. Fine. That is a scared seven year old, not a bleeding one.*

Then the arm.

*Long, shallow, outside, along. Not across. Nothing spurting, so nothing arterial. Nothing white in it, so I still have the muscle.* He got his belt off one-handed, which took two tries. *It is going to need a great deal of cleaning and about eleven stitches and somebody who is not me.*

*And there is nobody within a very long walk of me in any direction.*

He sat on the stones in the cold and let that be true for a moment, because pretending it was not true would cost more.

Above him the sky had gone completely black and there was no moon at all, and the only light anywhere was a farm on the far side of the valley, which was one window, and very far away.

## 7.3

The sensible thing was the farm window.

He worked it out sitting on the stones with his belt round his arm above the cut. One window, across the valley, maybe two kilometres by road and rather less if he went down and up, which he was not going to do in the dark with one arm. Forty minutes. A farmhouse, a telephone, an adult, and a story he would have to invent while bleeding.

He got up and started toward it and got about ninety metres.

Then he stopped, and stood in the middle of an unlit prefectural road, and had the argument he had been avoiding for four years.

*Nobody is going to find me,* he thought. *That is not bad luck. That is the thing I built.*

Going out by **Cloud** is not hiding. He had said that to himself a hundred times and had been rather pleased with it, and it had always been true, and it had never once been the whole truth.

Because what a Cloud does is make a space that people politely decline to enter. It works on a bus. It works in a market in Genoa. It had worked on a woman with artichokes, and then a man, and then a dog. It had worked every day of his life since he was three years old… and it was working **right now**, on an empty road, on nobody at all.

*And if a car comes,* he thought, *it will go past me. It will go past me and the driver will not know why, and he will get home and not think about it again.*

*Because I taught the entire world to leave me alone… and the entire world turned out to be extremely good at it.*

*How many cars have gone past me in four years? How many people have stepped round me in a market and not known why? And I was pleased about it, every single time. I counted it as a skill.*

So he had to put it down.

He stood there for some time with that.

It is difficult to explain how hard that was, and he would not have tried. The Cloud had come up when he was three and had never gone down. Not at the shrine. Not asleep. Not in a market in Liguria with an Arcobaleno pricing him from six metres. It was the one thing that had been entirely his in either life, and it asked nothing, and it had never once let him down.

*Put it down.*

*Why? What happens if I put it down and a car still does not come? What happens if I put it down and something else is out here?*

*Then I am a seven year old on a road with a bad arm and no wall. Which is what I am. Which is what I have been for eight minutes and have not admitted.*

*And there is one more thing under all of it, and I have been walking round it for four years.*

*If I put it down and somebody comes, then somebody came. And then I am a person who was found on a road, being looked for, and that is a fact about me that I have never once allowed to exist.*

*Fifty-one children. Five hundred and sixty seven plans. A **Sky** of somewhere, with a contract four hundred years old behind him… and not one single line in any of it where the thing being fetched is me.*

He stood in the dark with his belt round his own arm and understood, rather late, that the wall had never been about being safe.

He put it down.

It went out slowly, in layers, with the reluctance of a thing that has never once been asked to, and the road did not change in any way whatsoever, because there was nobody on it.

And then Tsuna stood in the open dark with nothing round him at all, for the first time since he was three years old, and found that the thing he felt was not fear.

It was **loud**.

Every one of them was there, and they had been there the whole time, and the wall had been sitting on top of them like a hand over a radio.

Grandmother, thirty-eight kilometres away and asleep, a steady heavy old **Mist** that had been in the same place for fifty years, like a hand left flat on a table. Ayame, nearer the front of the house and awake at this hour and cross about something, a **Storm** going round and round in a small bright circle. Nagi, a turning thing so light it barely registered until you took a wall off it, and then it was everywhere, and it was going to be enormous.

And Indra was a flat cold **Rain** surface that had never once in four years moved. He had always privately filed it under *asleep*. He understood now, on an unlit road with his arm open, that it had never been asleep for a single second of its life.

*How long have they been audible?* he thought. *How long have I had that and not listened to it?*

*Four years. It has been four years.*

And on the northern edge of all of it, something that was not where it should be.

It was moving. It had been moving for some time. It was coming down the coast road at a speed that made no sense at all for the thing making it, and it was close, and it was *furious*.

*Oh,* thought Tsuna.

*Oh no. He is **nine**!*

## 7.4

Hibari Kyouya arrived at nine minutes past ten, on a bicycle, having ridden thirty-eight kilometres in the dark without a light.

Nobody has ever satisfactorily explained the bicycle. He was nine years old and the machine was not his, and the coast road out of Namimori climbs for the first six kilometres, and he did the whole of it in under three hours in January. His mother worked out the route afterwards and then stopped working it out.

He came round the last bend at a speed that had no brakes in it, saw Tsuna in the road, and did not slow down until he had got past him by four metres, which meant the stop was not a decision.

Then he got off the bicycle and let it fall over.

He did not run. He walked, and he walked the four metres back at a completely even pace, and that was somehow the most alarming thing that had happened all evening.

He was nine years old. He was soaked to the shoulders from a river of cold air, and there was a great deal of gravel in one knee, and he had done thirty-eight kilometres of unlit coast road on a bicycle built for somebody taller. He had not spoken to anybody since Monday. He had turned down three meals. He had been sitting on a wall above the north road for four days, getting quietly larger. And at eleven minutes past seven, something in his chest had moved thirty-eight kilometres to the south, and gone hard, and then gone quiet.

He stopped a metre and a half away.

"Where," said Hibari Kyouya.

Not *are you all right*. Not *what happened*. Tsuna, holding his own arm, heard the whole of the next three years arrive in one word and understood, with total clarity, that he had done this.

"Kyouya."

"**Where.**"

"Over the wall, down the scrub, south, about three hours ago." He heard himself say it. He heard how fast he said it. "He is not armed any more, he is on foot, and he is going to try for the station at…"

He stopped.

He stopped because he had answered. He had answered immediately, completely, with a bearing and a timescale and a threat assessment, in the voice he had used for sixty years to brief people who were about to go and do something on his behalf.

*I did not decide to do that.*

"Kyouya. Wait."

"No."

Tsuna pointed out that he was nine years old, which was true, and which was the weakest sentence he had produced in either lifetime, and he heard it fall over before it had finished leaving him.

"And you are *seven*," said Kyouya, "and you are standing in a road, in the dark, thirty-eight kilometres outside my territory, holding your arm, and you have been doing that for three hours, because nobody could find you." His voice did not rise at all. That was the thing. "Do you know why nobody could find you?"

Tsuna said that he did. Kyouya waited. It was the flat patience of a boy who has ridden thirty-eight kilometres in the dark and has decided the rest of this conversation will be held on his terms.

So Tsuna said it out loud. He had put a wall up when he was three years old. He had never once taken it down. And it had worked perfectly, every day, for four years… including tonight.

"You took it down tonight."

"Yes."

"Why tonight."

And Tsuna, who had been managing this conversation extremely well for eleven seconds, found that he did not have anything to put in the gap.

"Because I could not do it by myself," he said.

Kyouya stood in the road and looked at him.

He was a **Cloud** of nine years old with gravel in his knee and a bicycle lying in a ditch, and he had ridden thirty-eight kilometres on the strength of a feeling in his chest, and what was on his face was not sympathy.

It was a decision being made about the next several years, very quickly, by somebody who was going to be extremely difficult to argue with about it later.

"Sit down," he said. "You are grey."

"I am fine."

"*Sit down!*"

Tsuna sat down.

Kyouya crouched, picked up the ruined sleeve between two fingers, looked at what was underneath it for about four seconds, and put it back down with a care that was entirely at odds with everything else he had done since arriving.

He said that it was stitches. Tsuna agreed that it was about eleven of them, and mentioned, because it seemed relevant and because he had begun to be slightly unreliable about what was relevant, that he had walked twenty kilometres before any of this had started.

"Then we are not walking." Kyouya stood up. "There is a house with a light on across the valley. You are going to stay exactly where you are, and I am going to go and be extremely rude to a farmer, and he is going to drive us to Namimori because it will be easier than the alternative."

"Kyouya…"

"You do not get to be difficult about this," said Hibari Kyouya, "because you are the one who took the wall down, and taking it down is a *request*, and I came. So it is done. That part is over." He picked up his bicycle. "Be angry about the rest afterwards, if you like. I am going to be."

He got about six metres before he stopped.

"The man," he said, without turning round. "You let him go."

"He ran."

"You **let him go**, and then you sat down on the stones, and you did not follow." He was quiet for a second. "I want to know whether that was because you could not, or because you decided not to."

Tsuna looked at the back of a nine year old's head.

*Say could not. It is true. It is even mostly true.*

"Both," he said.

"Hn," said Kyouya.

He did not go. He stood there with the bicycle in one hand and his back turned. Tsuna had been waiting four days to have this conversation and had not found one usable sentence in the whole of that time… and he discovered now that it was going to happen anyway, on gravel, at ten o'clock at night, whether he had a sentence or not.

"I owe you one," he said.

"You owe me several."

"Monday." Tsuna made himself say it properly. "You stood on a wall for two hours and you did not move, and Belphegor threw three knives at you, and Ayame took the second one, and you still did not move. I have been trying since Tuesday to work out what to say about that, and everything I get is an insult."

"Say the insulting one."

So he said thank you, to the back of a nine year old's head, on an unlit road, and it came out sounding exactly as thin as he had known for four days it was going to sound.

Kyouya turned round and agreed that it was insulting, and Tsuna said that he knew, and neither of them moved.

"Do you know why?"

*Because thank you is what you say to somebody who did you a favour,* thought Tsuna, *and a favour is a thing that could have gone the other way, and you would like me to understand that it could not.*

"Because it was not for me," he said.

"It was **entirely** for you." Kyouya's voice went up for the first time all night, and it was not loud, it was worse than loud. "It was for you. I did it standing still, which is the only way it works. And I have been sitting on a wall for four days because I cannot decide whether that makes me useful or makes me furniture!"

The road was extremely quiet.

"It makes you a **Cloud**," said Tsuna.

"That is not an answer!"

"No. It is a definition. I am giving it to you because I do not think anybody has, and somebody should have by now." He was cold. The arm had gone from hurting to something duller and further off, and he kept talking anyway, because this mattered more. "A **Storm** goes and does a thing. A **Rain** takes the heat out of it. A Cloud holds a position, and you cannot tell from outside whether a held position is strength or furniture, and that is not a flaw in you, Kyouya. That is the actual shape of the job."

"I do not like it."

"No. You are nine years old and you rode thirty-eight kilometres in the dark, so I did not think you would."

Kyouya thought about this.

"I am going to change it," he said.

"Change what?"

"All of it," said Hibari Kyouya. "This does not happen again. Not to you, and not in my town, and not thirty-eight kilometres outside it either, and I am not going to be a wall that somebody walks past."

And then he did go and be rude to a farmer.

## 7.5

Sugita Gen'ichi put eleven stitches into a seven year old's left forearm at twenty past midnight, and it was the fourth most interesting thing that happened to him that night.

The farmer had driven them. He had been extremely rude to, and had enjoyed it enormously. He refused money twice, and then accepted a box of something from Ayame at the door, in a transaction that nobody explained to him and that he did not ask about.

The surgery light was the good one. The arm was clean by then, and the channel of it was long and shallow and going to scar badly rather than deeply. The boy lay on the table and held still, with the total unnerving stillness of somebody who has been sewn up before.

That was the first interesting thing.

The second was that at no point in two hours did anybody in the Sawada house ask **how he was**.

They asked other things instead. They asked a great many other things, at speed, in a very organised way, while Gen'ichi worked… and every one of the questions was about the **man**.

Ayame came in with her own left arm still in its sling and a notebook balanced against her hip, announced to the room that this house had now had **two** in one week, and that she had been first! Then she took a statement. An actual statement: time, place, bearing, description, sequence. She read it back twice. She asked whether the man had spoken English or Italian, and then whether the English had been *good*, and wrote the answer down.

Hibari Seiran arrived at some point without anybody hearing her come in, stood at the end of the table for ninety seconds, asked exactly one question, and left.

The question was: *"Which way was he going when he went over the wall."*

And then there was the boy on the wall outside, who would not come in, and who had ridden thirty-eight kilometres in the dark and had to be physically prevented from getting back on the bicycle at half past one in the morning.

Gen'ichi had lived in this town for eleven months. He had signed a piece of paper in November that said this child was entirely ordinary. He had watched this household deal with a knifed housekeeper the previous Monday with the calm of a fire brigade.

And at ten to one in the morning, tying off the last stitch on a seven year old who had not made a sound, he finally understood that he was the only person in the room who did not know what was going on.

"Sawada-sama," he said, to the doorway, without looking up from the arm. "May I ask a question?"

She had been standing there for some unknown length of time, and he had a suspicion he did not examine that she had been standing there for most of it. She told him he might ask a great many, and that he would be finishing the arm first, and there was nothing in her voice that made it a refusal.

So he finished the arm, and tied off, and cleaned up, and took rather longer over all three than the work required.

Then she sent everybody out, which took some doing, and sat down on the other side of the surgery table and put her hands flat on it.

"Bring the book," she said.

Gen'ichi did not move for about four seconds.

"I have kept that book for thirty-one years," he said, "and I have never once shown it to anybody, including my wife."

"I know. My father's doctor kept one too. When he died, his daughter burned it in the yard with the rest of his papers, and I have thought about that particular fire once a month for forty years." Musashi did not look away from him at any point. "Bring the book, Sugita-sensei. I am not going to take it away from you and I am not going to ask to read it. I am going to fill it in."

He brought the book.

She turned to the page with the eight words on it. She did not ask him where the page was; she went to it, which told him she had known about the list for longer than he had been writing it.

"*Honō*," she read. "*Tenmei. Kohaku. Gen'ei. Ruriko. Ketsueki. Chiyu. Eienzakuramura.*" She put her finger under the second one. "You have written these down for a year and nobody has told you what they are, and I am the person who decided that, and it was my decision and not an oversight. Do you understand the difference?"

"…Yes."

"Good. Then I will tell you why I am changing it, and then I will tell you the words."

She turned her hand over.

"On Monday a woman of this house took a knife through the arm in order to keep a clause in a contract. On Thursday a child of this house was cut open on a road thirty-eight kilometres away by a man who had come from Italy to do it, and nobody could find him for three hours. Twice in one week. My plan was to wait until he was older." Her mouth did something without being a smile. "My plan has been overtaken."

He asked about the certificate he had signed in November, and he asked it plainly, without any of the padding a man puts round a question he expects to be refused.

"No," said Musashi. "It is not true."

Gen'ichi sat very still, and discovered that the thing he felt was not surprise, and had not been surprise for some weeks.

"I knew it was not true when I asked you to examine him," she said. "I knew you would find nothing, because there is nothing to find with the instruments you own, and I let you put your name to it, and I did that deliberately, and it has protected him for three months." She folded her hands. "You may be angry about that. I would be. But you will have to be angry after I have told you the words, because you are going to need them by the weekend."

*She used me,* thought Gen'ichi.

And then, immediately after, in the flat voice his father had used for hard things: *yes. And she has just told you, at one in the morning, in a room with no witnesses, when she did not have to tell you at all.*

"Start with the second word," he said, and opened the book, and took out his pen.

She started with the second word, and she did not give him a lecture. She gave him one definition properly, in the manner of somebody giving directions to a man who will have to drive the road himself.

"A **Flame** is not a thing a person has," she said. "That is how they say it in Italy, and Italy has been saying it wrong for four hundred years, and the mistake is so old over there that it has stopped being a mistake and started being a philosophy. Here it is a thing a person **is**. The word is **Honō**. Every one of the eight on your page is a way of saying what kind."

"What kind are there?"

"Seven." She counted them against the table with one finger, unhurried, in the manner of a woman who was taught them at four years old and has never had cause to check. "**Ketsueki**, which is the one that goes and does. **Yasuragi**, which takes the heat out of a room, and of which this town has a version that does the opposite. **Chiyu**, which mends. **Hogo**, which holds. **Getsufuji**, which stays where it is put, and which is a great deal harder than it looks from outside. **Gen'ei**, which is what I am, and which is the one your instruments will never find."

Gen'ichi's pen had stopped moving. "Sawada-sama, that is six."

"Yes," said Musashi. "It is."

She let that sit for as long as it needed to sit, which was about four seconds, and she did not help him with it.

"**Kotei**," she said. "The one the other six come home to. The one the contract is written round, in the box in the storeroom under the north hall, which came out on Monday morning for the first time in my life and went back in on Monday night. It is the rarest of the seven by a very long way. The last one this town had was born in the sixteen hundreds, and there is a child asleep in your back room with eleven stitches in his arm."

"…Sawada-sama."

"**Tenmei** is what you call him when you are being formal." She had not raised her voice at any point. "**Kohaku** is what Ishii-san calls him, because she is fond of him, and because it is what his grandmother's grandmother would have been called. Kotei is what the contract calls him, and the contract has never in four hundred years cared what anybody was fond of."

"And the certificate I signed says he has nothing."

"The certificate says your instruments found nothing, which is true, and which is the most useful true sentence anybody has written about that child in seven years." Musashi turned her cup a quarter turn and left it there. "Now ask me the one you have actually been holding since eleven o'clock."

Gen'ichi looked at the page. Then he looked at the door of the room where the boy was asleep.

"Why can he do what he did tonight? He is seven. A grown man came out of a gate behind him with a knife, and the man went over a wall, and the boy checked his own pulse first. Which of your seven words is that?"

"Ah," said Sawada Musashi. "No. That one I do not know either, and I have had seven years and the advantage of being in the room."

She got up, with some difficulty, and rested her weight on the table with both hands.

"Write it down, sensei," she said. "That is what the book is for."

He wrote for another forty minutes.

At the end of it he looked at the page, which now had, in his own handwriting, under the eight words he had carried for a year, a ninth line that was not a word at all. It was a measurement. It was the two figures from the drifting episodes in December, the ones he had written *I do not know what they are, watch* beside… and beside them now, in the same hand, the beginning of an explanation.

He did not cross out the November certificate.

He thought about it. His pen was actually on the page. And then he did not, because the certificate was a true record of what he had measured with the instruments he had, and a book for things you do not understand is ruined the moment you start removing the things you were wrong about.

So he wrote the new line underneath it instead, and dated it, and put the pen down.

"Sawada-sama."

"Mm."

"The boy has been examined by me four times, and he has never once flinched, and tonight he checked his own pulse before he looked at his own arm." Gen'ichi closed the book. "That is not in any of your eight words, is it."

Musashi looked at him for a while.

"No," she said. "It is not."

"Then I will keep watching that one."

"Yes," said Sawada Musashi. "I rather thought you might."

## 7.6

Here is what the elements of a **Sky** do, and nobody tells them to.

There is nothing about it in the contract. Musashi checked, at some point that spring, because she is thorough and because she wanted to know whether she was obliged to stop it. The instrument in the box covers attack, refuge, jurisdiction and answer, and it has four hundred years of marginal notes in six hands… and not one line of it says anything whatsoever about what a **Ketsueki** or a **Getsufuji** or an inverted **Yasuragi** does afterwards, on their own time, about a man who put a hand on the one they came home to.

It does not say, because it does not need to. You do not write down the part that has never once had to be encouraged.

**Hibari Kyouya** did not sleep. He sat on the wall outside the surgery until four in the morning with gravel still in his knee. Then he went home, changed, and was standing at the school gate at seven twenty with a list he had written at some point between those two facts.

Nobody has ever seen that list, and its existence is not in doubt. By Tuesday there were eleven boys from the year above him standing at the water tower crossing, in a line, doing nothing at all in a manner that took up a great deal of room. By the end of February there were thirty-one of them and they had worked out a rota, and somebody's mother had started bringing rice balls at four o'clock, which nobody had asked for and nobody refused.

He was nine years old. He did not explain the arrangement to a single person, and he did not have to. A town that has spent four hundred years reading a **Getsufuji** the way other towns read weather does not require an explanation, and would not have known what to do with one. Within two years the thing had a name, and a room, and a set of armbands that no adult had approved and no adult was going to bring up.

Ask him at nineteen why it started and he will look at you as though you have asked why a wall is upright. Ask his mother and she will change the subject. Ask Ayame, who keeps the ledger for the rice balls, and she will tell you the exact date, and she will tell you it with her sleeve pushed up.

**Hibari Seiran** was away for nine days, beginning on the Saturday, and she told the house she was going to Kyoto.

She did go to Kyoto. That part was checkable, and somebody did check, quietly, some years later for entirely unrelated reasons, and she had certainly been there and had certainly stayed where she said she had stayed. She came back on a Thursday afternoon, ate an amount of food that Ayame described afterwards as *structural*, and slept for fourteen hours without moving.

Nobody in that family ever asked her anything about it, then or afterwards, and the not-asking was not delicacy. It was the same arrangement the whole town runs on: you are told what you are given, and reaching for a question early only gets you the wrong one.

The only thing anybody noticed was small and took a while to add up. Hibari Indra had spent eleven years being the woman who arrives forty minutes after you would like her to, and had built an entire reputation on it, and from that spring onward she was **never late again** for anything involving anybody in that house. Not once. Not by four minutes.

Her Flame is **Rain**, and it is inverted. A Rain settles a thing down. Hers does the opposite, and always has, and everybody who has ever met her has treated that as a charming eccentricity, and every single one of them has been wrong about how charming it is.

**Kawahira** did nothing at all, publicly, for a very long time.

In a house in Palermo that winter, however, certain small things began to be **two centimetres to the right** of where they had been left.

A comb. A chair, at breakfast, for eleven consecutive days. The third book from the end of a shelf its owner had not touched in a year. Nothing valuable ever went missing and nothing was ever damaged. Not one item of it could be reported to anybody without the reporter sounding unwell… and it went on for four months. The man it was happening to was one of the Ninth's sons. By April he was not sleeping, by June he had dismissed two members of his own household over it, and by the autumn he was not a candidate for anything at all.

Nobody proved a single thing, and nobody ever will, because there is nothing to prove. A **Mist** of sufficient quality does not need to do very much. It needs to do a very small thing, eleven hundred times, to somebody who cannot say it out loud.

And it was not one house. That is the part that nobody assembled, because the three pieces of it never sat on the same desk.

The son got four months of two centimetres, because the man on the road had been his, and a man's people are a man's business.

The External Advisor got something quieter and, in its way, considerably worse. Beginning that February, every clerical error in his office started to be correct. Old ones. Errors from years back, in his own hand, tidied by nobody at all.

So a man walking into his own archive in Palermo would find a decade of his working life in better order than he had left it… and would understand, slowly, over about a fortnight, that the only thing still wrong in the External Advisor's office was the External Advisor.

And the Ninth, who is a **Sky** himself, and who had let the whole of it happen by not knowing what his own people were doing, received exactly one item, once. It was on his desk on a Tuesday morning in March. It was not threatening and it was not valuable, and he was entirely certain he had not put it there. Nothing of the kind ever happened to him again for the rest of his life.

Nobody who noticed any of it ever used the word cruel, because nobody who noticed any of it was in a position to.

What they noticed was a **Mist**. An enormous one, held down extremely hard, and unmistakably **angry**.

An angry Mist is a thing every professional in that business has an inherited opinion about, and the opinion has two parts.

The first part is to make a quiet private note to be somewhere else for a while, and to be somewhere else without appearing to have decided anything.

The second part is to keep a very interested eye on the unlucky bastard who caused it, and to enjoy oneself thoroughly while doing so. No entertainment in that business lasts better than watching a man find out.

And on a Sunday in February, an unremarkable man in a knitted hat sat on the engawa of the Sawada house with a cup of tea he had been given without asking, and complained at some length about the price of ink.

Tsuna knew. Not the details, and not for a while, but he knew the shape of it within a week, and he sat with it in the same room where he had learned the other thing.

*They are doing this because somebody touched me,* he thought. *Not because of what I did. Not because of what I am for. Because somebody put a hand on me on a road.*

*Can I stop it? No. Would they listen if I asked? They would listen extremely politely. Would it change anything at all?*

*No. Not one centimetre of it.*

He had spent an entire winter discovering that the people around him did enormous, competent, terrifying things without needing him to be awake for any of it.

*Is it the same thing?* he made himself ask, because he had promised himself on a parapet above Kawaba that he would stop doing the dishonest version. *Is the **Mist** who held seven house bonds for nine hours the same woman who defined seven words to a doctor at one in the morning? Is the **Cloud** who stood still on a wall for two hours the same boy who rode thirty-eight kilometres in the dark without a light? Yes. And the **Rain** who went to Kyoto for nine days and came back and ate like a soldier, is she the same one who has stood four metres behind her son every single day of his life? Yes. She is exactly the same one, and that is the entire point, and I have been refusing it since January.*

In January it had saved a town, and he had sat on a cold step and felt useless about it, and had written the feeling down in his head under a heading he was rather proud of.

In February it went the other way, and it was the same thing, and it was not useless at all, and he found he could not have one without the other.

He had asked, on a step in the dark, *what was I for, then.*

It was the wrong question. It took a knife on a road, eleven stitches, and a nine year old with gravel in his knee to work out why… and the answer, when it came, was embarrassingly small.

*What was I for.* Four words, and every one of them assumes the same thing: that he is the one doing the having. That a **Sky** is a man with people, in the way that a man has a coat and a set of plans and five hundred and sixty seven contingencies filed by date.

*But what if it runs the other way? What if I am not the one who has them? What if they have me, and have had me since I was one year old and could not consent to a single part of it… and the entire question of what I am **for** is a question that only I have ever thought was open?*

Nobody in Namimori has ever agreed to that. Not once in four hundred years.

---

The arm healed badly and the scar is still there, long and pale along the outside of the left forearm, and he is asked about it perhaps twice a year for the rest of his life.

He always gives the same answer, which is that he fell off a wall when he was seven years old. It is a bad lie and he knows it is a bad lie. He has never bothered to improve it, and nobody who matters has ever asked twice.

And in a small town in Mie, on the second Tuesday of every month, a very old woman still gets on a bus.
