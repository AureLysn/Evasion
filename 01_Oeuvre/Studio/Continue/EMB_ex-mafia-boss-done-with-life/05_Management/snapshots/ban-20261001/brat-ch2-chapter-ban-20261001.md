# Local Ex-Mafia Boss Is Assigned The Colour Green. Local Cloud Assigns Himself Something Larger.

## 2.0

Four seconds.

Tsuna had counted a great many things in two lifetimes, and he was good at it, and he knew exactly how long four seconds was. It was the time it took a competent man to clear a doorway. It was two breaths.

It was also the gap he had once put between a question and an answer on purpose, in a room full of people who were counting it with him. He had wanted them to know he was choosing.

On Wednesday morning, at a school gate, a boy in round glasses had asked him if he wanted to play, and Sawada Tsunayoshi had stood there for four seconds and had not been choosing anything at all.

He had been looking at a dead man's face.

It was Saturday now. The river was up from the week's rain and running brown, and the park by it was mostly mud, and he had been standing at the edge of the mud for some minutes without going in.

He was six years old. He had a satchel he did not need and boots he had been told twice to wear. Somewhere behind him, at a polite distance that was not polite at all, a member of the Hibari household was standing under a tree and pretending to have business with the tree.

Somewhere off behind the tree line his *Kotei* sat in four hundred years of sakaki, doing the only job it had, and the *Amakakeru Tsubasa* it fed went on lying over the whole town like a second sky nobody could see. He had stopped being able to feel the edge of it about a year ago. His grandmother said that was normal. His grandmother said a great many things were normal that were not normal anywhere else in Japan.

In his head he still called it *Flame*, which was the Vongola word, which was a word for a thing a man **has**. Here it was *Fire*, and here a man **was** it, and he had been six years old for six years now, and the two words had still not finished arguing.

*Irie Shouichi.*

The name had arrived before the face had, which was backwards, and which was how he knew it was real. Manufactured memories came in the right order. The true ones came sideways and out of sequence and with the wrong details attached, and this one had arrived with a smell: hot electronics, and the particular sourness of a room where somebody has been living on convenience-store rice balls for nine days.

Ten years. That was the number, and the number was the whole of it.

A boy had met him at fourteen and had understood something about the shape of the world that nobody else understood. Then he had gone away and carried it by himself for ten years. He had built a machine out of it. He had let everyone believe he was the enemy, because the plan worked better that way and the plan was what mattered. He had done the arithmetic and found himself to be an acceptable cost.

And at the end, when they were all sitting in the wreckage of the thing they had barely survived, Tsuna had asked him why he had not said anything.

Shouichi had pushed his glasses up his nose with one finger. He had looked genuinely confused. He had said:

*"Because you were busy."*

Tsuna had not had an answer for that. He had gone on not having one for about forty years.

He had run the numbers himself, afterwards, as a man does when a thing will not let go of him. There had been fourteen separate points at which Shouichi could have asked him for help. He had counted them. He had a list, in a drawer, in a life that no longer existed, in a world that no longer existed.

The boy had never once asked.

"You're in the mud," said a voice.

Tsuna came back. It was not the dizzy way, thank every kami there was. The dizzy way had stopped in December and he intended it to stay stopped.

It was the ordinary way, which was worse in its own fashion. The ordinary way meant he had simply been standing in a park thinking about a dead man while a boy his own age walked right up to him.

*He is not dead,* Tsuna corrected. Carefully. As he had been correcting himself for six years…

The boy on the river bank was not dead and had never been born, and in this world he was nothing at all except a small boy with a stomach ache and a mother who checked his medicine at the school gate.

"I'm not in the mud," said Tsuna. "I'm…considering the mud."

"You're in it up to the top of your boot."

He looked down. He was in it up to the top of his boot.

"Sugita-sensei told me to wear the boots," he said.

"Sugita-sensei is why I have to take powder before lunch," said Shouichi, with the flat loathing of a person who has recently taken powder before lunch. "Come on. They're doing teams."

There were six of them, plus Tsuna, which made seven, which made teams awkward. The tallest one solved this by declaring himself the referee and then playing anyway.

Nine Lives Rangers turned out to be a game in which everybody had a colour and a number of lives. Each colour also had abilities, which the tallest boy described at the start and which were then quietly renegotiated every time anybody used one. There were no pieces and no board. There was a stretch of river bank, a concrete pipe, and a fallen willow that was agreed to be a fortress on the grounds that it looked like one.

Green, it emerged, was the one who could not be hit while standing still.

"That's not rubbish," said Tsuna.

"It's rubbish because you have to *stand still*," said the tall boy, in the tone of a man explaining water to a fish. "Nobody wants to stand still. Also Green dies in episode forty."

"Everyone dies in episode forty!" said a girl with a stick. "That's the whole point of episode forty!"

"Green dies *first*."

Tsuna had been shot at approximately as often as he had eaten breakfast. He had once spent six hours in an air duct for the sole purpose of not moving.

He looked at the fallen willow, and at the concrete pipe, and at the sight lines between them, and concluded that whoever wrote this programme knew more about tactics than anyone was giving them credit for.

He stood still behind the willow for ninety seconds and eliminated four people.

There was some argument about this.

"You *cheated*! That is cheating!" said the tall boy, delighted and outraged in roughly equal parts.

"I stood still."

"You stood still *on purpose*!"

"There's a different way to do it?"

The girl with the stick laughed so hard she sat down in the mud, which cost her a life, which she disputed.

It went on for an hour.

Tsuna was not good at it, which surprised him until he worked out why. The game was not about winning. He kept accidentally winning, and each time he did, the game stopped being fun for about a minute while everybody re-argued the rules.

So he started standing still in stupid places. This worked much better. The tall boy eliminated him twice and was insufferable about it, and Tsuna discovered that being insufferable at is, in its own way, quite restful.

At some point he noticed he had not thought about the list in the drawer for forty minutes.

Then Shouichi sat down on the pipe and did not get up.

Tsuna saw it from thirty metres away and read it instantly and completely, without any say in the matter. The hand going flat against the shirt. The shoulders coming in. The careful small breathing of somebody who has decided a thing is manageable and is about to be wrong about that.

He was already moving before he decided to. That was becoming a habit this week and he did not care for it.

And then Shouichi lifted his head and called out, across the whole river bank, in front of everybody:

"Sawada-kun! Can you come here? I need to sit down for a bit and I don't want them to think I've quit."

Tsuna stopped.

It was, on any reasonable accounting, nothing at all. It was a child with a sore stomach asking another child to come over.

Six other people heard it and not one of them looked up. The tall boy shouted *"BOO!"* without turning round. The girl with the stick shouted *"he's not quitting, he's dying, that's different!"* And the game went on exactly as it was.

Tsuna stood in the mud with his heart doing something undignified.

*He asked.*

Out loud. In front of witnesses. Within four seconds of needing to. For a thing that did not matter at all.

The other one had waited ten years. He had sat in a room with a machine and a plan and a stomach that must have been doing exactly this. He had made a list of reasons not to ask, and had found every one of them sufficient. *Because you were busy.*

This one had not even considered it. This one had needed to sit down, and had not wanted to look like a quitter, and had therefore shouted about it across a river bank.

They were not the same person. Tsuna had known that for four days as a man knows a fact. He knew it differently now, standing in the mud, with the difference shouted at him at a volume the entire park could hear.

He went and sat down on the pipe.

"Is it bad?"

"It's…medium." Shouichi had his glasses off and was cleaning them on his shirt, which was filthy, so this was making them worse. "It goes away if I sit."

"How long?"

"Ten minutes? I don't know. Mum times it, I…I don't." He put the glasses back on, smeared. "Don't tell her about this one."

"Why not?"

"Because then it's on the list, and the list means…the doctor." He said *the list* with real feeling. "And then it's Tokyo and they put a tube down you."

"They wouldn't put a tube down you."

"Ichirou-kun's cousin had a tube down him!"

"Ichirou-kun's cousin," said Tsuna, "is nine, and lies."

Shouichi considered this with the seriousness it deserved and evidently found it persuasive, because he relaxed by about a centimetre.

On the bank, the tall boy had discovered that if you stood absolutely still in the concrete pipe the acoustics made you sound enormous, and was now conducting a war from inside it in the voice of a god. The girl with the stick was hitting the pipe.

"You should tell her," said Tsuna.

"You sound like an adult."

He had, in fairness, been one.

"I sound like someone who has seen what happens when people don't…say things," said Tsuna, which was true, and which was as close to the whole of it as he was ever going to get. "Tell her it happened. Don't tell her it was medium. Let her decide if it goes on the list."

"That's basically telling her."

"Yes."

"…*Fine.*" Shouichi slumped. Then, sideways, without any particular weight: "Are you coming next Saturday?"

And there it was: the second one. Four days and two invitations, from a boy who had no idea he was doing anything at all.

Tsuna looked at the river, which was brown and fast and going somewhere, and thought about a drawer in a house that had never been built in this world, with a list in it of fourteen times somebody had not asked him for help.

"Yes," he said. And then, because he had spent six years being very careful and had apparently used up the whole supply on Wednesday: "Shouichi."

He heard it land. Not in Shouichi, who had already started saying something about how the tall boy cheated with the pipe and how there should be a rule. In himself. It was a small noise…like a latch.

Nobody in his previous life had called that boy by his given name until he was nineteen years old and it was very nearly too late.

"…What?" said Shouichi.

"Nothing."

"You said my name funny."

"I said it correctly. You've been hearing it wrong for six years."

"*Sawada-kun.*"

"Tsuna," said Tsuna.

Shouichi blinked at him through the smeared glasses. Then he shrugged, in the manner of somebody being handed a fact of no particular interest, and said, "Okay. Tsuna. There should be a rule about the pipe."

"There should," Tsuna agreed.

That was all. That was the entire event. A boy with a sore stomach had accepted a name and gone straight back to a grievance about acoustics, and somewhere behind them a member of the Hibari household went on having business with a tree.

It was the first thing Tsuna had chosen in this life that nobody had needed him to choose.

## 2.1

The first edge came at twenty past four that afternoon, and it came the way all of them came, which was correctly and without any decoration at all.

Tsuna was on the veranda with his boots off and his feet in the sun and a bowl of something Ayame had produced without being asked, and he was not thinking about anything, which was itself so unusual that he had noticed it twice.

Then the light at the inner gate changed, and Hibari Tetsuya's older cousin was standing there, and had probably been standing there for a while.

"Tenmei-sama."

Tsuna put the bowl down.

"It has started," she said. "Four men, at the junction by the water tower, forty minutes ago. They are moving toward the shopping street. They have not gone toward the shop."

"Yet."

"Yet."

"How many of ours?"

"Nine."

"That is a great many for four men."

"Indra-sama said nine," she said, in the voice of somebody reporting weather, and Tsuna thought, not for the first time, that he had spent decades building an organisation that could not have produced that sentence.

"Is she there?"

"No. She is here."

Of course she was. Of course she had known before he did, and had put nine people at a junction, and had then come and sat on the far side of this house so that when it began she would be in the same building as him. He had not asked her to do that. He had not asked her to do any of it, and she had gone on doing it anyway, and the word for that was a word he had been declining to use for several months.

"Thank you," he said. "Tell me when the second thing happens, and not one minute before it."

The woman bowed and went, and Tsuna picked the bowl back up because his hands wanted something, and did not eat anything in it.

Indra came out at a quarter to five and sat down on the step below him without asking, which was how she did everything.

"You are not eating that."

"No."

"Would you like me to take it away, or would you like it to sit there?"

"Sit there," said Tsuna.

She let it sit there. Out past the second gate the light was going long and yellow across the stones. The *Honō* board at the first torii would be catching it. Eleven people were probably standing in front of it on their way home from work, without knowing why they did that.

"Nine," he said, after a while. "Why nine?"

"Because four is the number they brought, and the number you send is not the number they brought." She said it the way other people said the days of the week. "Do you want the reasoning or the answer?"

"Both…in that order."

"Four men who intend to kill a shopkeeper are not soldiers. They are workmen. Workmen stop when the work becomes expensive." She turned a hand over. "Two would have made it a fight. Four would have made it a fair fight, which is worse. Nine makes it arithmetic, and workmen can do arithmetic."

"And the answer?"

"Nine is what I had who could stand in a street for an hour without looking like they were standing in a street."

Tsuna laughed, once, before he could stop it. "That is the answer, isn't it."

"It usually is."

He looked at the bowl he was not eating.

"Seiran-san."

That got her attention. He almost never used it. In the house she was Indra-san, and the ritual name was for the shrine and for formal rooms and for the one time she had knelt in front of him in front of witnesses and used it herself.

"Yes."

"When did you know?"

"That it was today?" A pause, precisely as long as honesty required. "At ten past two. The girl in the street sent word that a man had asked her the way to the Yamamoto place, and she gave him wrong directions, and then she ran."

"Ten past two." He worked it… "You put nine people at a junction in an hour and fifty minutes."

"An hour and ten. The other forty minutes were spent getting Kusakabe's uncle to stop arguing about the water tower." A flicker, almost nothing. "He was right about the water tower. That is what made it slow."

"Why didn't you tell me at ten past two?"

"Because you would have gone," said Hibari Indra.

Tsuna opened his mouth, and found, upon inspection, that this was true.

"I would have gone."

"Yes."

"And that would have been…very stupid."

"Yes."

"How long have you known that about me?"

"Tenmei-sama," said Indra, "I have known that about you since the first week."

Here was the part he had never been able to explain to anybody, in either life.

The waiting was not the hard part. He had been very good at the waiting, professionally, for years. You set the thing in motion and then you sat with your hands still while other people did it, and the only skill involved was not interfering, and he had that skill the way other men had a good arm.

The hard part was that he had known this was coming for **six years**.

He had known it at one month old, lying in a crib with a mind full of a dead world, going through his inventory.

Somewhere out there a woman was alive who had not been alive at this point in the other version. And a set of men existed who would, eventually, walk into a sushi restaurant in a small town and change a boy's entire life in about ninety seconds. Tsuna had written that down. Item, of five hundred and sixty-seven. He had put watchers on it and he had checked the watchers twice a year, and in between he had eaten dinner and learned his grandmother's family history and got mud on his boots.

And he had never once, in six years, gone to that shop and said: *someone is coming.*

He had reasons. He had extremely good reasons, and they were correct, and he could have written them out in order for anyone who asked. You do not warn a civilian about a threat you cannot prove. Doing it makes the threat real to them years early and takes their sleep for nothing. You do not tell a family they are being watched by people they have never met. You do not explain how you know.

And underneath all of the correct reasons was the one he had never written down, which was this: he did not know how to say it. There was no sentence. He had run the language for it perhaps a hundred times. Every single version ended with a woman looking at a six-year-old and asking *how do you know*. After that there were only two roads, and both of them went somewhere he could not follow her.

So he had watched. Which was the thing that worked.

A very long time ago, a man with a fedora and no patience had told him that he was too soft, and had said it as a diagnosis, and had been right. Tsuna had spent a great deal of the intervening time fixing it. He had become the sort of person who could hold a thing like this for six years without letting it show on his face. He had done it well. It was not a skill he had ever wanted to be good at.

The sun moved off the veranda. He put his feet back in his socks.

The second edge came at six minutes past six.

"It is finished," said the same woman, from the same place at the gate. "Nobody in the household was harmed. Nobody went into the shop."

Tsuna let out a breath he had not been keeping track of.

"Ours?"

"Two broken bones. Both ours, both minor. Kusakabe-san's uncle is very pleased with himself and will tell you about it if you let him."

"I will let him. Later." He made himself say the rest of it evenly. "And them?"

"Alive. All four."

"Where?"

"With the Hibari," she said, which was an answer, and which was as much of an answer as he was going to get, and which he had long ago decided not to push on, because Namimori was a sanctuary and sanctuaries are kept by people who do the necessary thing quietly at the edges. He was fairly sure he did not want the detail. He was entirely sure that wanting or not wanting it did not change his share of it.

"Thank you."

Kusakabe's uncle told him about it two days later, at considerable length, in the garden, with a rake.

"Nine of us!" he said. "Nine, Tenmei-sama, and I will tell you frankly that eight was plenty and the ninth was me!"

"You were at the water tower."

"I was at the water tower because I *said* the water tower, and nobody listened, and then where did they come from…the water tower!" He struck the ground with the rake. "Seiran-sama listened. Seiran-sama always listens, she simply listens forty minutes after you would like her to."

"What did they have?"

"Two knives, one bad pistol, and a *Ketsueki* who did not know what he was." The rake came up, entirely forgotten as a tool. "Not a *Kogyoku*, mind. No training, no name, nothing. Just a man with a temper and a bit of Storm in him he had never been taught to hold. He set fire to a fence!"

"His own fence?"

"Somebody's fence!"

Tsuna kept his face in order with an effort he would be proud of later.

"And then?"

"And then Tetsuya's father put him in the drainage ditch," said the old man, with enormous satisfaction, "and sat on him, and explained the *Honō* laws to him for eleven minutes while we waited. Eleven minutes! The man was crying by the end. Not from the sitting."

"From the eleven minutes."

"From the eleven minutes." The rake went back to the ground. "Tenmei-sama, we have not had a proper one in two years. My knees are sixty-one years old and I have not slept so well since the spring."

Tsuna went and looked at the persimmon trees for a while after that, and found that his hands were steady, and was not entirely sure what to do with the fact that a man had nearly died in a street and the nine people who prevented it had gone home pleased.

That was Namimori. That had always been Namimori, in this life and in the other one, and the difference was that in the other one it had belonged to a boy with a tonfa and in this one it apparently belonged to him.

The woman hesitated, which was new.

"Tenmei-sama. The lady at the shop has asked to come here."

Tsuna, who had planned for a very large number of things, had not planned for that.

"When?"

"She is at the outer gate now."

"Now?"

"She walked up the hill," said the woman, "while I was walking up the hill. She is faster than me and I am not going to discuss it further."

## 2.2

The thing about Yamamoto Sachiko, which he had not known, because in the other world she had been a fact rather than a person, was that she was not tall.

She came up the shrine road in her work clothes with her sleeves still tied back, and she had not stopped to change them, and Tsuna understood a great deal about the last two hours from that detail alone. She had a boy with her: six years old, and already promising to be taller than her, standing very straight because he had been told to and not because he understood why.

Musashi met her at the second gate, because of course she did.

"Yamamoto-san."

"Ruriko-sama." The bow was proper and very quick. "I'm sorry to come without sending. I have a question and I don't know the correct way to ask it, so I'm going to ask it badly."

"Then come and sit down and ask it badly," said his grandmother, "and I will answer it as well as I am able."

They sat in the room with the south window. Ayame brought tea that nobody drank. Takeshi was given a plate of something and taken to look at the garden by a woman he had never met, and went, because he was six and there was a garden.

And Sachiko looked across the low table, past Musashi, at the six-year-old sitting at her left.

She looked at him for a long time.

"They stood in front of my door," she said. "Four of them. There were nine people between them and my door and I did not know a single one of their names, and one of them was a girl of maybe nineteen who had clearly been standing in my street for some weeks, because I have *seen* her, Ruriko-sama. I gave her directions to the station in March."

Nobody said a word.

"So my question is not *who were they*." Her hands were flat on her knees. "My question is: how long?"

Musashi's mouth opened.

"Six years," said Tsuna.

His grandmother turned her head and looked at him. He did not look back, because if he looked back he would stop, and if he stopped he would start constructing something, and he had been constructing things at this woman for six years without ever once saying a word to her.

"Six years," Sachiko repeated.

"Since before your son could walk."

"And you knew."

Here it was. He had run the language a hundred times and none of the versions had been any good, and none of them had ever included the woman herself sitting three feet away with flour still on her forearm.

"I knew there was a danger to you," he said. "I did not know when. I did not know their names or their faces. I knew enough to put people on the street and not enough to tell you anything you could have used." He made himself finish it. "And I did not come and tell you, and that was a decision, and I made it, and I have gone on making it every year since."

"Masaya," said Musashi, very quietly.

"She asked how long, ba. She gets the number."

Sachiko had gone quite still.

"You are six," she said.

"I am."

"And you decided."

"Yes."

"By yourself."

"Yes."

He waited for it. He had a fair idea of what it would be, because he had been on the receiving end of this particular conversation from the other chair perhaps a dozen times, and it always came out the same way and he had never once blamed anybody for it. *You should have told me. It was my life. It was mine to know.* He had an answer ready that was honest and insufficient, and he intended to give it without defending himself, because she was entitled to the whole of it and because he had not earned the defence.

What she said was:

"Does my husband know?"

Tsuna's careful arrangement came apart.

"…No."

"Does anyone in my family know."

"No."

"Then don't tell them," said Yamamoto Sachiko.

The room was extremely quiet.

"Yamamoto-san," said Musashi.

"I'm not finished, Ruriko-sama, and if I stop I won't start again, so…please." She took a breath. "I have thought about it the whole way up this hill and I would like to say it in one go."

She turned back to Tsuna.

"My husband is a good man. He was a different thing before he was a good man. If he finds out that four men came to our door he will go and look for the fifth. He will find one. There is always…one. And then my son will grow up in that house instead of the one he is currently growing up in." She said it flatly, as a woman reading out a delivery order. "So he doesn't get told. That is my decision and I am making it in front of you so that you can't make it for me later."

Tsuna found that he had nothing at all to say.

"Second," said Sachiko. "We are not leaving."

"Nobody asked you to leave."

"I know. I'm telling you anyway, because I spent the walk deciding it and I want it said out loud in front of a witness." Her chin came up a little. "My mother-in-law's family have run that shop since before the war. I have been in Namimori eleven years and I did not choose it, I married into it. And this afternoon nine people I have never met stood in the street for me. I'm not going anywhere. I'd like that on the record with whoever it is that keeps the records here."

"That would be my grandmother," said Tsuna.

"Then it's on the record," said Musashi.

"Third." And here, for the first time, her voice did something. "Don't you *ever* do that to me again."

"…Which part?"

"Any of it. Pick." She was not crying. She was doing the thing that is harder than crying. She was continuing to speak clearly while everything under the sentence came apart. "You are six years old. You have been carrying my family for six years and you did not tell me. And I understand why. I understand *exactly* why, I have been going through it the entire walk, and I cannot find the place where you should have done differently. That's what I can't stand. I have been trying to be angry with you since four o'clock and there is nowhere…to put it."

"You can put it on me," said Tsuna. "I don't mind."

"*I* mind," said Sachiko. "I'm a grown woman and you are…in the first year of school."

And that, he thought later, was the moment of the whole afternoon. It was not the assassins, and it was not the nine people standing in her street.

A woman had looked at him, and had been unable to be angry with him, and had been *furious about that* on his behalf. At the age he was. And it had cost her something to say so.

"Fourth," she said, steadier. "The girl. The one I gave directions to. Does she have a name?"

"She does."

"I want it. I want to know what to call her when I take food out to her, because I am going to take food out to her, and I am not going to stand in my own street calling someone *excuse me* for another six years."

Musashi made a sound that in a less formidable woman would have been a laugh.

"I'll find out," said Tsuna.

"Good." Sachiko picked up her tea at last, looked at it, and set it down without drinking it. "That's all of it. Thank you for answering the number. Most people would have given me a speech."

"I considered it."

"I could tell," she said. "You had the face."

"One more thing," said Tsuna. "May I ask you something?"

"You may ask me anything, apparently," said Sachiko, "you have been running my life for six years."

"That's fair."

"It wasn't, actually. I'm sorry. Ask."

"If I had come to you," he said. "Six years ago. A child of your son's age, at your door, saying that at some point men would come. What would you have done?"

Sachiko thought about it. She thought about it properly, for long enough that Musashi poured more tea nobody wanted.

"I'd have told my husband," she said at last. "Within the hour. I wouldn't even have decided to, it would just have come out of me at dinner." She looked up. "And then we'd be having a different conversation today, wouldn't we, and there'd be a fifth man somewhere and my son would know what his father's hands are for."

"Yes."

"Was that the reason?"

"It was one of four," said Tsuna. "It wasn't the biggest one."

"What was the biggest one?"

"That I couldn't think of a way to say it," he said, "that ended with you still able to sleep."

Sachiko Yamamoto looked at him for a while.

"You're not going to have an easy life…are you," she said.

Outside in the garden, Takeshi Yamamoto, who would grow up in a house where nothing had happened, had found the persimmon trees and was attempting to climb one, and being prevented, and negotiating.

Sachiko watched him through the window for a moment.

"He's going to want to do something loud with his life," she said. "Baseball, probably. His father's already talking about it." A pause. "I'd like him to get to."

"He will," said Tsuna.

He had no business saying that. He said it anyway, and meant it, and did not for one second believe that meaning it made it true.

After she had gone, he sat where he was for a while.

His grandmother did not say anything for a long time, which was her way of saying something.

"You gave her the number," she said at last.

"She asked for it."

"You have not given me the number."

Tsuna looked at the tea he had not drunk either.

"I know," he said.

## 2.3

The Cloud came on a Tuesday, nine days later, with a dead pheasant.

Tsuna had been expecting something. Not this, in particular, but something. The gifts had been coming since the autumn at a rate of roughly one a month, and then in the last fortnight they had stopped.

In the language his cousin was conducting this entire conversation in, the stopping was a considerably louder statement than the gifts.

He had looked it up. This was the humiliating part, and he intended to take it to his grave. He had gone into his grandmother's library and worked through two shelves of folklore and one extremely dry volume on territorial fauna. In secret. At night. Like a man cramming for an exam.

Somewhere around the third lacquered box he had realised that he did not actually know the rules of what was being done to him.

What he had learned was that there were no rules. There was a shape. The shape said: *escalation, then silence, then presentation in person.*

So he had been expecting something.

At four in the afternoon on a Tuesday, Hibari Kyouya walked through the inner gate of the Sawada compound without being announced. School uniform. Mud on the knees. A pheasant carried by the feet.

"Small animal."

"Kyouya."

"This is yours."

He put it on the step. It was, Tsuna noted with a professional detachment he was not proud of, a genuinely excellent pheasant: whole, unmarked, still warm. Taken clean, by somebody who had gone to some trouble to take it clean.

"Thank you," said Tsuna.

"It is not a gift."

"…All right."

"A gift is a thing you give and then it is finished." Kyouya said this with the enormous patience of a person explaining something to a creature of limited faculties, which was, Tsuna had come to understand, simply how he spoke to everyone. "This is not finished."

"What is it, then?"

"The last one."

And then he stood there, in the mud-kneed uniform, and waited, and Tsuna understood with an unpleasant lurch that a question had just been asked and was currently sitting in the air between them, and that the boy in front of him was six years old and would stand there until dark rather than ask it in words.

*Ah,* he thought. *There it is.*

He had spent the better part of six years not doing this. He had been perfectly clear with himself about the reasons, and the reasons were sound, and he could recite them.

They were different people, all of them. They had their own lives in this world, and those lives were not shaped around him. Going and collecting them one at a time, like a man retrieving his coat from a cloakroom, was the single most selfish thing available to him. He had promised himself. He had written it down, even, in a way, in the part of his inventory that had no items in it.

And then there was Kyouya, who had not read any of that, and who had spent six years arriving.

"I'm going to say something," said Tsuna, "and you're going to hate it."

"Then do not say it."

"I'm going to say it anyway…do you know what you're asking for?"

Kyouya looked at him the way a cat looks at a door.

"Yes."

"Say it, then."

"No."

"*Kyouya.*"

"You are the reason this territory is worth having," said Hibari Kyouya, with the same inflection he might have used about drainage. "If you are damaged, it is damaged. Something got past me in the winter and I do not know what it was. That will not happen twice. To make certain of it I require the connection, because without it I am guessing, and I do not guess."

He said it as a logistics problem. He meant it as a logistics problem. That was the thing that nobody in Tsuna's previous life had ever managed to get through their heads about this person, and it had caused an extraordinary amount of unnecessary suffering.

"That's not how it works," said Tsuna. "It isn't a sensor. It goes both ways. You'd feel me. I'd feel you. You don't get to have that only in the direction that's convenient."

"I know."

"You would be *bound*, Kyouya. To a person. You would not be able to simply leave."

"I am aware of what the word means."

"And you want that."

"I have wanted it since the winter," said Kyouya, "which you know, because you have been reading about it at night with a candle like a coward."

Tsuna felt his entire face go hot. "How could you possibly…"

"Kusakabe's aunt cleans the library."

"That is a *scandalous* abuse of a domestic arrangement!"

"It is a *territory*," said Kyouya, and for one absolutely unmistakable instant, the corner of his mouth moved.

Musashi did it three evenings later, in the shrine proper.

She had taken one look at the two of them and said that whatever else this was going to be, it was not going to be two children doing something permanent to each other on the back step with a dead bird.

It was not a large ceremony, and it was not supposed to be. Musashi had explained the reasoning on the way over, at length, in the tone she used for things she had decided were educational.

"Who is it for?" she had asked him.

"Us."

"Wrong. Try again."

"…The shrine?"

"Also wrong. And worse, because it is…nearly right." She had not slowed down. "It is for the *Fire*. A bond between two *Honō* is a thing that happens in the world whether anybody watches it or not, and the ceremony does not cause it. The ceremony **names** it. A thing with a name can be argued about later. A thing without one can only be denied."

"That's very cynical for a priestess."

"Masaya, I have been the *Ruri* of this shrine for forty-eight years. Where do you imagine the cynicism came from?"

There were five people in the room. The two of them. His grandmother in full dress, with the fan. Ayame at the lamps. And, standing at the back with her hands folded and her face doing nothing at all, Kyouya's mother.

Tsuna had wondered whether she would come. He had wondered, more carefully, what she would think.

*Fire,* his grandmother said, in the old words, with the fan in her hand and her voice pitched to carry into the beams. It was not the word he thought in. In his head it had been *Flame* for two lifetimes, a substance, a thing a person had, a resource you allocated across a family like a budget. Here it was a different word and a different grammar. Here a man did not *have* it. Here he *was* it, and a bond was not a contract between two parties who each brought something to the table. It was a statement about what two things were to each other, made out loud, in front of a god, in a building that had been standing for four hundred years.

He had stopped finding that quaint about five years ago.

Ayame lit the lamps with her *Ketsueki* and the light came up red and stayed red, and the shadows did not move as shadows ought to. A *Kogyoku* can do that. Nobody in this room found it remarkable, and Tsuna had long ago stopped finding it remarkable either, which he considered one of the more genuinely alarming facts about his current life.

Then Musashi said the long form.

It took eleven minutes.

He had read it on paper. He had not understood, until he heard it out loud in a four-hundred-year-old room, that the long form is not a prayer. It is a **register**. It states, in order, what each party is, so that nobody can later claim to have been something else.

It began with the witnesses, because a register begins with who was standing there.

*Sawada Ruriko. Ruri of this shrine. Gen'ei, whole.*

*Ayame of this house. Kogyoku. Ketsueki, whole.*

*Hibari Seiran of the Hibari. Seigyoku. Yasuragi, whole.*

Indra said her own line from the back of the room without being prompted, in a voice Tsuna had heard exactly twice before, and both of those times somebody had knelt afterwards.

Then the two of them.

*Sawada Masaya. Kohaku of this house. Kotei, whole, unsealed, and held.*

That last word was new to him, and he did not like it, and he did not ask…

*Hibari Kyouya. Shimizuakira of the Hibari. Getsufuji, whole, unsealed, and unattached.*

"Unattached?" said Tsuna, who had promised himself he would not interrupt.

"Until the end of the sentence," said Musashi, without looking up, "yes."

"Sorry."

"You will be. Kneel properly, you are listing."

Kyouya had been drilled in his part that afternoon by a man from his own household, and had clearly found the drilling intolerable. When it came to him he said it in six words and did not stumble on any of them, and Tsuna, who knew precisely how much that cost a six-year-old, kept his face entirely still.

Then his grandmother looked at Tsuna, and said the name that was not a name, the way she had said it once before over a cradle.

*Tenmei.*

And Tsuna said the thing that opened.

He had been braced for something like the *Amakakeru Tsubasa*. That had been the only comparison he had, and it was a bad one, and he should have known better.

A dome is a *Kotei* doing the one thing a *Kotei* does at scale. Harmony is a different verb. His grandmother had spent an entire winter trying to get this through to him, mostly by refusing to define it and then looking meaningfully at the garden, and he had not understood a single word of it until this exact moment on this exact set of mats.

It was nothing like it. The dome had gone up over a whole town, enormous and impersonal, weather rather than an event. This was small and extremely specific and it came in from the side, as real things did.

And it was a **Cloud**. He had forgotten, somehow, across two lifetimes and six years, what a Cloud actually felt like from the inside of a bond.

*Getsufuji.* Moon-wisteria, in the old naming, and whoever had chosen that word had been paying attention: the thing that arrived was not a wall and was not a weapon. It was **propagation**. It felt like something that grows where it is put, takes hold of whatever is nearest, and then declines, permanently and without argument, to be removed.

In his previous life he had once watched a man hold a corridor for nine hours on that principle alone.

*Ah,* he thought again, quite helplessly, as it folded into place behind his sternum and settled there as though it had been waiting in the hallway. *There you are.*

Across the mats, Hibari Kyouya's chin came up a fraction of a degree, and he looked around the room once, rapidly, the way a man checks a perimeter.

"Well?" said Tsuna.

"It is adequate," said Kyouya.

Behind him, his mother closed her eyes briefly, and Tsuna could not tell, then or later, whether it was relief or something much more complicated.

"Is that it?" said Kyouya, into the silence.

"That is it."

"It took eleven minutes."

"It took four hundred years," said Musashi, closing the fan, "and eleven minutes. *Akizuki.*"

Kyouya's head came round very fast.

He had never been called it before. Tsuna watched him receive a ritual name in front of his mother and four hundred years of ceiling. He watched him decide, visibly, in about a second and a half, that he was going to pretend this had not affected him.

He watched him fail.

"…That is acceptable," said Hibari Kyouya.

"How gracious," said his grandmother.

Afterwards there were sweets, because his grandmother believed that a thing without food at the end of it had not properly happened. Kyouya ate four and declared them acceptable. Then he stood up, announced that he had a patrol, and left through the garden rather than the gate, on the grounds that the gate was longer.

Tsuna sat on the step in the dark with the taste of sugar in his mouth and the new weight sitting quietly behind his breastbone, and did his accounting, because he always did.

And then there was the other thing, which he had been carefully not looking at all evening.

A bond runs between two *crux*. That was the mechanism, stated plainly in every book his grandmother owned: two *Honō* stand close enough for long enough, and their Fire agrees to overlap, and the overlap lives in the place where a person's Fire sits.

His did not sit anywhere. His *Kotei* was in a tree.

It had been in the tree since he was one month old. His own body had taken one look at what it was carrying and had pushed it as far from the *crux* as it could reach, and that was the only reason he had lived past the first winter. The *Amakakeru Tsubasa* over Namimori ran off it. The sakaki held it. He went out there and synchronised with the neutral core of that tree as other people took medicine. It dragged the whole arrangement a little closer to where it should be.

It was not enough. It was never going to be enough. A child's body is a ceiling, and he was going to keep growing into it and then past what it could hold.

So the honest question, the one he had been declining to ask in front of witnesses, was: **what exactly had Kyouya just bonded to?**

He sat with that for a while.

The answer, as far as he could tell from the inside, was that it had worked, and that it had worked *sideways*. Whatever had folded in behind his sternum tonight was not sitting where a bond was supposed to sit. It had gone to the place his Fire actually lived, out under a tree in the dark, and it had attached itself there instead, without asking anybody, in the manner of a *Getsufuji* attaching to the nearest solid thing.

Which meant that Hibari Kyouya was now bonded, by whatever the technical term would be if anybody had ever had cause to invent one, to a four-hundred-year-old tree with a Sky in it.

Tsuna put his head in his hands.

It was funny. It was genuinely funny, and there was nobody alive he could explain the joke to, and that combination had been the shape of his entire second life.

And underneath the funny part: the *Getsufuji* had found his Fire immediately. It had not been confused. It had gone straight out to the sakaki as though the route were marked, which meant the route **was** marked, which meant a sufficiently sensitive person standing in the right place could in principle follow it.

He added that to the list. Not the list of five hundred and sixty-six. The other one, the one with no items on it, the one he was not keeping.

He had said he would not collect them. He had held that for six years. It had cost him something every single time one of them walked past him in the street with a face he knew and no idea who he was. He had gone on holding it, and he had been right to.

And tonight he had let one in.

The honest version was this: he had not chosen Kyouya. He had never chosen Kyouya. In either world, what had actually happened was that Kyouya had decided something and had then arrived at the necessary speed until it was true. The other one had done it with a tonfa and a challenge on a rooftop. This one had done it with four lacquered boxes and a pheasant.

So he had not broken the promise. He had simply been outmanoeuvred by a six-year-old, which was, he reflected, the story of both of his lives.

## 2.4

The connection was not what Kyouya had expected, and he spent the walk home revising.

He had anticipated an instrument. Something with a direction to it, like a compass. It would tell him where the small animal was and whether the small animal was intact, and he would consult it at intervals in the manner of a man checking a fence line. He had made a number of plans on that basis. Several of them would now need amending.

What he had instead was both worse and better than that. It was not a readout. It was a *presence*, sitting somewhere below his ribs and slightly to the left, in the manner of a room behind a man with someone standing in it. It offered no data. It simply meant that the question *is it intact* had stopped being a question, permanently. In the same way the location of his own hands had stopped being a question.

He found, after some consideration, that he did not dislike it.

This was noted and set aside for later examination.

There was also the matter of the name.

*Akizuki.* Autumn-moon. He had been a *Shimizuakira* since he was four years old and had known it the way he knew his own height, as a measurement, as a thing on a form. Nobody had ever said the other word at him. The other word was for ceremonies, and ceremonies were for herbivores, and he had held that position with total consistency for three years.

He had held it until forty minutes ago, when a woman with a fan said it across a room and something in his chest went *sideways*.

He examined the reaction on the walk and did not reach a conclusion.

His *Getsufuji* had not changed. That much he was certain of. He tested it against the wall of the old bathhouse, briefly, without stopping. The purple caught, and held, and let go, exactly as it always had.

So the Fire was the same and the name was new, and the only thing that had actually altered was that there was now a room behind him with someone in it.

Acceptable.

More usefully: the winter problem was now solved. In the winter, something had come into his territory and had done something to the small animal. Then it had gone out again without passing any point at which Kyouya was standing. He had never established what. He had changed the patrol routes the following morning. He had doubled the number of false carnivores delivered to the window. And he had spent eleven weeks in a condition of low continuous fury that considerably improved his form.

None of that had been sufficient, because all of it was *detection*, and detection has gaps. The connection had no gaps. Whatever came next would not get past him, because it would not need to get past him; he would already be there, in the only sense that mattered.

Efficient. He approved of efficiency.

He came down the shrine road at a pace that would have been a run in anybody else, and turned along the river. The willow had a hole under it that the ordinary patrol did not cover. And a route, as he had established some time ago, is only a route if it keeps being proved.

Keystone species. That was the term, from the book with the poor binding.

Remove one particular animal from a place and the whole place becomes a different place. The plants go. The water goes. Over a sufficient interval the shape of the ground itself alters. The book had been discussing sea otters. It had been extremely dull about it. Kyouya had read the chapter four times.

Namimori had one. Namimori had had one for six years and had not noticed, because herbivores do not notice anything. They were currently all indoors, watching television, allowing the place they lived in to be decided for them by whoever bothered to decide it. Kyouya had long ago stopped finding this enraging and now merely found it a fact of the landscape, like weather, or Tetsuya.

So the maintenance of the keystone was not a sentiment. It was the most load-bearing item of territorial management available to him. It had been sitting unsecured for six years. Tonight it was secured.

He thought about the word *bound* and turned it over.

The small animal had used it as a warning. *You would not be able to simply leave.* He had understood the sentence perfectly and had found it irrelevant. He was mildly insulted on his own behalf that it had needed explaining at all. Leaving was not a thing he did. Things left him. That was the arrangement. That was how it had always gone. A carnivore establishes what he can hold, and holds it, and some of what he holds walks off anyway, and he learns not to build on those parts.

Which brought him, as it always did at this hour on this stretch of the river, to the matter of the bigger carnivore.

Two years. He had put the figure together himself, from things that had not been said to him. A passport renewed early. A conversation stopped when he came into the room. Three of the southern patrols quietly handed to Tetsuya's father over the spring. Two years, possibly less. He did not resent it. He had never once resented it. A carnivore of that size does not stay in a territory this small forever. Pretending otherwise would be a herbivore's way of thinking.

What he objected to was the arithmetic. Two years was not enough time to be taught the rest of it, and there was a great deal of the rest of it. He had been rationing the questions he asked, because asking them made the time move faster.

She had been at the ceremony tonight. She had stood at the back and said nothing, which was correct, and she had closed her eyes for a moment at the end, which was not information he could use.

Kyouya put it away, as he did every evening, in the place where he kept it.

Twenty-three months, then. He would use them.

He went under the willow, found the hole, found it empty, and was satisfied. Behind his ribs and slightly to the left, in a house on the hill, the small animal was sitting on a step in the dark, eating sugar and thinking about something complicated. Kyouya knew this without knowing one word of the content.

He found that he was walking faster, for no reason he was prepared to write down.

## 2.5

The paperwork took five months, which Tsuna thought was quite fast, and which his grandmother described as *an outrage*.

It could not be rushed. That was the whole art of it. A file that arrives complete is a file somebody built. And a file somebody built is a file somebody will eventually read properly. So it went the other way. It accumulated, one ordinary piece of paper at a time, from the end of that summer to the middle of autumn, and not one of the pieces was about him.

In June he had a chest infection, and the ward clinic form was filled in by hand and filed. In September a school medical produced measurements that were unremarkable in every direction. In early October there was a fall from a wall, and a sprained wrist, and a note. The X-ray was taken in Tokyo by a man who had never heard of Namimori. He wrote *healthy, active, no findings* at the bottom of the page, because that was what he saw.

And in November, three weeks after his seventh birthday, Sugita Gen'ichi signed the certificate.

It said the boy had come through a long illness in infancy and had made a complete recovery. It said testing had returned no evidence of the hereditary condition present in the maternal line.

*Flame negative.*

In Namimori the phrase was a joke, and it was told as one. The fish seller's wife said it to her sister in Osaka on the telephone in a voice of tremendous innocence. The committee outside the tobacconist, who by then had been failing to decide about the shrine roof for ten months, took to greeting each other with it. Kusakabe's uncle put it on a paper lantern at the following summer's festival, which Indra made him take down, and which he described for the rest of his life as the funniest thing he had ever done.

Outside Namimori it was a fact in a file, and facts in files are the most durable objects in the world.

That was the whole design. A *Sanctuary* does not hide by being invisible; it hides by being **documented**, thoroughly and boringly, in the right offices, by the right people, in a form that answers every question a clerk is paid to ask. The *Honō* laws had understood that four hundred years before there were clerks. His grandmother had explained it to him with the fan, twice.

Nobody made him lie. That was the part Tsuna had been careful about, and the reason had nothing to do with kindness. A man who has been made to lie is a man with a grievance. Grievances keep. So the form asked a question, and the question had a technical meaning. Under that meaning the answer was no. The thing it asked about was not in his body.

It was in a tree.

Gen'ichi had read the question twice. Tsuna had watched him do it.

Then the old man had signed it, and blotted it, and put the pen down.

"Sawada-kun," he said, in a perfectly level voice. "I am going to ask you one question…and then I am never going to ask you anything again."

"All right."

"Is this document going to get you hurt?"

"No," said Tsuna. "It's going to stop me being found."

Sugita had nodded slowly. He had capped his pen. And then he had gone home and got out a black book and written four lines in it, which Tsuna did not see, and would not see for a long time, and which said:

*Signed it. The question was technically answerable and I answered it technically, which is the first genuinely dishonest thing I have done in thirty-two years of practice.*

*He did not ask me to. That is worse.*

*He is seven and he thanked me and he used the word "found", not "caught". I have been turning that word over all evening.*

*Add to the list: whatever is wrong with that child is not a disease, and somebody official is looking for it.*

The file went to the ward, and from the ward into the prefectural system. Eleven months later it reached a grey cabinet in an office in Italy.

A young man whose job was to maintain the external branches of a family tree found a closed entry, read a line and a half of it, and closed it again.

In Italy, that was the whole of it. A closed entry. A line and a half. A young man with forty more files to do before lunch.

Tsuna had spent a further four months arranging for it to be exactly that boring, and when word came back that it had worked he sat in the garden for an hour and did not feel any of the things he had expected to feel.

He had been braced for…relief. What arrived instead was the flat administrative quiet of a job closing.

And underneath it, unwelcome and immediate, one thought. He had just spent those four months, and a great deal of other people's goodwill, on making certain that a family who had never wanted him would go on not looking for him.

Item. One of five hundred and sixty-six.

His grandmother found him out there at dusk and did not ask what he was doing, which was the single most useful thing anybody did for him that year.

In Namimori, nothing changed.

The fish seller's wife went on calling him Tenmei-sama. The committee outside the tobacconist went on failing to reach a decision about the shrine roof for a further two years. Yamamoto Sachiko learned that the girl in her street was called Chiyo.

Tsuna had gone and got the name himself, which had caused a small administrative crisis in the Hibari household, because nobody could work out which of four people was supposed to have authorised telling a shopkeeper the name of a watcher. Indra had settled it in one sentence. *"Tenmei-sama asked."*

Sachiko fed the girl twice a week from then on, and never once asked her a question she would have had to refuse to answer, and at some point in the following winter began sending her home with things for her mother. Nagi's Mist grew in until she could hold an illusion through a whole meal, which she mostly used to pretend to have eaten the aubergine.

On Saturdays there was a game by the river, and Green stood still.

And on a morning in early summer, at the outer gate, Irie Shouichi shouted "Tsuna!" across a public road without thinking about it at all, and a boy who had spent two lifetimes being extremely careful about what he answered to turned round straight away and answered to it.
