# Local Doctor Examines A Perfectly Healthy Child. Local Doctor Does Not Sleep Well After.

## 1.1

Sugita Gen'ichi had been the Sawada family's doctor for thirty-one years, and in all that time the house had summoned him exactly four ways.

A boy sent running, which meant someone was bleeding. A telephone call, which meant someone had a fever and the household wanted it said out loud so it could be worried about properly. A note left at the clinic door, which meant an old person's knees. And Ayame at eight in the morning with her hands folded, which meant the mistress wanted him and had decided, in advance, how much he would be told.

It was Ayame.

"Sugita-sensei." She bowed. She always bowed exactly the same depth, for him and for the postman and, he assumed, for the Emperor. "Ruriko-sama asks whether you are free this morning."

"I am free this morning." He was not. He had the Kobayashi grandmother at ten and a boy with a fishhook in his thumb at eleven, and the Kobayashi grandmother would forgive him and the boy would not. "Who is unwell?"

"The young master."

He waited. Ayame waited better. She had thirty years of practice at waiting and he had thirty years of practice at losing.

"Ayame-san. Is he vomiting, is he hot, is he bleeding, or has he fallen out of something?"

"He is very well," said Ayame.

Gen'ichi put on his coat.

They walked. The clinic sat at the bottom of the shrine road. This had been a piece of extremely stupid optimism on the part of his father, who built it there in the belief that a doctor should be near the priests. In practice it meant that Gen'ichi walked uphill for a living. In May it meant that he walked uphill through everything the hill had decided to grow that week.

Namimori went about its morning around them. The fish shop was open. The old men outside the tobacconist had arranged themselves in their usual configuration, which Gen'ichi privately thought of as *the committee*. It convened at six and dissolved at noon and had never in living memory produced a decision. Two housewives were arguing about a bicycle. Someone's radio was playing something cheerful and badly.

"Sensei!" called the smallest of the committee, who was ninety-one and had outlived three doctors. "Sensei, is it true they are doing the roof at the shrine?"

"I have no idea."

"They should do the roof. It has wanted doing since the war." The old man settled back into his chair. "Tell Tenmei-sama about the roof."

"Tell him yourself."

"I would not presume," said the old man comfortably, and the rest of the committee made a noise of agreement, and Gen'ichi walked on with the distinct feeling that a joke had been made at his expense in a language he had not been issued.

And there were more people on the shrine road than there should have been on a Tuesday.

It was not a crowd by any measure he would have defended. He counted eleven on the way up, which was eleven more than a Tuesday deserved. Every one of them had come up empty-handed and was going down the same way, so they had not come to buy anything or to leave anything either. They had come to stand near the torii for a minute and then go to work.

That had been true for about a year now, and Gen'ichi had noticed it in the way a man notices weather, which is to say that he had registered it without once asking what was causing it.

The noticeboard at the first torii had been repainted in the autumn. He had walked past it perhaps four hundred times since. It gave the name of the shrine and the name of the god. Underneath, in the old characters that the sign painter had plainly enjoyed, stood the line that had been on that board since before Gen'ichi could read: *the only Taisha in the country given to Honō*.

He had known that sentence since he was five. He had never in fifty-eight years asked anybody what *Honō* was, because it was a shrine word, and shrine words belonged to the shrine, in the same way that the timetable at the station belonged to the railway.

"Busy," he said.

"Yes," said Ayame.

"Is there a festival?"

"No, sensei."

"Then why are they going up?"

"To look at the *Honō* board," said Ayame, "and to go to work."

He gave up on that one too.

At the second bend the Hibari boy went past him downhill at a flat run, in a school uniform with the collar open, carrying a wooden sword most of the way off the ground and an expression of enormous purpose. He did not appear to see Gen'ichi at all. Gen'ichi, who had set that boy's left wrist twice and had opinions about how the wrist had come to need setting, watched him go.

"That child is going to break something."

"Probably," Ayame agreed.

The Sawada house stood inside the shrine compound, behind the second gate, in a garden that Kazuki kept in a state of aggressive perfection. In May Gen'ichi had to be careful where he put his feet. There were stones, and moss that was not there by accident, and Kazuki's persimmon trees along the north wall, and against the storehouse the wisteria, which was not in flower and would not be until the year turned cold.

There was a woman on the veranda, and Gen'ichi knew her at once, and that was the trouble.

Hibari Indra stood the way soldiers stand in photographs, with her weight even and her hands loose. She was drinking tea and looking at nothing in a manner that took in everything. When Gen'ichi came through the inner gate her eyes moved to him and finished with him in about a second and a half.

He had been assessed by specialists in Tokyo who had taken longer.

He had known her for eleven years. She was Ruriko's sister-in-law, she had married the younger of the Hibari brothers, she ran that house and a good deal of the town with it, and she was the mother of the boy whose wrist he had set twice. He had sat in her front room last month over a matter of the Kusakabe boy's ear.

She did not live here. That was the entire point. The Hibari lived behind their own wall on the other side of Namimori, and had done since before Gen'ichi was born, and nobody married into that family and then moved out of it.

"Sensei," said Ayame. "Indra-sama has come to live here."

"Doctor," said Indra.

"Madam." He bowed. "Are you well?"

"I am."

"And the boy's wrist?"

"Healed. He is using it to do the thing that broke it."

"Yes," said Gen'ichi. "He would." He let the pause run on, which was longer than he had let one run in about ten years. "Forgive me. Has something happened at your house?"

"Nothing has happened at my house."

"Then I will not trouble you." He heard himself say it and thought, distantly, that thirty-one years ago he would have asked the second question, and that somewhere between then and now he had stopped.

Ayame slid the door for him. Warm air came out, and the smell of charcoal and old wood and something green and medicinal that was not one of his.

Somewhere at the back of the house a girl's voice was reciting something, stopping, and starting again. Gen'ichi had been hearing that voice since the autumn. He had asked once whose she was, and had been told that she was learning with Ruriko-sama, which is not an answer to that question, and he had let it go, because he had let a great many things go in this house.

"Sensei," said Sawada Ruriko, from inside. "Thank you for coming."

She was sixty-three years old. He knew this because he held her chart, and because he was sixty-three years old himself, and they had been born eleven days apart. Once a year, on the day between their birthdays, they had a cup of tea about it. Neither of them had ever said out loud what the tea was for.

She was kneeling at the low table with her hands in her lap. Behind her, the family altar had been dusted, and there were fresh offerings on it, and the persimmons were the good ones.

"Ruriko-sama."

"Masaya is in the back room." She did not look up. "I would like you to examine him."

"Ayame-san tells me he is very well."

"He is very well," she said.

Gen'ichi set his bag down.

"Then perhaps," he said, "you could tell me what he is very well *from*."

## 1.2

The back room had been the sewing room when Gen'ichi was young enough to be sent into it to be scolded. It had a good south window. Someone had put a child's desk under the window and a great many books on the desk, and the books were not children's books.

There was a lacquered box on the floor by the door, the lid off, the paper still folded inside it. Gen'ichi read the tag on his way past, because he was that sort of man, and the tag said *to the Kohaku of the Sawada*, and under that, smaller and rather cramped, *from the Hibari*. The hand had not finished learning to write.

He had seen the word *Kohaku* four times now and had got no further with it than he had the first time.

The child was sitting on the tatami with his legs crossed, doing nothing at all.

Gen'ichi had examined perhaps four thousand children. Children did not do nothing. Children did five things at once badly, or they did one thing with the whole of their body, or they were asleep. This one sat with his hands on his knees and his back straight and his face turned slightly toward the window, and he was as still as the stones outside.

"Sawada-kun."

The boy turned his head. Six years old, small for it, with the soft ridiculous hair the whole family had. He looked at Gen'ichi and his face did something quick and complicated and arrived, a beat late, at *pleased*.

"Sugita-sensei." He bowed from where he sat, which was correct, and which no six-year-old on earth did without being told twice. "Thank you for coming out before your surgery opens."

"You are very polite."

"My grandmother is listening," the boy said.

From the other room, quite clearly: "I am."

Gen'ichi laughed before he could decide not to, and something in the boy's shoulders came down half a centimetre, and Gen'ichi filed that away without knowing yet what drawer it went in.

He opened the bag. "May I look at you?"

"Yes."

"Tell me if anything hurts."

"Nothing hurts," the boy said, with the promptness of a child who has said it before and been disbelieved before.

Gen'ichi looked at him anyway, because that is the job.

Pulse: good. Strong, even, on the slow side of normal for the age, which in an adult would have meant an athlete and in a six-year-old meant nothing he could name. Chest clear. Heart clean. No murmur. Ears clean, throat clean, glands soft. Eyes tracked, pupils even, no lag. Reflexes brisk. Grip, both hands, better than it had any right to be.

Skin warm and dry. Colour good. Nails good. Teeth good, and the gums of a child who was actually eating vegetables, which in Gen'ichi's experience happened in roughly one household in nine.

Height and weight sat below the line for his age, though not by a margin that would have worried him in anybody else. He wrote the numbers down.

"Sawada-kun, have you been eating?"

"Constantly," said the boy, in a tone of some feeling. "It is a full-time occupation. My grandmother has *staff*."

"Do you like the food?"

"I like the food very much. I would like it more if there were less of it arriving at once."

"Hm." Gen'ichi made the note. *Appetite good. Intake supervised, possibly over-supervised.* "Stand up for me, please. Walk to the door and back."

The boy walked to the door and back. His gait was even. He turned neatly, without the wobble.

"Now close your eyes and stand with your feet together."

He did. He stood there like a small pillar. Gen'ichi counted to twenty and the boy did not sway once.

"Thank you. You can open them."

"Did I pass?"

"There is nothing to pass," said Gen'ichi. "Sit down."

He sat. Gen'ichi took the chart out of the bag, and this was the part he had been walking uphill toward for twenty minutes.

The Sawada household chart was four generations deep and written in three hands, two of them his father's and one of them his. The boy's section was thin. Birth, elsewhere, recorded second-hand. Vaccinations, late, done in a batch. A broken collarbone at four that had healed straight.

And one entry in his own handwriting, from a morning five months ago, recording three events that had happened in a single night. He had begun all three with the same word, because he had not had a better one.

*Fainting.*

He read his own notes. He had written them carefully, in the manner of a man who suspects he will be asked to produce them later.

*December, morning. Called at request of household. Three events overnight by household account, the last ending not long before I was sent for.*

*First, approx. 4 min. Second, approx. 11 min. Third, approx. 20 min. Patient unresponsive throughout each, per household. No convulsion witnessed. No incontinence. No tongue injury. Recovery spontaneous, full and immediate on each occasion. Oriented at once. No confusion, no headache, no post-event drowsiness. Denies aura. Says he was "adjusting".*

*Grandmother present throughout and would not leave him. Patient apologised to me, which I did not like. Duration increasing across the three. Referral advised in the strongest terms. Declined.*

*The household will not say what the night was. Something was held at the shrine. I was not told what, and I did not ask twice.*

Under that, in a different ink, because he had come back to it in the evening with a cup of tea and his own conscience:

*If these are seizures they are the wrong shape. If they are faints they are the wrong length. I do not know what they are. Watch.*

And then nothing. For five months, nothing.

Gen'ichi turned the page over as though there might be something on the back. There was not.

"Sawada-kun. In December you were unwell three times in one night."

"Yes."

"Do you remember them?"

"All three."

"Tell me about the last one."

The boy considered. He considered the way an adult considers, with his eyes somewhere in the middle distance and his mouth still.

"I was in the garden," he said. "It was warm. I remember deciding to sit down, because I could feel it coming, and sitting down seemed better than the alternative. Then I was somewhere else for a while. Then I was back and my grandmother was holding on to me and it was a good deal later than I expected."

"What do you mean, somewhere else?"

"Far away," said the boy.

"Asleep?"

"No."

"Dreaming?"

"No."

"Frightened?"

"No," the boy said, and then, with what Gen'ichi would have sworn was the beginning of a real answer, "it is not the sort of thing that…"

He stopped.

Gen'ichi waited. He was good at waiting. He had learned it from Ayame.

"It was not frightening," the boy said. "It was inconvenient."

"And since that night?"

"Nothing."

"Nothing at all?"

"Not once."

Gen'ichi looked at the chart and then at the child and then at the chart. "Sawada-kun, what changed?"

And there it was: the smallest possible pause. A quarter of a second. The kind of pause that in a grown man meant *I am selecting*.

"I got better," said Sawada Masaya.

"Children do not get better from something that happened three times before breakfast and then never again."

"Don't they?" said the boy, with polite interest. "That seems like a very firm rule."

Gen'ichi, who had a bad knee and a worse temper before lunch and thirty-one years of being handled by this family, heard himself say: "Did someone treat you?"

The pause again.

"No," said the boy.

Which was, Gen'ichi thought later, a genuinely remarkable answer, because it was probably true, and it was not an answer to the question he had asked.

"Did someone give you something?"

"Sensei," said the boy gently, "you are asking my grandmother's questions to me."

He said it without any edge at all. He said it the way a colleague says *that is not my department*, and he was six, and he had a milk tooth missing on the bottom left.

Gen'ichi closed the chart.

"Yes," he said. "I am. I apologise."

"You do not need to apologise to me."

"I do, actually. You are my patient. You are the only person in this house who is my patient." He put the chart in the bag. "The rest of them are obstacles."

The boy laughed.

It was a real one, surprised out of him, and it made him sound six. For about two seconds he was a small child on a tatami mat in a bright room, laughing at a rude old man. Then it stopped, and the stillness came back over him like a lid.

Gen'ichi stood, and his knee told him about it, and he ignored it.

"You are in excellent health," he said. "I can find nothing wrong with you. I want you to understand that I am not saying that to be kind."

"I know."

"Do you?"

"You are not a kind man, sensei," said the boy. "You are a thorough one. It is much better."

## 1.3

Tea, then. There was always tea. It was how this house said *the examination is over and the negotiation begins*.

Ayame brought it in and it was exactly right, which it always was, and Gen'ichi had long ago stopped finding that impressive and started finding it faintly unnerving.

There was a man waiting at the outer gate as she came through, and Gen'ichi heard the whole of the exchange, because the doors in this house were paper and everybody in it behaved as though they were not.

"I have brought the cedar from Mie," the man said. "For Ruriko-sama. They told me at the station to ask for the *Gen'ei* of Namimori and I did not like to say it out loud, so I have been saying the name instead, all the way up the hill."

"Ruriko-sama is at tea," said Ayame. "Leave it at the storehouse. She will thank you herself."

*Gen'ei of Namimori.* Gen'ichi turned the phrase over and got nothing out of it. He had heard it before, twice that he could place, both times from people who had come from somewhere else, and both times it had been said the way a man says a rank.

Ruriko poured. Outside, someone was sweeping the path with great vigour and no urgency.

"Well?" she said.

"There is nothing wrong with him."

"Good."

"Ruriko-sama." Gen'ichi set his cup down. "That is not good. That is a problem."

She raised an eyebrow at him. She had been raising that eyebrow at him since they were nine.

"In December your grandson lost consciousness three times between dusk and dawn. Four minutes, then eleven, then twenty. Whatever they were, they were lengthening, and they were lengthening in a straight line." He held up a hand before she could speak. "I asked you to take him to Tokyo that same morning. You refused. I recorded that you refused, because I am not a fool, and because if that boy had died in the new year I intended to be able to say what I had asked for."

"I know," said Ruriko.

"Then they stopped. They did not happen in January, and they have not happened in the five months since." He picked the cup back up because his hand wanted something to do. "Ruriko-sama, I have been practising medicine in this town since before your daughter was born. A thing that is getting worse does not simply stop. Something stops it."

"Perhaps he grew out of it."

"Between one night and the next?"

"Children are resilient," said Ruriko.

"Children are *fragile*, and you have known me too long to try that on me."

She looked at him. Something passed over her face that he did not have a name for and did not like, because it was not annoyance, and annoyance was what he had expected and what he could have worked with.

It was closer to apology.

"Sensei," she said. "Would you like to hear about my grandmother?"

"No," said Gen'ichi.

"Her name was Miyamoto Omasa. She married into this house, which in those days was a considerable scandal, because…"

"Ruriko-sama."

"…because she was not from Namimori, and there was a feeling at the time that the head of this family should be…"

"*Ruriko-sama.*"

She stopped, and drank her tea, and waited, and in that moment she was so exactly Ayame that Gen'ichi understood where Ayame had learned it.

"You have done this to me before," he said. "Three times that I can count. Once when your husband died. Once when your daughter left. And once in December, when I asked what he had been doing all night, and I got forty minutes on the history of the wisteria."

"It is an interesting tree."

"It is a *tree*, madam."

"It was a gift to this family," said Sawada Ruriko, "from a woman who has been dead a very long time, and it flowers in the winter, and nobody has ever been able to make a cutting of it take."

Gen'ichi opened his mouth. Then he shut it.

That was the trouble with this house. Every single time he pushed on the wall, the wall said something like that, and then went quiet, and left him standing there holding it.

"I am going to say a thing," he said, "and then I am going to drink my tea and go and look at a fishhook. Is that acceptable?"

"Please."

"Half the words in this town are not in my dictionary."

She said nothing.

"I have lived here for sixty-three years. I know what a *kannushi* is. I know what goes on the *tamaya* and I know why the offerings changed last spring, because Kazuki told me, at great length, about the persimmons." He turned the cup around. "But the head of the Hibari house is sitting on your veranda with her tea, eleven years married into a family that has never once let anybody move out of it, and Ayame-san tells me she lives here now. Eleven people went up this road this morning on a Tuesday to stand at the torii for one minute. There is a gift on your step in a lacquered box, and I nearly went over it coming in. It is the fourth one I have nearly gone over since the autumn. Every one of them is addressed to a six-year-old, in a hand that has not finished learning to write."

Ruriko's mouth moved very slightly.

"And in the market last week," said Gen'ichi, "the fish seller's wife called your grandson *Tenmei-sama*, and nobody laughed, and nobody looked up, and she said it the way I would say *sensei*, and then she asked me about her knee."

The sweeping outside had stopped.

"I do not know what that word means," he said. "I have heard it perhaps forty times since the summer. I have never once heard anyone explain it, because everyone who says it already knows, and I am the only person in this town who is still waiting for the sentence to finish."

"Sensei…"

"I am not asking you to tell me."

That got her. Her head came up.

"I am sixty-three," said Gen'ichi. "I have watched this family keep its business for four generations and I have never once needed to know any of it, because none of it ever landed on my table. Your religion is not my concern. Your visitors are not my concern. Your *wisteria* is not my concern." He set the cup down. "But in December a six-year-old lost twenty minutes of his life in this house, and that is my concern, that is the one thing in this compound that is *mine*, and when it stopped happening nobody in this house told me why."

"There is one more thing," said Gen'ichi, "and then I will stop, because I can hear myself."

She waited.

"In October I came up here at night for Kazuki's chest. The lanterns in the garden were lit, all of them, and it was raining, and they did not go out." He watched her face and got nothing from it. "I asked Kazuki about it the next week, because he will talk to anybody about anything. He said that Ayame-san had done them with her *Ketsueki*, and that they would be fine until morning."

"Kazuki-kun talks too much."

"Kazuki-kun is the only person in this compound who talks at all," said Gen'ichi. "And when I asked him what *Ketsueki* was, he looked at me with the most terrible pity I have ever received from a gardener, and he changed the subject, and he has been very kind to me ever since."

Something happened at the corner of Ruriko's mouth that in a lesser woman would have been a wince.

"And two months ago," he said, "a woman came to my clinic from Kyoto. Her mother's sister lives on the west side. She had come a long way and she had the wrong address, and she asked me, very politely, whether I was the *Chiyu* here."

Now Ruriko did look up.

"I told her I was the doctor," said Gen'ichi. "She said *Chiyu* again, slowly and a little louder, as one does for a foreigner who has not understood." "She apologised and went away and I have thought about it perhaps once a week since. Ruriko-sama, I have been a physician in this town for thirty-one years. There is a word in this town for what I do. It is not a word I was taught, it is not a word I have ever been offered, and a stranger from Kyoto knew it and I did not."

"*Chiyu* is not what you do," said Ruriko.

"Then what is it?"

"It is a word about *Honō*," she said, "and you have told me twice this morning that *Honō* is not your concern."

"I said your religion was not my concern. I am beginning to suspect that those are two different sentences."

"They are," said Ruriko.

"Then a *Chiyu* is a person."

"Sensei…"

"Sensei…"

"Is there someone in this town who heals people, and is that person not me?"

Ruriko Sawada looked at her hands.

"You understand what that means," he said, more quietly. "Someone knew. Someone in this house knew what was wrong with that child, and knew what would put it right. Nobody thought it worth mentioning to the man who was writing *I do not know what these are* in a book."

"It was not like that."

"Then what was it like?"

And Sawada Ruriko, who had faced down the elder council of her own clan at twenty-five and had not blinked, said: "I was not certain it would work."

Gen'ichi sat very still.

"So there was something."

Her eyes closed. Briefly. It was the first mistake he had ever seen her make.

"Sensei," she said, "you have been very good to this family."

"That is a closing remark."

"It is."

"You are dismissing me."

"I am *thanking* you," said Ruriko, "which is a thing I do not do often, and I would be grateful if you would take it, because the alternative is that I lie to you, and I find I would rather not."

Which was, he had to admit, the most honest thing anyone in this house had said to him in thirty-one years.

He finished his tea. It was excellent.

At the inner gate, putting his shoes on, he looked back down the veranda, and through the open door of the back room he could see the child's desk and the corner of the boy's shoulder and a spill of late morning light across the mats.

There was something on the desk beside the books.

A length of bamboo. Thirty centimetres, perhaps. Capped at one end and stoppered at the other, plain, unvarnished, with a paper tag tied on with string, the way a pharmacist ties a tag.

It was the only ugly object in the room.

"Ruriko-sama," he said. "What is in the tube?"

There was a pause from behind him, and it was a long one, and it was not a priestess selecting a story.

"Medicine," said Sawada Ruriko.

And Gen'ichi, who had dispensed medicine in this town for thirty-one years, who knew the name of every pharmacist within forty kilometres and had trained two of them, said, "Ah," and put his other shoe on, and went down the hill.

## 1.4

He did the fishhook. He did the Kobayashi grandmother, who forgave him, and who wanted to talk about her granddaughter's marriage prospects, and who got twenty minutes on the subject because Gen'ichi's mind was not in the room.

He closed the clinic at seven. He ate. He washed the bowl.

Then he got the book out.

It was not the chart. The charts went in the cabinet and belonged, in a sense he took seriously, to the patients. The book was a black-covered ledger, the fourth of its kind, and it was his, and what went into it were the things that were not yet facts.

His father had kept one. His father had called it *the box of things I have not understood yet*, and had said, on the one occasion he ever said anything useful about the work, that the most dangerous moment in a doctor's life is the moment a mystery stops bothering him.

Gen'ichi had opened the first page of the first one at twenty-six. He was on the fourth.

He turned past the last entry, which was from August and concerned a rash in the Yamamoto family that had turned out to be washing powder, and he uncapped the pen, and then he sat with it uncapped for a while.

Because here was the difficulty, and he wanted it stated properly.

There was nothing wrong with the child. That was the finding. That was the entire finding, and it was true, and he had taken it with his own hands an hour after breakfast. A well-grown, well-fed, neurologically unremarkable six-year-old boy, with excellent reflexes and a pulse on the slow side of normal. Three episodes of unexplained loss of consciousness in one night in December, and none in the five months since.

If a colleague in Tokyo had read that chart, he would have written *resolved* and closed it. It happened. Children had odd years, and a fair number of those years went into the cabinet without ever being given a name.

And Gen'ichi could not make himself write the word.

He sat with the pen and tried to be honest about why, because the alternative was that he was an old man who had got his feelings hurt in a nice room.

*Reason one.* The curve. Four minutes, eleven minutes, twenty, inside a single night. Three points on a line going up. Things that go up do not go flat. They go up until something interferes, or they go up until the patient dies. He had seen both. He had never seen the third thing.

*Reason two.* The recovery. He wrote it out again, slowly: *oriented at once, no confusion, no post-event drowsiness, denies aura*. That was not a faint. That was not a fit. A brain that has just been off does not come back polite. That boy had come back and *apologised to him*.

*Reason three.* The household. It was not a question of what the household had said to him. It was a question of what the household had done. There had been no doctor called in from outside. No second opinion, no Tokyo, no specialist. A family with this much money and this much fear does not simply decide to accept a mystery in its only grandson.

Unless it was not a mystery to them.

*Reason four.*

He wrote *Reason four* and looked at it.

Thirty-one years ago, when he was thirty-two and had been in his father's chair for six years and thought he was rather good, the earth had moved. He had worked for two days without sleeping. He had done things in the school gymnasium that he still, occasionally, dreamt about.

And on the third day they had brought him Sawada Ruriko, who had lost her father and her mother and her husband inside four minutes, and who had a head injury he could describe precisely and could not do one single thing about.

She had not woken up.

She did not wake that week, and she did not wake that month, and after a while the ward stopped asking him whether she had. He had gone up the hill every day for a year and a half. He had turned her, and watched her chest, and written the numbers down, and told her family the truth. The truth was that he did not expect her to wake, and that if she woke he could not say what would come back with her.

And then the Hibari had brought someone in from India.

He had never even been told their name. He had come up the hill one morning in the second winter and found people in the room he did not know. There was a smell he could not place, and a screen had been put up across the sliding door. Someone came out and bowed to him very correctly and said that Sugita-sensei's care had been invaluable and the family were grateful.

Nine days later Sawada Ruriko opened her eyes and asked for water.

He had examined her that afternoon. He had found a woman with an eighteen-month-old head injury and no deficit worth the name. He had asked what had been done. He had asked her, and he had asked her brother-in-law, and he had asked the Hibari, and he had asked, in the end, the priest at the gate, which was a low thing to do and he had done it anyway.

And every single one of them had been perfectly polite.

He had gone home and got out the second ledger and written four lines, and he could still remember them, and he had never crossed them out.

He turned back through the book now, three volumes back, on paper that had gone the colour of weak tea, and there they were in a younger man's handwriting.

*S.R. woke. I did not do it.*
*Someone did.*
*They will not tell me, and they are not ashamed, which means they do not think it is mine to know.*
*Perhaps it is not. But I was the one who sat with her.*

Gen'ichi put his hand flat on the page.

Thirty-one years. He had built a whole professional life on top of those four lines. He had been careful and thorough and unremarkable, and he had never once gone looking, because a man who goes looking in a town like this ends up either a fool or a nuisance, and he had a practice to run.

And this morning a woman he had known since they were nine years old sat on the other side of a low table and said *I was not certain it would work*. Then she thanked him. Then she used the word *medicine* about an object that was not medicine.

He turned forward to the empty page.

He wrote the date. He wrote the examination out in full, every measurement, every negative, because the negatives were the finding. He wrote the December entry out again beside it so that the two would sit on one page where a person could see them together.

Then he wrote:

*Conclusion: the patient is well.*

*I do not accept it.*

*Not because I doubt the examination. The examination is good. I doubt the ending. A process that was accelerating has stopped, and nobody treated him, and everyone in that house knows why, and the reason is sitting on his desk in a bamboo tube with a pharmacist's tag on it.*

*Second time in my life. Same house. Same silence. Same courtesy.*

*They are not lying to me. That is what makes it difficult. They are declining to tell me, which is their right, and they are polite about it, which is their habit. The result is that I am the only adult with a professional duty to that child who does not know what is happening to him.*

He stopped, and read that back, and found it accurate.

*So. Options.*

*1. Write "resolved" and close the chart. Correct medicine. He is well. If he is well in a year he was always going to be well.*

*2. Force it. Refer him over the family's objection. Involve the ward office. Make it somebody else's mystery.*

He looked at the second one, and thought about a small boy sitting straight-backed in a bright room saying *it was not frightening, it was inconvenient*, and thought about what a ward office would do with that sentence.

He drew a line through option two.

*3. Stay.*

*Attend. Examine him regularly, whether or not I am asked. Write everything down, including the things I am told are not my business, especially those. Do not ask the grandmother again, because she has now refused me once honestly and a second refusal would cost her something and would not gain me anything. Ask him. He is six and he is the most careful liar I have ever met, and the places where he is careful are information.*

*Be there. That is all. I cannot make them tell me. I can make sure that when it starts again there is a man in the room who has been writing it down from the beginning.*

*Because it will start again. A thing that was climbing does not stop climbing. It has been given something, and something is not the same as cured, and one day it will not be enough, and on that day this family will need somebody who kept the numbers.*

*I would like to be that person. I have wanted to be that person for thirty-one years and I have never once been allowed. I intend to be difficult about it this time.*

He read the whole page over.

Then, because he was a fair man and because the book was for the things he did not understand and not for the things he had decided, he added one more line at the bottom, smaller:

*N.B. It is also possible that the child simply got better. It is possible that I am an old man who has waited thirty-one years for a second chance at a question, and has decided that a healthy boy will do.*

*Watch anyway.*

Then he turned to the back of the ledger, where he had never written anything, and where his father had kept a page of drug dosages that Gen'ichi had long since committed to memory.

He ruled a line across the top and wrote a heading, and felt faintly ridiculous doing it, and did it anyway.

*Words used in this town which I do not have.*

*Honō.* On the shrine board. Before I could read. Everyone here has looked at that board their whole lives. Nobody has ever explained it to me and I have never asked, and I am now fifty-eight years past the point where asking would have been normal.

*Tenmei.* Used of the boy, and only of the boy. Heard first in the summer from a stranger, then from the fish seller's wife, then from a child late for school, then this morning from a man of ninety-one who has known me since I was born. Said as a title and never once as a joke. Roughly forty occasions since June. Nobody explains *Tenmei* because nobody in this town believes it requires explaining.

*Kohaku.* On the tag of the fourth lacquered box, this morning. *To the Kohaku of the Sawada, from the Hibari.* Four boxes since the autumn and not one of them addressed to *Sawada Masaya*. A child is sending them. I know the hand, because I have seen it on a school form, and the child is about six. He does not think *Kohaku* needs explaining either.

*Gen'ei.* Of R.S. Said by outsiders. Said as a rank.

*Ruriko.* Which I have called her for forty years without once wondering why a woman baptised Musashi is addressed by a different name entirely in her own house.

*Ketsueki.* Ayame. Lanterns, in rain, October. R.S. did not deny it, which is a kind of answer.

*Chiyu.* Whatever a *Chiyu* is, it is close enough to what I am that a stranger with the wrong address came to my door looking for one. R.S. says a *Chiyu* is a word about *Honō*, and would not say more, and did not tell me there was nobody in this town who is one.

*Kotei.* Once. Two men on the platform at the station in August, talking about somewhere else. One of them used *Kotei* and the other one lowered his voice, and they both looked up the hill.

He stopped there and looked at the list.

Eight words. Eight words in a town of nine thousand people where he had been born. He could not define a single one of them. Every person he had heard use them had used them as a man uses a word he learned before he could read.

He added one more line underneath, and this one he wrote slowly.

*Eienzakuramura.* Carved on the base of the old stone by the west field. I have eaten lunch beside that stone since I was thirty. I have never been able to make it mean anything and I have never asked anyone, because I assumed it was the mason's name.

*I no longer assume that.*

Underneath, he wrote:

*This list is not medicine and has no business in a patient record, which is why it is at the back.*

*But I would put it this way. If a physician moved to a foreign country and found that the family he attended used seven words he could not translate, nobody would think him a fool for writing them down. He would be thought a fool for not.*

*I have not moved anywhere. That is the only part of this that is embarrassing.*

He capped the pen. His tea had gone cold an hour ago. Outside, the wind had got up, and somewhere down the hill a shutter was banging, and it was somebody else's shutter and somebody else's problem.

Gen'ichi closed the book and put it back on the shelf, where the other three were, and where there was still room for a fifth.

## 1.5

Wednesday. Half past seven. The school gate.

He did the school twice a month by arrangement with the ward, which mostly meant looking at eyes and ears and signing a form. He had kept it up for thirty years because it let him catch things early in children whose parents would not bring them in.

Also because the school gate at half past seven was the best place in Namimori to find out what was actually happening.

Irie Tomoko was there, as she was every morning, in a good coat, crouched down in front of her son with her hands on his shoulders and an expression of enormous calm that Gen'ichi recognised professionally as *terror, managed*.

"…and you take it before you eat, not after. Before. Shouichi. Before."

"Before," said Shouichi.

"And if your stomach hurts you go to the nurse, you do not wait until home time…"

"Mother."

"…and if the nurse is not there you go to Tanaka-sensei, and if…"

"*Mother.*"

Gen'ichi came up beside them. "Irie-san."

"Sensei!" She stood, and brushed her knees, and did not look even slightly embarrassed, which he liked her for. "Good morning."

"How is he?"

"Fine. He's fine." A beat. "He was up twice on Sunday night."

"Pain, or sick?"

"Pain."

"After what?"

She thought. She always thought before she answered, and her answers were always usable, which made her roughly one parent in forty. "Curry. And he was… there was a test on Monday."

"Hm." Gen'ichi looked down at the boy. Round glasses, too big for him. Red hair, standing up at the back. Small, thin, with the particular grey tinge under the eyes that Gen'ichi had been watching for six months and did not like.

"Irie-kun. Does it hurt now?"

"No."

"Does it hurt before tests?"

Shouichi looked at his shoes.

"That was not a scolding," said Gen'ichi. "That was a question. I ask a lot of them. It is the whole job."

"…A bit," said Shouichi.

"Thank you. That is useful." He straightened. "Irie-san, keep the powder. Keep the times. And I want to know about the tests. If it is only tests, that is one thing and we can work with it. If it is tests *and* curry *and* nothing at all, that is a different thing and I want to see him properly."

"Yes, sensei."

"And feed him before he goes, even if he says he is not hungry. Especially then."

"He says he's not hungry every morning."

"Children," said Gen'ichi, "are the worst historians in medicine."

Tomoko laughed. Shouichi, who had clearly decided that his best chance of survival was not being present for this, had already taken three steps toward the gate.

Which is when the other one came up the road.

He was not alone. Gen'ichi saw Ayame first, walking at a respectful half-pace behind, carrying nothing, which meant she was not going to the market. And in front of her, in a plain dark coat, with a satchel on his shoulder and his collar buttoned to the top, came Sawada Masaya.

Gen'ichi watched the gate.

He had been standing at that gate twice a month for thirty years and he knew exactly what it sounded like at half past seven, which was: appalling. It was the loudest forty square metres in the town.

The noise did not stop. That would have been something he could have put in the book.

What happened was smaller and far worse.

It *moved*. A pocket of quiet came up the road with the boy inside it, four or five metres across, travelling with him. It was made of children who stopped shouting and adults who turned slightly, and of one woman with a bicycle who stepped back off the path without appearing to decide anything. Behind him the noise closed up again perfectly.

Two mothers by the wall bowed, and it was the shallow automatic bow a person gives a shrine they walk past every morning of their lives.

And a boy of about nine, going past at a run with his bag over one shoulder, called out "*Morning, Tenmei-sama!*" in exactly the tone of a nine-year-old late for school, and was gone.

A girl by the wall said "*Tenmei-sama*" without looking up from her shoe buckle. The caretaker, who was sixty and deaf in one ear and had never once in thirty years used an honorific about anybody, straightened off his broom and said "Morning, Tenmei-sama," and went back to the leaves.

Nobody laughed. Nobody looked up.

Gen'ichi stood there with a clipboard in his hand and felt the last thirty years rearrange themselves slightly.

Because that was not reverence, and he had seen a great deal of reverence in sixty-three years of living beside a shrine. Reverence was stiff and self-conscious and it made people behave badly afterwards. This was something else. This was the tone of voice a town uses about a thing it has entirely finished being surprised by.

They were not in awe of that child.

They were *used* to him.

And Sugita Gen'ichi had lived here his whole life. He had delivered a quarter of the adults in this street, and he knew which of these families were feuding and which were sleeping together and which were about to lose a shop. He had somehow been living in a different town from all of them.

"Sensei?" said Tomoko.

"Irie-san." His voice came out normal, which surprised him. "How long has that been going on?"

"How long has what been going on?"

"That."

She followed his eyes. She was not a slow woman. He watched her work out precisely what he was asking, and watched her decide, and it was not the Sawada decision. She did not shut a door. She went carefully instead, testing each sentence before she put her weight on it.

"About a year," she said. "Since he came out."

"Came out of what?"

"He was ill," said Tomoko. "As a baby. For a long time. He wasn't… he didn't come into town."

"That is not what I asked."

"I know," she said.

Gen'ichi breathed out.

"Irie-san. Do you know what the word means?"

She hesitated. Then, with what he recognised with a lurch as pity: "Sensei, I'm not from here either."

*Either.*

He was still standing with that word in his hands when the boy came level with them.

"Sugita-sensei." Masaya bowed. "Good morning. Are you well?"

"I am the doctor. I ask that."

"You never answer it, though."

"I am in excellent health and none of your business," said Gen'ichi. "What are you doing at a school? You do not go to this school."

"I am delivering a letter for my grandmother, and Ayame-san is making sure I do not delight in the errand for too long." He glanced back. Ayame's face did not move. "And then I am going home to read a book about drainage."

"Drainage."

"The east field floods," said the boy, with real gloom. "It has flooded for eleven years. Everyone has agreed for eleven years that something should be done about it. I have read four books."

"That sounds like a job for the ward office."

"Sensei, I have *met* the ward office."

Gen'ichi laughed. It came out of him for the second time in two days, which was more than the previous month had managed.

And that was when Irie Shouichi, who had got as far as the gate and then come back, because his mother had his sports bag and he needed it, walked directly into the middle of the quiet pocket without noticing that it was there.

Gen'ichi saw the exact moment the boy registered it.

Shouichi stopped. He looked at the woman by the wall who had bowed. He looked at his mother. He looked up, a long way up for four centimetres of difference, at the other boy's face.

And he did not bow, and he did not go quiet, and he did not step back.

"Are you the one from the shrine?" said Shouichi.

The air got interesting.

Ayame did not move. Tomoko's hand came up about ten centimetres and stopped. And Sawada Masaya, six years old, who had spent the last four minutes managing an old doctor with the ease of a man handling a difficult supplier, went completely blank.

Gen'ichi had been watching faces for thirty-one years. He had made a decent living out of the half-second before people answer.

He had never seen a child do *that*. It was not surprise. It was a man reaching for a tool and finding the drawer empty.

"…Yes," said Masaya.

"You're small," Shouichi observed.

"I am aware."

"Me too. My mum says it's the stomach thing." Shouichi pushed his glasses up. "Do you go to school?"

"No."

"*Ever?*"

"I have tutors."

"Oh." A pause, while six years of hard experience were consulted. "That's awful."

"It is *fine*."

"It's awful," said Shouichi, with the flat certainty of a child who has been to school and can therefore testify. "Do you know how to play Nine Lives Rangers?"

"…No."

"Do you want to?"

Gen'ichi, afterwards, in the book, would put it like this: *the child did not answer for four seconds*. He counted them. He counted them because he was a doctor and because something was happening in front of him that he did not have a category for, and when he had no category he counted.

Four seconds is a very long time at a school gate at half past seven.

Sawada Masaya stood in the middle of his pocket of quiet, holding his grandmother's letter, in a town that stepped out of his way. He looked at a skinny boy in glasses who had just asked whether he wanted to play. The skinny boy waited with his hand out and a mild impatience that he would have offered to anybody at all.

"Yes," said Masaya.

"Okay! Saturday. Not at your house, it's up a hill." Shouichi grabbed his sports bag out of his mother's frozen hands. "The park by the river. Ten o'clock, don't be late! There's six of us, you can be Green, nobody wants Green."

"Why does nobody want Green?"

"Green's rubbish," said Shouichi, already running. "You'll be fine!"

And he was through the gate and gone into the noise.

For a second the three adults stood there without producing a single word between them. The woman with the bicycle got back onto the path. Somewhere inside the building a teacher began ringing a handbell with more enthusiasm than rhythm.

Tomoko put her hand over her mouth. Gen'ichi could not tell whether she was appalled or about to cry, and concluded, from the eyes, that it was both.

Ayame had not moved at all. But she was looking at the back of the young master's head with an expression Gen'ichi had seen exactly once before, on the face of a nurse in a corridor thirty years ago, and it took him the rest of the morning to work out what it was.

She was looking at him as a person looks at someone who has been handed good news and has not finished reading it.

Masaya stood very still.

"Sawada-kun," said Gen'ichi.

"…Yes."

"Green is not rubbish. Green is defensive. The boy is thinking of the television programme, which is badly written." He tucked the clipboard under his arm. "Do you have boots?"

"Boots?"

"The park by the river floods," said Gen'ichi. "It has flooded for eleven years. Everyone has agreed for eleven years that something should be done about it."

The boy looked up at him.

And the thing that came over his face then was not the quick complicated thing from the sewing room, and it was not the blankness from thirty seconds ago. It was slower than both, and it arrived in pieces, and Gen'ichi watched a child who had spent an entire conversation four steps ahead of a grown man arrive, visibly, late.

"Oh," said Sawada Masaya.

"Wear the boots," said Gen'ichi.

He went in to sign the ward form. It took eleven minutes. He looked at forty-one sets of eyes and ears, found two cases of something going round and one child who needed glasses and was pretending not to, and wrote it all down.

Then he stood in the empty gateway with the bell finished and the road quiet and got the small notebook out of his coat, the one that fed the ledger, and wrote three lines standing up.

*Eight words, and the one they use most is Tenmei, and it belongs to a boy of six.*

*The whole town has a word for him and I do not have it. That is not new. What is new is that I have finally noticed I am the one standing outside.*

*Irie boy asked him to play. He had to think about it.*

*Six years old and he had to think about it.*

He put the notebook away.

Down the shrine road, the eleven people were going up again, on a Wednesday, to stand for a minute in front of a board that said *Honō* and to say a word about a six-year-old that every one of them had known since childhood.

Gen'ichi went and looked at a knee.
