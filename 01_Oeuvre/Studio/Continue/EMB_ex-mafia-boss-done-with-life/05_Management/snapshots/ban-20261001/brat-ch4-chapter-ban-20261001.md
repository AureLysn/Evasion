# Local Child Runs Away From Home, Leaves Note. Local Arcobaleno Cannot Stop Looking At Something That Isn't There.

## 4.1

They moved his desk on a Tuesday, while he was at the shrine office, and they did it out of love.

That was the part he kept turning over. Nobody had been unkind. Nobody had even been wrong. He came back at five with ink on two fingers and a folder under his arm. The storeroom at the end of the west passage was a storeroom again, swept and empty and smelling of the cold. Every single thing he owned was now in the small room off the main hall, where the sliding door did not close all the way.

Ayame was still carrying in the last of the ledgers. She saw his face and set the stack down and stood up very straight, the way a Ketsueki of this house stands when she has decided to take something on the chin.

"Ruriko-sama said the west passage is cold," she said.

"It is cold, and it has been cold since I was four."

"She said you would say exactly that, in exactly that voice."

He looked at the room. It was a good room. It got the morning and it was two steps from the kitchen. Somebody passing in the hall could also hear anything that happened in it, which was the entire point, and nobody was going to say so.

"Where is Grandmother now?"

"At the office until six." Ayame paused. "Tenmei-sama. Nobody in this house is angry with you."

"I know."

"You had three of them in one night, the winter before last. Ruriko-sama sat in that passage on the floor for four hours, because there was nowhere to sit… and afterwards she could not stand up by herself." Ayame said it to the ledgers. "So she made a decision about the room. That is all it is."

*That is all it is,* he thought. *And it is also a corridor with a person in it, permanently, for the rest of my life… arranged by people who love me and who did not ask.*

"Thank you for carrying them," he said. "You didn't have to do the ledgers, they're heavy."

"They are not heavy."

"They're extremely heavy and I've watched you carry them twice."

Ayame's mouth did something that was almost a smile, and she left. He stood in his good warm room with the door that did not close, and understood that he was not going to survive the winter in it.

He went through the rest of the day the way he did everything, which was thoroughly. He answered six letters. He ate. He let Nagi win at a card game she was not winning, and then let her notice, which made it worse and was the point.

At the eight o'clock rounds Indra came through the hall and stopped at his door without appearing to stop.

"You have not said anything about the room," she said.

"There's nothing to say about it. It's a better room."

"Mm." Yasuragi came off her the way it always did, in a slow spread, like warmth going out of a bath. He had watched her put a room of forty people to sleep with it once and then stay awake herself all night to guard the corridor. "Sugita is coming Thursday."

"He came last Thursday."

"He is coming Thursday," said Hibari Seiran, and went on down the hall.

Grandmother came in at six and did not pretend she had not done it.

That was the thing about her that he had never once been able to get round. She came in, she looked at the shelves, she sat down on the floor of a room she had emptied of his consent that afternoon, and she waited.

"Say the thing you have been not saying since five o'clock," she said.

"It is a better room than the storeroom was."

"It is a better room, and that is not what I asked you."

Mist came off her very faintly when she was tired, the way it does at the end of a long day, which was the only time it came off her at all. A Ruriko of her standing does not leak. She had spent fifty years not leaking, in front of people who would have killed her for it. These days the only person alive who got to see it was a seven year old sitting on a floor.

"Why didn't you ask me?" he said.

"Because you would have said yes." He opened his mouth and then shut it.

"You would have said yes in about a second and a half," said Sawada Musashi, "and then you would have found a reason for it, and the reason would have been a good one, and I would not have been able to tell whether I had asked you a question or handed you an instruction. I have watched you do that to Ayame twice this month." She folded her hands. "So I did not ask. I did the rude thing on purpose, so that at least one of us would know it was rude."

*That is the worst possible answer,* he thought, *because it is completely correct, and she is apologising, and she is still not moving my desk back.*

"How long am I in there for?" he said.

"Until the winter turns, and possibly a month past it."

"And after the winter turns, what happens?"

"And then we will see." She got up in stages, the way she did now. "Tsuna. I sat on that floor for four hours in the dark and I could not get up afterwards, and I did not tell you that, and now I have. Do what you like with it."

She went out. She did not slide the door shut, because the door did not shut.

He sat where he was and listened to her go down the hall. One step, then a pause, then one step. That was new this year, and nobody in the house mentioned it.

*She is seventy-four,* he thought. *She is a Gen'ei of the Sawada, she has held this shrine for fifty years, and she sat on a cold floor in the dark for four hours because a child she did not ask for was somewhere else inside his own head… and the thing she is apologising for tonight is a room.*

Tsuna sat on the floor of his good room for a while. The fox came out of his sleeve and walked the length of the new shelves, taking an inventory of a building it disapproved of. Then it sat down on the ledger that had the least to do with anything.

"Don't," he said. "I know." It looked at him.

"I know, Tomoshibi."

There was a shape under all of it he had been not looking at for eleven days, since a wall above a river, and it had the same three words in it every time. *I don't know.* He did not know what he liked. He did not know what he wanted on a day when nobody needed anything. He had spent four years in this life building something that was, when you took the paperwork off it, a very good imitation of the one before. And the people in this house had now arranged themselves gently around that imitation… so that it could go on in a warmer room.

They would keep doing it. That was the unbearable part and it had nothing to do with blame. They would keep doing it beautifully, forever. He would get older inside it… and one day he would be a very kind old man who had never once found out what he was like.

At half past ten he got up and packed.

It took eleven minutes, because he had been unconsciously packing for eleven days. Two changes of clothes. The good grey coat with the pocket. A roll of notes in three currencies from the box behind the third shelf. A folder. The tube, because the tube went where he went even though nothing lived in it any more.

Then he sat down at the desk that was in the wrong room and wrote the note, and the note took longer than the packing. He got it down to three lines in the end, but it took five drafts… and the drafts were the interesting part.

The first one ran to a page and a half and explained everything, with dates. He burned it. The second explained nothing and read like a ransom note, which under the circumstances was funny for about one second. The third apologised so thoroughly that it was really a request to be forgiven in advance, and he recognised what it was doing and disliked it enough to burn that one twice.

The fourth was fine and he threw it away anyway, because it ended with *I am sorry to be a burden* and that sentence had been following him around for two lifetimes and he was not going to let it out of the house on paper.

> *I have gone to Italy. There is one thing there I did not finish and I am going to finish it.*
>
> *I am not in trouble and nobody has taken me. I will be home by the eleventh.*
>
> *Please do not send anyone after me. If you send Seiran-san I will be very embarrassed in front of foreigners.*
>
> *Tsuna.*

He read it twice, which was one time fewer than he would have read it last year.

He knew exactly what the house would do with it. Grandmother would go white and then very calm. Indra would be at the harbour inside forty minutes and would find nothing, because she was good and because he was better at this than a seven year old had any business being. Nagi would not sleep. Ayame would carry things.

And they would all still be there on the eleventh.

That was the thing. He turned it over at the door and could not make it come out any other way. *They will be frightened, and they will be angry, and not one of them is going to leave.* A year ago he could not have written that sentence down. Two years ago he would not have gone, and it would have looked like consideration, and it would have been fear with good manners on.

He put the note where the tea tray goes, because Ayame would find it first and Ayame did not panic.

"Come on," he said. The fox came.

## 4.2

Going out by Cloud is not hiding, and it took him most of a year to understand why the difference mattered.

Hiding is something you do to a thing that is there. His Sky was there. It was in a tree in the garden he was currently walking past, and it was the size of sixty years, and it had spent all of both his lives doing the one thing a Sky does, which is to be noticed. A Sky pulls. It leans out at people. In the old life he had walked into rooms and watched conversations bend toward him like grass, and there had been years when he thought that was a thing he was doing on purpose.

You cannot hide that. You can put a lid on it and the lid will glow.

He had tried the lid once, at five, in the storeroom, out of curiosity and bad judgement. It had lasted eleven seconds and Ayame had come at a run from the kitchen with a knife still in her hand, and Grandmother had arrived thirty seconds later from the shrine office three hundred metres away, and neither of them had asked what the light was.

That was the trouble with a Sky. You cannot put a lid on a thing whose entire nature is to be the centre of the room. You can only make the lid glow.

The Cloud was different, and the difference was not strength. He had almost nothing of it. What he had was a small stubborn quality that did not want anybody inside about two arms' length of him… and the remarkable part, the part that had taken him most of a year, was that it never once had to say so.

He had assumed at first that it worked like camouflage. It does not. Nobody fails to see a Cloud. What happens is that forty people on a street, not one of whom could tell you they had decided anything, all quietly decline to be the one who walks into that space. A Sky pulls a room toward it. A Cloud is a thing a room agrees, without ever discussing it, to leave a gap around.

And the direction of it was the joke. From outside it looks like a man being kept apart from everybody. From inside it is the exact opposite. He was not the one being shut out. He was carrying the quarantine about with him, and the world backed off from it the way the world backs off from a dog it does not know, politely, and without once being asked.

Nothing got pressed down. Nothing got spent. He was never at any point hidden… and he crossed half of Liguria without one person coming near enough to want his name.

There was a hole in that, and he knew there was a hole in it, and he had decided he could live with the hole for eight days. A moving pocket of good manners is invisible to every person alive except the one sort who has been trained to look for exactly that… and there was no reason on earth for that sort of person to be looking at a Japanese child on a coast road in January.

In the old life he had never had a second flame worth the name, and he had known that the way a man knows he is not musical. It was on his file. It had been measured by three separate Vongola technicians with three separate machines, and all three had written down some variation of *Sky, dominant, no significant secondary*, and nobody had ever thought about that Cloud again, least of all him.

The thing he had now was not much. But it had been *there* the whole time… and in this life, unsealed and unmeasured and written on nobody's file at all, it had quietly turned out to be the most useful thing he owned.

Tomoshibi rode in the coat pocket and did not approve of the ferry.

The shrine at the bottom of the hill had its lamps lit at eleven at night, which no shrine on this coast does, and an extremely old woman was sitting at the gate with a flask and two cups.

"Kohaku-sama," she said.

"Ishii-san. It is nearly eleven at night."

"My grandson's boat leaves at midnight for Maizuru and my grandson does not ask questions, because he was raised properly." She poured. "Drink that. It is cold on the water and you are a child, whatever else you are."

He drank it. It was barley tea, and it was too hot, and he stood in the dark in front of a woman eleven times his age and did not have one single thing to say.

"Does the shrine office know?" he managed.

"The shrine office is three women and me, and two of us are asleep." Ishii-san considered. "The one who is not asleep and who is not me will tell Ruriko-sama at about seven, because she is a good girl and she is frightened of your grandmother, and seven is when your grandmother gets up. Is that acceptable?"

"That's…" He did the arithmetic. Seven hours. "That's generous."

"That is *arithmetic*," said Ishii-san. "I am eighty-one, not soft."

"You are wondering how I knew," she said.

"No. You are the fourth shrine between my house and the water, and the second one with the lamps lit at this hour. I stopped wondering somewhere around the first one." He handed the cup back. "What I am wondering is why not one of you is trying to stop me."

Ishii-san thought about that with the seriousness it deserved.

"Because the contract does not say that," she said at last. "It says we keep the town. It says we keep the Kotei. It does not say we keep him *in*." She took the cup. "Also I am eighty-one and you are seven, and if I try to hold on to you I will fall over, and then we will both be embarrassed."

He laughed. It came out of him sideways and he did not expect it.

"Ishii-san. If somebody comes and asks."

"Then a boat left at midnight with nobody on it." She was already folding the cloth. "Go on. Kaneko at the paper shop has a nephew in Kobe, and Kobe has a man who does freight, and after that I am told it stops being my business and starts being God's."

"Why are none of you afraid of me?" he said.

The question came out before he had approved it, and it surprised both of them. Ishii-san put the flask down.

"Kohaku-sama," she said, "when my mother was a girl there was no Kotei, and the contract was a thing the old people said to keep children off the water, and every year one or two families gave up and went to Osaka. I have watched this town be a place that was slowly ending. Do you understand?" She screwed the cap on. "You are seven years old and you are frightened that we are frightened of you. That is not the problem you think it is. Get on the boat."

The boat went out at midnight with nobody on it. The grandson did not ask questions and had, as advertised, been raised properly. He gave Tsuna a folded tarpaulin to sit on, pointed at the thermos, said the word for *there* and the word for *sleep*, and went back to the wheel and stayed there.

Tsuna sat at the back with the tube across his knees and the cold coming up through the deck, and he watched the shore go from a shape into a line into nothing at all, and about six kilometres out he felt something at the back of his head sit up.

He did not turn around, because there was nothing to turn around to. It was not a sound. It was the thing at the other end of a rope noticing that the rope had moved, and it happened at the exact moment the last of Namimori went under the horizon, and Tsuna put his hand flat on the deck and said a word a seven year old should not know.

Hibari Kyouya woke at nine minutes past midnight, which was not unusual, and sat up in the dark, which was.

He went through it properly, because a thing you cannot name is a thing that has not been examined enough. The house was correct. The grounds were correct. Tetsuya was asleep in the east wing and had been snoring since ten, a fact Kyouya had verified on three separate occasions and had strong feelings about.

Namimori itself was quiet in the way it went quiet after eleven, which he could read down to about the level of a single unfamiliar car.

Nothing was wrong. And something had *gone*.

He sat with it for some time. He had carried the second thing since the autumn, since the lanterns and the old woman and a word said over his head that he had let happen, and in the eleven months since then it had done nothing at all. It sat somewhere behind his ribs like a coat he had forgotten he was wearing… a Cloud's idea of a knot, tied once and then ignored. He had stopped checking on it. Nobody checks on a wall.

Now it had a direction. West, and then not west, and then simply *further*… at a speed that was not a speed anyone walks.

The small animal was leaving the territory.

Kyouya considered getting up. He considered the harbour, and the road, and the five people he could wake who would not ask why. He did the arithmetic of it and found that all of it arrived somewhere between two and nine hours after the thing had already happened, which made it decoration.

He lay back down.

"Tch," said Hibari Kyouya, to his ceiling.

He did not sleep. That was fine; he had not planned to. He lay in the dark with his attention on the thread, a hand on a rail in a dark room, and he noted without any interest in the fact that it had not gone thin.

Whatever it was tied to was still tied.

## 4.3

Italy smelled wrong, and it took him until the second day to work out that the wrong thing was that it smelled like the other life.

The word for it here was *Cielo*, or *Sky* when the man saying it wanted you to know he had worked internationally. Nobody said Kotei. Nobody said Kohaku. In the freight office in Genoa a bored clerk with Storm all over him like a bad haircut called somebody a *Mist bastard* into a telephone, cheerfully, in the tone a man uses for *accountant*… and Tsuna stood holding a folder and felt the floor of the world tilt about three degrees.

In Namimori a person *is* their fire, and you call them by it, and it is closer to a name than a job.

Here a Flame was a thing you *had*. Like a car. You could lose it, borrow one, be between them.

He had known that for sixty years. He had said *my Guardians* about six people in exactly that tone, and had never once heard it.

He tested it on the second morning, because he could not help himself.

He stood in a crowded market in Genoa with the folder under his arm and let the Cloud go… all of it, for five seconds. It was not much. It came up thin and low and unwelcoming in a way that had no expression on it anywhere… and the effect was not that people stopped seeing him. A woman with a crate of artichokes walked straight at him, adjusted around him at the last possible moment without once looking, and went on. Forty seconds later a man did the same thing. Then a dog did it.

The dog was the part that settled it for him. A dog does not fail to notice. A dog decides.

Then he let it drop, and a nun asked him within a minute and a half whether he was lost. *So that is what it is,* he thought, standing in the middle of a market. *Not invisible. Just… not the interesting one.*

He thought about doing the same test with the other thing, for about a quarter of a second, in a public square, in a country full of people who had been trained since birth to notice exactly that. Then he thought about what a Sky the size of his does to a crowd, and he put his hands in his pockets and went to find the freight office.

The network passed him along like a parcel with opinions.

Kaneko-san's nephew in Kobe handed him to a freight man who asked nothing and fed him twice. The freight man handed him to a Japanese grocer in Genoa named Oda who had left Mie in 1961 and who wept, actually wept, on being shown a letter with the Namimori shrine seal on it, and who then over-fed him so aggressively that Tsuna had to hide bread in the tube. Oda's daughter-in-law drove him to the coast road. Nobody asked what a seven year old was doing. Two of them asked whether he was eating enough, and one of them asked whether his mother knew, and he said *my grandmother knows*, which was true by about eleven hours.

Tomoshibi hated all of it and reviewed everyone.

The freight man: acceptable. Oda: excessively loud, food adequate… ranked third. The daughter-in-law: suspicious, on the grounds that she had a dog. The dog: an enemy of the state. The Genoese pigeons: intolerable, and to be pursued through a piazza at knee height, invisibly, which produced a wave of pigeons erupting out of nothing in front of an outdoor café and one elderly man crossing himself at a seven year old who was standing on the far side of the square trying to look like architecture.

"Stop it," Tsuna said, through his teeth, in Japanese, smiling at nobody. The pigeons went up again, all of them, in one white detonation across the whole square. "*Tomoshibi!*"

An old man at the next table said something admiring about the birds. A waiter came over to ask whether the young signore was lost. The young signore said his uncle was at the tabaccheria. There was no uncle. There was no tabaccheria.

He had said it in the accent of a child from somewhere else in Italy, which was a thing he could do, and which was the most dangerous habit he had brought across the water with him. A Japanese boy of seven who cannot order a drink is a lost tourist, and lost tourists are fed. A Japanese boy of seven who can order a drink *and argue about the price of it* is a question… and questions in this country get written down somewhere.

The waiter brought him a glass of something orange anyway and refused payment, and the fox sat on the table and looked at the orange drink with the expression of a customs official.

On the fourth day, in a church he had ducked into because it was raining, the fox found the candles.

He got it away from the second rack before anything happened, which was a mercy, and stood at the back holding a struggling pocket while an elderly priest explained something to him at length in a dialect he could not follow a word of. The priest patted his head twice. The fox, from inside the coat, radiated an opinion about the priest so specific and so uncharitable that Tsuna had to go outside and stand in the rain.

"He was being *kind*," he said. The pocket said nothing.

"You can't rank a priest." The pocket ranked the priest.

And in between all of that, in the gaps, it was the loneliest he had been in either life.

That was the part that surprised him, because he had been alone before in ways that had a body count. This was different and much smaller. It was standing in a square at three in the afternoon, seven years old, in a country whose language he had done forty years of business in and was not allowed to be good at. Three thousand kilometres past the edge of the only place that had ever belonged to him, holding a folder… and nobody alive knew which street he was on.

He counted. Six days until the eleventh. Nine people who would be worried, and he made himself list all nine by name, because it was his doing and he was not going to be a coward about the arithmetic of it.

"Do you know what the worst part is?" he said, to a fox, on a wall, in the rain. "The worst part is that it's *interesting*. I've been here four days and I keep finding things I want to look at, and there's nobody to say it to, and I keep turning round."

He turned round.

"See? Like that. Four days and I'm still doing it." The rain went on.

"At home if I turn round there's somebody there," he said. "That's it. That's the whole discovery. I came three thousand kilometres to find out that I turn round."

He thought about Namimori for a while after that, which was a mistake and he knew it was a mistake while he was doing it.

At this hour Ayame would be locking the office. Nagi would have been asleep for an hour, with a Mist still running slowly over the ceiling above her futon, because she could not yet put one down on purpose and Grandmother had long since stopped asking her to. Indra would be walking the north road. The lamps would be going out along the shrine steps, top to bottom, in the order they had gone out for four hundred years.

And there would be a room with his desk in it, with the door that did not close, and nobody in it at all.

Then he did the thing he had come to do.

Her name was Elena and she was eleven, and she was in a state children's home outside Savona under a name that was not hers, because when a famiglia is erased in one night the paperwork of its victims does not erase itself in any tidy way.

Fifty-one children had gone to Mie. His own prefecture. Some of them near enough that the same bus went past, and he had the addresses of all fifty-one, and he had been to none of them. He had the list. He had checked the list eleven times in eighteen months… and the list was correct. The list was correct and it had fifty-one names on it and the Estraneo intake ledger, which had surfaced in the leaked file four months ago, had fifty-two.

He had spent those four months at a desk finding out who number fifty-two was, and then where, and the answer had turned out to be *a girl with a nerve condition in her left hand who had been moved out of the compound eight weeks before the end, for reasons the ledger recorded as `unsuitable`*.

Nobody had gone back for her. Everyone had counted the same number twice… and gone home. He stood outside the fence at Savona for a long time, and then he did not go in.

It would have been easy. That was the thing. A seven year old can go almost anywhere if he is willing to be a seven year old about it. He could have been inside the building in three minutes and in front of her in five. He could have said *I am the reason your family is dead, and I am sorry I counted wrong*.

And she would have been eleven years old, and she would have had a stranger in front of her saying words she had no use for, and the only person the conversation would have helped was him.

*You do not get to be forgiven by somebody you have not met,* he thought. *That's not what she's for.* He went to a lawyer instead.

The lawyer was an old Genovese woman who took cash, asked no questions, and specialised in exactly the kind of quiet paperwork that had moved fifty-one other children across a planet.

She counted the money twice, established that he was the client and not the client's errand, established that he was seven and decided this was worse, and then read the three letters he slid across without once changing her expression. He appreciated that more than anything anybody had said to him in eight days.

The letters were the school, the local registry, and the woman herself. She had raised four. Her third had the same condition in the same hand. "Does anyone know you are doing this?" the lawyer asked.

"Nobody knows, and nobody is going to hear it from me."

"Hm," said the lawyer, and stamped it. It took two days and most of the money in the coat. There would be a family in Mie, a school, a woman who had already raised four and had opinions about nerve conditions, and a name Elena could keep or throw away when she was old enough to decide.

He never met her. That was deliberate, and it was the single most grown-up thing he did on the entire trip.

"There," he said, on the seafront at Genoa, to a fox. "That's it. That's the whole thing. Six days for one signature."

The fox, who had been rating seabirds, did not answer.

"I could have done it by post," said Tsuna. He watched the water for a while.

"I didn't want to do it by post," he said.

## 4.4

He should have gone home on the seventh day. He stayed for the eighth because Oda's grandson had a saint's day and there was a cake, and that is the entire mechanism by which the next four years of his life were decided.

On the eighth morning the fox brought him a newspaper.

It was not the right newspaper. It was three days old, it was in a language he had once signed a treaty in, and it had been retrieved from under a café chair with a determination that suggested the café had lost an argument. The fox put it on his knee and sat on the corner and waited.

Page five. Eleven centimetres of type about a fire at a records office in Savona. Tsuna read it once, whole, at a speed no seven year old has… and then read it twice more with a finger under the line, not because the words were difficult but because they were not, and he wanted them to hold still while he decided what they meant.

*Incendio.* Fire. Records office. Savona. Three days ago. Records offices do not burn. That is a thing civilians believe. They flood, they get audited, and occasionally somebody walks out of one with a folder inside a coat… but they do not burn. The only people who burn a records office are the people who have already got what they wanted, and who have decided nobody else should.

He sat with the paper on his knee in a country he understood every word of, and he did the arithmetic he had been refusing to do since Genoa.

*Who burns a records office in the same province where I have spent six days pulling a name out of a records office?*

He knew the answer before he finished the question. That was the trouble with the answer.

Eighteen months ago, a famiglia had been removed in one night. The removal had been thorough, and it had been followed, within a day, by a complete file of that famiglia's crimes arriving in the mafia's own systems, courtesy of a leak signed *Sparked*.

He had wanted that. He had asked for it. It had been the entire point, because a hole in the world where the Estraneo used to be is a rumour, and a documented hole is a verdict.

But a documented hole is also an *interesting* hole. And there is exactly one organisation on this continent that reads a verdict like that, waits a decent interval, and then sends people to find out who is handing out verdicts in its territory without filling in the forms.

He had been reading the Vongola's own trail for four months without recognising it. The intake ledger surfacing. The records office in Savona. Number fifty-two.

*They have been walking the same corridor I have,* he thought, *and they started later, and they are faster… and they are not looking for a girl.*

He put the paper down on the bench and looked at his hands, which were a child's hands, with ink on two fingers.

*How long?* he thought. *How long have they been in the province? A week? Since Thursday? Since before I got here?*

And then, with a lurch that had nothing to do with fear and everything to do with professional embarrassment: *how long have they been reading my handwriting?*

"Tomoshibi." He kept his voice flat. "How long have they been in Savona?"

The fox had been in Savona. The fox had been in Savona for two days, because the fox went where he went and had no notion of what was worth mentioning, and what came back to him was not a date but an impression with a shape: a hotel with too few cars and all of them good; a man at a window who did not sit down; a laugh in a corridor at three in the morning from somebody who was not drunk.

And under all of it, unmistakable to a thing that had been made out of him, a Sky.

It was not his. It belonged to somebody else, it was close by, and it was enormous. Tsuna's hand went flat on the newspaper.

He knew that pressure. He had spent thirty years in rooms full of it, and there were maybe nine people alive at any given moment who put out that much and did not run a country, and the number of them who would personally attend a records office fire in a provincial town was smaller than that.

"Well," he said, out loud, in Japanese, on a bench in Genoa. "That's the Varia, then."

The fox radiated nothing at all, which is what it did when it agreed.

*Nine people who love me,* he thought, *and one of them is eighty-one, and every single one of them is going to find out how I died in a newspaper they cannot read.*

He did not go to Savona. He was seven and stupid and homesick, and he was still not so stupid as that. He went to the station, and he bought a ticket north with the last of the coat money, and he stood on the platform for eleven minutes with his hood up, being nowhere in particular as hard as he had ever done it.

At minute twelve a small figure in an indigo hood came up the platform through the crowd without touching the ground, and stopped in front of him, and looked at a place about forty centimetres to the left of his shoulder.

Which was exactly where the fox was sitting.

## 4.5

"That," said the figure, "is a *kudagitsune*."

It was not a question. It was not addressed to him either, entirely. It had the flat annoyance of somebody reading a price tag that says something impossible.

Tsuna did not move. He had spent sixty-odd years learning that the first half-second is the only part of these you get to choose, and he used it to look where a normal child would look, which was up, at the floating person, and not to the left.

"I am sorry, what?" he said, in Italian, with the vowels put deliberately in the wrong places.

"Do not." The hood turned very slightly. "Your accent is worse than it needs to be and you did not look at it. A child would have looked at it. A child would have looked at it *first*, because it is the only thing on this platform worth looking at."

Under the hood there was nothing much to see. A mouth, two upside-down purple triangles on the cheeks, and around their neck on a thick chain an Arcobaleno Pacifier the colour of a bruise.

It was not glowing. Tsuna noted that with the part of his mind that never stopped taking notes. *Not glowing means no other holder within range. It also means they are not worried enough to be checking.* He filed both, and went on being a tourist.

"I have no idea what you are talking about," he said.

"You are talking to it at night. It rides in your pocket. It is currently sitting on absolutely nothing, at shoulder height, and it has been rating the people on this platform. I have been *ranked*. Do you know what I charge, to be looked at by something I cannot bill?" Mammon drifted three centimetres closer. "Where did you get it."

"Where did I get *what*, exactly?"

"A pipe fox." A pause. "Nobody has one of those. Do you understand how rare that is, or are you going to keep doing the face?"

"What face?"

"That one." Mammon did not gesture. "There are perhaps eleven of us in a generation who can see one at all. I have met four of the eleven. Not one of the four had *a live one in a coat*, and two of them would have paid a great deal of money for the privilege."

Tsuna weighed it.

He was not going to get out of this by being a child, because the child act had already been graded and returned. He was not going to get out of it by being a Sky, because being a Sky on a public platform in Liguria was the one thing that ended with him never seeing Namimori again.

So he went the other way.

He stopped being nowhere in particular. For the first time in eight days he let something come up off him on purpose, and what came up was small and flat and unfriendly, and it had exactly one opinion in it.

*Mine.* Mammon stopped moving.

"Cloud," they said.

"Yes. It is a Cloud, and it is mine, and you already knew that."

"You are eleven years old."

"I'm seven, and the fox is mine, and I'm going home." He kept his voice level and let the Cloud sit on it, which made it sound like a door. "I haven't taken anything of yours. I haven't done anything in your territory. I'd like to get on the train."

"That is a great many separate claims for one sentence."

"They're all true."

"Are they? Truth is free. I have never once been paid for truth." Mammon tilted the hood. "They are all *checkable*, which is a different commodity, and that is the one I deal in."

They reached into the cloak and came out with a roll of white paper, and Tsuna, who had read every reliable account of the Arcobaleno that existed and several unreliable ones, recognised what was about to happen roughly one second before it happened and did not manage to look away in time.

Mammon sneezed into the paper.

It was not a small sneeze. It was a comprehensive one, and the sound of it in a railway station was the single least dignified thing Tsuna had witnessed in two lifetimes, and what was left on the paper was not a mist of anything poetic.

It went on for some time.

"That is *disgusting*," he said, with total sincerity, before he could stop himself.

"It is effective."

"Those two things are not opposites!"

"In my experience they are correlated."

"You are an *Arcobaleno*!" He heard his voice go up and could not get it back down. "You are one of the seven strongest living Flame users on this planet and your tracking method is your *nose*?"

"They are frequently the same document," said Mammon, which Tsuna was going to think about later, at length, and dislike.

The wet on the paper was moving. It was not spreading the way wet spreads. It was crawling out into thin grey lines, and the lines were becoming a coastline, and the coastline was becoming a bay he had known his whole second life, with a river mouth and a shrine road and a hill.

Mammon looked at it for a good ten seconds.

"Japan," they said. "Interesting."

"Please put it away."

"That is not a hotel. It is not an orphanage, and it is not an address on this continent." The paper turned toward him, and the grey lines on it went on quietly assembling a bay he could have drawn from memory. "That is a *town*. It is yours. And it is the only thing on this paper, which is not how this works. Do you know how many places come up when I do that to an ordinary man?"

"No."

"Four. Sometimes six. A house, a mother, a bar he likes… a woman he should not visit." The paper folded. "You got one. Seven years old, and the whole of you is one town."

"Please put it away."

"A seven year old Cloud, with a yōkai in his coat, whose territory is one town in Japan, standing in a country where every Sky, Storm and Mist for six provinces answers to my employer, standing forty kilometres from the site of a records office fire that my employer's squad set on Thursday." The paper folded itself back into the cloak. "Do you understand why I am not going to let you get on the train?"

"So," said Mammon. "Do you understand why I am not going to let you get on the train?"

Tsuna understood perfectly.

He also understood the thing Mammon had just said and probably had not meant to say, which was *that my employer's squad set*, and which told him the fire had been ordered and not stumbled into, and he put that away for a life in which he had time for it.

"I'd like to speak to whoever you answer to," he said.

"No, you would not."

"I would. Because you're going to report this either way and I'd rather be standing in the room when it's described."

Something happened under the hood that might have been surprise.

Then the platform went cold, the way a platform does when something enormous walks onto it, and a voice said, from twenty metres back, in the tone of a man discovering a stain:

"Mammon. What is that."

Tsuna did not turn round. He did not have to. He had been a Sky for sixty years, and there was another Sky at the end of the platform with the lid entirely off. Every hair on his arms was telling him how big it was. The readout came back somewhere north of anything he had met in this life, and most of what he had met in the last one.

*Seventeen,* he thought, with a distant professional part of his brain. *He's seventeen. There aren't any scars yet.*

"Boss," said Mammon. "A Cloud."

"It's a *child*."

"It is both."

The cold came closer. Tsuna kept his eyes on the timetable board and his Cloud flat and his Sky where it had been for four years, which was in a tree in a garden in a country three thousand kilometres east, and he had never in his life been so grateful that the most dangerous thing about him was not currently in his body.

"Turn around," said Xanxus. He turned around.

The man was very tall, and he had no marks on his face at all.

That was the thing Tsuna would think about for years afterwards, at odd hours, in the middle of other work. There were no scars on him anywhere, not one mark, on a face that had never been near the Zero Point Breakthrough, on a boy of perhaps seventeen who already had four people standing behind him in a formation nobody had been ordered into.

And it took Tsuna about a second and a half to work out why the formation was bothering him, and then he wished he had not.

Not one of them was a grown man. A Rain with a sword and a schoolboy's haircut, who had probably had a chemistry lesson inside the last fortnight. A Storm small enough that the coat had been taken in at the shoulders and taken in badly. A Lightning who was enormous and was going to be more enormous, and who still held his hands the way boys hold hands they have not finished growing into. And an Arcobaleno, which is its own sentence.

*Oh,* he thought.

He had spent sixty years in an organisation that did this, and he had signed things, and he had told himself the things he signed were about somebody else's children, in somebody else's Famiglia, carrying somebody else's Flames.

"Name," said Xanxus.

"Masaya."

It came out smooth and immediate, because it was true, and because it was the name that goes on paper, and because his grandmother had drilled him on which of his names belonged to whom before he was old enough to write either one.

"Family name."

And there it was. One beat of nothing, out of a boy who had answered every other thing before the question had finished arriving.

"Masaya is the one I use."

"That was not what I asked."

"No," he agreed, and did not give it to him.

"VOOOI!" said Squalo, to the sky at large, with feeling. "It's a *surname*, boss! Who in the name of God dies on a *surname*!"

Nobody answered him, and Tsuna spent the whole of that silence thinking about a man who had come to this country four hundred years ago and taken a Japanese surname off a shrine family and handed it down a line that had eventually produced him. Of all the words in both his lives, that one. To this man.

"Where are you from, Masaya?"

"Japan."

"I can *hear* Japan." No inflection at all. "Where in it?"

And there it was, five exchanges in, and Tsuna understood that the rumours about this man being a blunt instrument had been written by people he had let write them.

"A town," he said. "It's small. Nobody's heard of it."

"Mammon."

"It is in the north of Mie, and there is a shrine in it," said Mammon, "and he is lying about the *nobody has heard of it*, because he is proud of it, and that is a different muscle."

*That,* Tsuna thought, *is going to be a problem for the rest of my life.*

"What are you doing in Savona," said Xanxus.

"I've never been to Savona."

"That is true," said Mammon.

"What are you doing in Liguria."

"Paperwork." Squalo laughed, once, and stopped when nobody else did. "VOOOI! He's doing *paperwork*!"

"Whose," said Xanxus.

"Mine. I had a thing to sign and I signed it."

"You are seven years old."

"I know." Tsuna kept the Cloud flat and his hands out of his pockets. "It's a boring answer. I'm sorry it's a boring answer. If I'd known I was going to be asked I'd have brought a better one."

There was a pause of a length he did not enjoy.

"Estraneo," said Xanxus, and let the word sit there.

"I don't know anything about Estraneo."

He heard it land. He heard it land flat, in front of four people, one of whom was a first-class psychic who had been reading him since the second sentence, and he did not look at Mammon and Mammon did not say anything at all, and *that* was the moment Tsuna understood that his eight days were finished.

Because a silence from a professional liar-detector is not neutral. It is a decision about when to spend something.

"Hm," said Xanxus.

And then the danger changed shape, and stopped being about the fire.

He walked a slow half-circle, and he looked at Tsuna the entire way round, and what was in his face was no longer suspicion at all.

"Turn it back on," he said.

"Turn *what* back on? I don't know what you mean."

"The Cloud. You dropped it when I came up the platform. Turn it back on." Tsuna, after a moment, turned it back on.

Xanxus stood in it. That was the only way to describe what he did. He stood in the thin unfriendly nothing that came off a seven year old who did not want to be anywhere…

…and then he pushed.

It was not an attack. Tsuna knew it was not an attack about a quarter of a second before his body worked it out, which was the quarter of a second in which he nearly ruined everything. It was a Sky putting out one careful finger of itself and laying it against somebody else's flame to see what that flame did back. Every Sky alive learns the trick. Most of them learn it badly, at parties, on people who are not in a position to refuse. Tsuna had done it perhaps two hundred times in a life he was supposed to be finished with, across a table, with a cup in his hand, while a man talked about shipping.

He had never once been on this end of it.

It went in under the ribs on the left, warm and enormous and carrying no malice at all, and it asked the only question that test has ever asked, which is *do you and I fit*.

And his Cloud, which had spent this entire life being a small stubborn thing that wanted nobody within two arms' length of it, did not push back.

It did not do anything whatsoever. It sat there and let a stranger's Sky rest against it like a hand laid flat on a dog's back, and it was **comfortable**, and Tsuna stood on a platform in Liguria with a folder under his arm and understood, with a clarity he would have paid a very great deal to avoid, that he had just been asked a question about harmony by Xanxus of the Varia…

…and that something in him, in a body seven years old, had gone and answered it before he could get there.

Xanxus closed his eyes briefly, and something in his shoulders came down half a centimetre.

*Oh no,* thought Tsuna. "Squalo," Xanxus said, without opening his eyes. "How long does the Ninth's paperwork take on an acquisition."

"VOOOI, for an *unaffiliated minor*? Six weeks, boss, and that's if the…"

"Six weeks," Xanxus said, to Tsuna, and almost smiled.

It was not a threat. That was the alarming part. It was the face of a man who had seen something in a shop window.

What happened after that took eleven seconds and is not worth describing, because nothing in it was a fight. There was a train coming into platform four. There was a fox that could not be seen going through a luggage trolley at ankle height. There were forty commuters between him and everyone who wanted him… and there was a seven year old who had spent two lifetimes learning that the only winnable version of this is the one where you are already gone.

Tsuna went under the train on the far side, came up on the service track, and was three streets away before the doors closed.

He changed trains twice, both times badly, both times in the wrong direction first. He spent two hours in a goods yard outside Alessandria with the Cloud up so hard that a shunter walked past him at a distance of one metre. He threw the coat away at Vercelli, because it was a good coat and good coats are memorable. He moved the tube and the fox and the last four notes into a canvas bag a stranger had left on a bench… and he felt bad about the bag for about six seconds.

He was four hundred kilometres north before he let himself shake.

## 4.6

Mammon did not chase him, on the grounds that chasing is free and information is not.

They stood on the platform while Squalo shouted at a stationmaster, and they watched the place where the child had been, and they thought about the fox.

It had gone through the trolley. It had not gone around it, and it had not gone over. It had gone *through*, the way a thing goes through something it does not agree exists… and it had done that at the exact moment the boy moved, without being told. Which meant either a very old bond or a very strange one, and Mammon could not price either.

*A kudagitsune.* Not an illusion, and they had checked that at the second sentence. Not a box weapon either… wrong century, wrong everything. An actual mountain spirit in an actual child's actual coat, in Liguria, on a Tuesday.

Nobody had one of those. Not a Mist with forty years in… not an illusionist of the first class, not one of the three families on this continent who still kept an onmyōji on retainer and bragged about it at parties.

Mammon had priced a *kudagitsune* exactly once, eleven years ago, for a client who wanted one and who had not understood that the question was not about money. The answer had been that you do not buy one. It picks, and it almost never picks, and when it picks it stays.

And it had picked a seven year old in a grey coat who talked to it in Japanese on a public platform and then did not look at it.

There was also the business of the name, which Mammon had set aside at the time and had now come back to. The boy had answered eleven questions in about ninety seconds, most of them truthfully and two of them cleverly… and had gone completely still at precisely one, and it was the one an orphan has no earthly reason to protect. A child with no family does not decline to hand over a family name. A child with no family hands it over and then watches you fail to care.

And the child had not looked at it. That was the item Mammon kept returning to, the way a bookkeeper returns to a figure in a column that does not add. Eight days of surveillance data from the Genoa network would show a boy talking to empty air in six separate locations, and the boy had known that, and when a stranger named the thing out loud his eyes had gone *up* instead of *left*.

Children do not have that. Children look at the thing. Back at the hotel, they opened the ledger.

This was the part Mammon actually enjoyed, and they did it slowly, with the window open and the noise of the harbour coming up. The Varia had been walking the Estraneo file for nineteen weeks. The file itself was not the interesting object; the file was a dump, and dumps are cheap. What was interesting was that the squad had been *helped*.

Four times. Mammon had flagged all four at the time and been overruled, because when a job is going well nobody thanks the accountant for asking where the money is from.

The Trieste manifest, which had arrived at a Vongola listening post eleven days before anyone requested it.

The intake ledger, surfacing in a batch that had already been indexed twice without it.

Two names, corrected, in a hand nobody would admit to.

Mammon laid the four dates in a line and put the date of the Estraneo's removal underneath them, and then sat perfectly still, forty centimetres tall, in a hotel chair too big for them, looking at a column that did not add.

Three of the four helpful arrivals predated the investigation. One of them predated the *removal*.

Mammon checked it again, because a figure that says something impossible is usually a figure that has been copied wrong. It had not been copied wrong… which left one reading, and the reading was absurd. The Trieste manifest had come into a Vongola listening post eleven days before anybody in the organisation had a reason to want it, and four days before the Estraneo compound had stopped existing.

You cannot help an investigation before it exists. You can, however, be the person who arranged the thing the investigation is about. And who then spent eighteen months quietly steering the people investigating it toward the conclusions you would prefer. And who is very good at it… and who has apparently been doing it since before he could reach a door handle.

"Hm," said Mammon, to an empty room.

There was a knock. Levi, to say the boss wanted the platform report in ten minutes, and did Mammon want the good chair brought up.

"No. The chair is for people who are staying."

"Boss says ten minutes and he said it in the voice."

"I heard him the first time. Go away." The door shut.

Mammon closed the ledger.

They thought about what a complete answer was worth… and then about what an incomplete one was worth. The second number was larger, because a complete answer is spent the moment it is delivered and an incomplete one goes on earning. They thought about the boss, who had spent the last hour in a foul and thoughtful silence and had already used the word *acquisition* twice.

They thought about the boy's face when the paper had shown the bay, which had not been fear. Fear was cheap and Mammon had seen a great deal of it. What had been on the boy's face was the flat look of a man watching a door close that he had personally installed.

And they thought, briefly, about a thing they did not usually let themselves think about at all.

Eleven in a generation can see a *kudagitsune*. Mammon could see one because of what was hanging on the chain around their neck, and the chain was not removable, and they had spent an unreasonable share of the last several decades looking for anyone anywhere who knew a way to take it off.

A seven year old with a mountain spirit in his coat is not an answer to that.

But he is the first *new* thing Mammon had seen in eleven years, and new things are where answers come from, and a person who has been pricing the same closed market for a very long time will notice when an unfamiliar item appears on the shelf.

They went downstairs and reported that the boy was a Cloud. Unaffiliated. Seven, or lying about being seven. A territorial fixation on a single town in Japan, unusually total, and the town could be located inside a day.

All of which was true, and none of which was the thing.

They did not mention the fox.

They did not mention the column that did not add.

They did not mention that the boy owned a second name and had declined to spend it.

"Namimori," Xanxus said, trying the word out, and then, to the room: "Pack."

And in a village outside Aosta, four hundred kilometres north, a child sat on the floor of a bus shelter with his arms around his knees and his forehead down on them, shaking, with an invisible animal pressed flat against his neck, and six days left until the eleventh.
