# Local Assassination Squad Walks Into A Four Hundred Year Old Contract. Local Town Reads It To Them, Slowly.

## 5.1

Namimori knew on Thursday, and the town found out the way it found out everything, which was badly organised and extremely fast.

Ishii-san at the lower shrine had it first, from her grandson. His boat had been asked, in Maizuru, whether anyone had carried a child to the mainland in the first week of January. Her grandson had said no, because it was true; he had carried nobody, there had been nobody on the boat. Then he had rung his grandmother from a harbour telephone, because he was raised properly and because a question like that is itself a piece of information.

Ishii-san rang the shrine office. The shrine office rang Ayame. Ayame put the telephone down, went and stood in the doorway of the accounts room until Ruriko-sama looked up, and said six words.

It is worth writing down what those six words were. For the rest of that winter people in the town repeated them to each other like the first line of a story everyone already knows.

"Somebody is asking about the boat."

Ruriko-sama put her brush down. She did not ask which boat. She did not ask who was asking, or from where, or how anyone in Maizuru had got as far as a question at all. A woman who has run a shrine for fifty years does not waste her first sentence on a fact she will be given anyway.

*Which boat, though? And asking on whose behalf? And how far along is it already, if it has reached a harbour telephone before it reached me?* She thought all three, in order, and asked none of them.

"How long ago?"

"Thursday, before noon."

"Then we have until about Sunday," said Sawada Musashi, "and we are going to spend it being very boring."


By Friday morning it had reached the fish market, where it was improved. By Friday noon it had reached the primary school. A teacher told her class that if anyone saw foreigners they were to be polite and then go home… which was the wrong instruction, and produced eleven children who spent the afternoon patrolling.

By Friday evening Kaneko-san at the paper shop had sold out of the heavy black ink used for formal notices. She had not asked a single customer what it was for. She had a fair idea, and it was none of her business.

She did write to the Sawada house about it, mind. She wrote about the ink, and about the cat, which had now taken over the whole display window and had opinions about the weather. She put the two things in the same paragraph without remarking on the difference… which was, in its way, the most Namimori sentence written that week.

Nobody in the town said the word *Varia*. Most of them did not know it. What would they have done with it? A name is only useful if it tells you what a thing wants, and this one told them nothing at all. What travelled was smaller and more useful: **somebody is coming, and they are coming about Tenmei-sama, and they are not ours.**

The last part was the part that mattered. Ours and not ours is the only distinction a Sanctuary has ever really had. Every child in that town could have told you which side of it they were on before they could tie a knot.

There is a particular kind of quiet that a Sanctuary town does before an outside thing arrives, and it is not fear. Namimori had been doing it since the founding, and the shape of it had barely changed. The old people begin to remember where the documents are kept. The middle-aged begin to be very calm. And the young get sent on errands that do not need doing.

Tomoko closed the clinic at three and reopened it at half past with the shutters down to the sill. That is the position they go to when she expects to be working late, and does not expect to be seen working late. She then spent an hour laying out a tray of things she had not laid out since the winter of the fox wedding, and did not put it away again for eleven days.

Maro spent Saturday walking the perimeter she was not supposed to be able to walk any more. She saw nothing, and came back, and said so… and everyone understood that *nothing* from Maro is a measurement rather than an absence.

At the Yamamoto place, Sachiko took the knives out of the front of the shop and put them at the back, and did not explain why to her husband, who did not ask. She had been in this town eleven years, and had arrived from Namimori's point of view as a foreigner from two prefectures away. She had learned, somewhere around the fourth, that a question in Namimori is a thing you are *given*, and that reaching for one early only means you get the wrong one.

There is an argument that all of this is superstition dressed up in paperwork, and Namimori has heard it, and has a short answer.

The short answer is that the contract has never once failed. Not in the famine year. Not in 1945, when every other town on this coast burned and the fire arrived at the ridge line above the shrine and then, in a manner that four separate survivors described in four separate ways, **changed its mind**. The instrument in that box is not believed in. It is *used*, the way a town uses a seawall, and the fact that nobody now living can explain the mechanism is not considered a serious objection by people who can see the wall.

And at the Sawada compound, Sawada Musashi went down into the storeroom under the north hall with a lamp. Gen'ei of her house, head priest of Namimori Inari-Taisha, seventy-four years old. She stayed down there two hours.

She came back up with a box, and Ayame, who had carried every heavy thing in that house for thirty years, took one look at what she was carrying and did not offer to carry it. Ayame said *oh*, which she regretted immediately, and was told not to *oh* at anybody. She observed, in her own defence, that she had worked in this house for thirty years and had never once seen that box. Musashi agreed that she had not, set it down on the table, and sat down after it in stages.

"It comes out about once a lifetime," she said, "and I would have preferred to miss mine."

The box held the contract, and it did not hold a copy. Namimori had copies; every signatory house in East Asia had copies, and there were three in the prefectural archive that were legally worthless and historically fascinating. This was the instrument itself, on paper that had been remade twice and rewritten once, and the reason it came out of a storeroom instead of a vault was that the contract did not need protecting. Everyone who could threaten it was bound by it, which is a sentence that takes about four seconds to say and about four hundred years to arrange.

Ayame looked at it for some time. "Ruriko-sama. Is Tenmei-sama coming home before or after?"

"He said the eleventh."

"That is Monday."

"Yes," said Musashi. "It is."

## 5.2

They came in on Sunday afternoon, on the ordinary train, like people. That was the first thing the town had not expected, and it went through the market in about ninety seconds.

Everyone had prepared for something that arrived at night. What arrived was four adults and a very small person in a hood, in the middle of the day, buying tickets like anybody… on a platform where the vending machine had been broken since spring.

Nobody stopped them, and the not-stopping was so total that it read, to four of the five of them, as a failure of nerve.

It was not a failure of nerve. It had taken the town ninety minutes on Friday to decide it. The deciding was done in the shrine office by six people over cold tea, and the argument went in a direction that would have surprised anybody watching from outside.

Nobody proposed a barricade. Two people proposed a welcome. Ishii-san, on the telephone from the lower shrine, proposed that somebody meet them at the station with a map. Asked why, she said that a map is a very hard thing to refuse and an even harder thing to be rude to… and that she had once watched a man be defeated by a map at Nagoya station in 1961, and had never forgotten it.

So they gave them a map. What the town could not have explained, and what Mammon worked out somewhere around midnight, is that this is not hospitality. It is **jurisdiction**. Every courtesy extended to an outsider inside a Sanctuary is a small quiet act of filing: *you were received, you were assisted, you were not obstructed.* Four hundred years of that, entered in a book at an inn by a river.

A town that bars its door has a fight. A town that gives you a map has a **record**. This is the part outsiders never understand about a Sanctuary, and the part the Varia did not understand for another thirty-one hours.

The contract does not say the town may bar the door. It says something much stranger, and much harder to walk back out of.

**Anyone who enters is subject to the law of the place they have entered.** So Namimori did not stop them. Namimori *received* them.

The stationmaster bowed. The woman at the kiosk asked whether they wanted a map, and gave them one, correct and current, with the shrine marked. Two of the eleven patrolling children fell in behind the group for a hundred metres before an adult retrieved them. And an old man told Squalo, at length and without being asked, that the ramen place near the second torii had changed hands and was not what it was.

"VOOOI," said Squalo, to nobody, after the fourth such encounter. "What is *wrong* with them!"

"They are being polite," said Levi.

"They are being polite at me *specifically*!"

Belphegor, who had been in eleven countries and had never once been given directions, said that he did not like it, and that he would like to cut something small to settle his nerves. Xanxus told him to shut up without turning his head, which worked for approximately nineteen hours.

At the third crossing Mammon said, without pointing, because pointing is expensive, that the woman with the bicycle had adjusted her route twice to keep a wall between herself and Levi, had done it without once looking at him, and had not changed pace.

"Obviously they know," said Xanxus.

"That is not somebody who was warned this morning, boss. That is somebody who was **taught**, years ago, along with everybody else on this street."

Xanxus himself said almost nothing that afternoon.

He walked the main street of a town he had come to buy something out of, and he read it. A Sky the size of his does not have to try, and a Flame that big reads a street the way other men read a room. A street tells him what it is by how it bends. He noted the shrine road going up the hill. He noted that three separate people had checked where the shrine road was before they checked him… and that nobody in a town this size had looked at five armed foreigners with the face people make when they are surprised.

He noted, near the bottom of the hill, a boy of about nine standing in a side street with a tonfa in each hand, not hiding.

The boy looked at him with an interest that had nothing like fear in it. Xanxus looked back… and something in the shape of the afternoon changed by a small amount.

"Boss," said Mammon.

"I saw it."

"That is a Cloud, boss. A real one, not a half."

"I *saw* it." A pause. "Not one of ours, either. Too old for that."

"No," said Mammon. "Too old, and the wrong colour, and it has bitten somebody this week."

They took rooms at the inn by the river, which had rooms, and which charged them the ordinary rate and did not blink at cash, and where the proprietress asked how many nights and wrote it in a book.

She asked how many nights, patiently, twice, in the voice of a woman who has asked that question nine thousand times and will not be hurried on the nine thousand and first. Xanxus said three. She wrote *three*. She also wrote, in the column beside it, a small character that the Varia did not read and that meant *guests of the shrine*. It was not true yet. She had decided on her own authority that it would be simpler if it became true later, and she was, in the event, the only person in Namimori who got the whole week exactly right.

She noticed, and did not write down, that two of the five were nowhere near old enough to be signing anything, and that one of those two was wearing a **tiara**. She had kept an inn inside a Sanctuary for thirty years. There were worse names in that book, and most of them paid on time.

Mammon did not sleep. That was not unusual. Sleep is a habit rather than a requirement, and they had lost the habit some decades ago.

What was unusual was the reason. Mammon turned it over in the dark, with the window open and the river coming up… and could not make it come out to a number.

The town was not afraid. That was checkable, and they had checked it four separate ways in six hours. Not one heartbeat out of place at the station. No evacuation. No shuttered streets. A fish market at full volume on the morning a squad of the Vongola's assassins walked into it.

And yet every single adult over fifty had known exactly where they were standing at all times. *Somebody has drilled this place,* Mammon thought. *And they have not drilled them to run.*

*Drilled them to do what, then? Stand still? What kind of Sky teaches a town to stand still?*

They got up at two in the morning and went through the local paperwork, which was available, because the prefectural registry had a night terminal and no imagination. Land ownership: unremarkable. Tax: unremarkable. Population: unremarkable and slightly too stable.

Then, at the bottom of the municipal charter, a single line and a registry number. It sat in the section reserved for pre-Meiji instruments that had never been repealed, because nobody had ever found the authority to repeal them.

Mammon read it twice.

Then they went and looked up the registry number, and read what came back, and sat very still on a windowsill in an inn by a river in a country they had not been to in eleven years.

"Ah," they said. *Ah. There it is. And how much is this worth, and to whom, and how long do I have before somebody else finds it?*

The correct move was to wake the boss.

Mammon considered the correct move for a while. Then they considered what the correct move was *worth*, and the two numbers did not match… which is a thing that happens more often than people think, and which had made Mammon a great deal of money.

They put the registry printout in the cloak, and said nothing, and watched the river until the sky went grey.

## 5.3

The town answered on Monday morning, and it did not answer with force, and that surprised everyone present except the eleven people who had been expecting it for three days.

They came up the shrine road at nine.

Xanxus had decided overnight to do this the direct way. For him that meant arriving at the largest building in a town and asking the oldest person in it a question… which was, as it happens, almost exactly correct procedure.

Sawada Musashi was waiting at the top of the steps with a table.

It was not a barricade. It was a table, the low kind used for signing things, with a cloth on it and a box on the cloth. Behind it, on a cushion, sat a Gen'ei of seventy-four. Ayame stood at her left. And Hibari Seiran stood at her right, doing nothing whatsoever in a way that took up a great deal of room.

There were perhaps sixty townspeople on the steps below. None of them were armed. All of them had come.

"Good morning," said Musashi, in Japanese. "You will forgive me for not standing."

Mammon translated, flatly, and went on translating for the next twenty minutes without being asked, and without once looking at the boss. Their Japanese was better than anybody in that squad knew, and had been since the nineteen-forties, and this was the first time in eleven years that it had been worth anything to them.

"I want the boy," said Xanxus.

Musashi asked which boy, and then listed three by household, and waited while a man who had crossed a continent for this discovered that he could not supply a name.

"You know which boy," said Xanxus.

"I do. I wanted to hear whether you would say it, and you have not, which tells me you came here without one. Go on."

"Seven. Cloud. Went to Italy in January and came back with his mouth shut."

"Ah." Musashi folded her hands. "Tenmei-sama."

Xanxus observed that it was a title. Musashi agreed that it was a title, asked him whether he knew how many the boy had, accepted *no* without comment, and told him the number was three.

"The one you are least likely to enjoy," she added, "is the one the contract uses." She said it without any particular emphasis. "You are asking me for the Kotei of this Sanctuary, on the steps of the shrine that holds its contract, on a Monday, in front of the people who signed it. I want to be sure you know that, because in a moment I am going to read something aloud, and afterwards nobody will be able to say they were not told."

Squalo started to say something. Xanxus lifted one finger and he stopped.

Musashi had been watching Squalo while that happened, and went on watching for a moment after it.

She had run a shrine for fifty years and a household for longer, and she knew what she was looking at, and it was not an assassin. It was somebody who had not finished growing, in a coat cut for a larger man, being told to be quiet in front of strangers. Behind him, the small one with the tiara was picking at a loose thread and had been picking at it for four minutes.

She revised something, privately, and let nothing whatever show on her face… and it made the next eleven minutes slower rather than faster.

"Read it," said Xanxus.

So she read it. She read badly, which was the thing nobody in the Varia had prepared for.

She read slowly, in the flat careful voice of a very old woman working at an archaic register. She stopped to get the phrasing right. She corrected herself once, and at one point asked Ayame for a word. It took eleven minutes, and it was the least dramatic thing any of them had ever watched. By the third minute Belphegor had stopped fidgeting… because there is something about being read to, correctly and without hurry, by somebody who is not afraid of you.

Here is what the crowd did while she read, and it is the part worth keeping.

They did not move forward. They did not chant, or bristle, or do any of the things a crowd does in the versions of this story that got told later in other towns. Sixty people stood on a set of stone steps in January and **listened**, with the particular concentrated stillness of a congregation at the one part of a service they know by heart… and somewhere around the fifth minute the Varia stopped watching the old woman and started watching them.

That was the moment Xanxus understood what he had walked into, and it was not the clause that did it. It was the faces. The instrument said, in the order she read it:

That this place was a Sanctuary, established by agreement of every major Flame power in East Asia, and that the agreement had never lapsed.

That no party might interfere in the internal affairs of a Sanctuary. That a Sanctuary took no side, offered refuge without condition, and did not act beyond its own boundary.

That any outsider entering was subject to the law of the Sanctuary for as long as they remained.

That the residents of a Sanctuary might **defend themselves if attacked by an outsider**.

Musashi stopped there and looked up.

"I am going to read that one again," she said.

She read it again. She read it at exactly the same speed, with the same small stumble on the same archaic verb, and did not look at anybody while she did it.

Down on the steps stood sixty people who had heard this clause at festivals and funerals their whole lives. Not one of them had ever heard it read *to an outsider*. They stood in the cold and listened to their own law being said out loud at somebody.

Kaneko-san, who was near the back with the cat inexplicably inside her coat, said afterwards that it was the proudest eleven minutes of her life and that she had not understood a word of the middle section. The cat, she added, had understood even less and had behaved better than the foreigners.

"You have now heard it twice," she said. "Does anyone need it a third time? I am happy to. We have all afternoon and I am already sitting down."

Xanxus did not answer that.

"I am told that in your organisation there is a word for a thing that is said twice on purpose," she went on. "We have never needed one. We simply do it, and then afterwards there is no argument."

"Is that a threat," said Xanxus.

"Do I look like a woman who threatens people? I am seventy-four years old and I cannot get off this cushion without help, and the girl who helps me is at this moment carrying a tray of tea down that road to your men, because I told her to."

"No." She turned the page. "The threat is the next clause, and it is not mine."

And she read the last part, which is the part that four hundred years of nobody touching Namimori had been built on, and which every signatory house in East Asia carries in a box in a storeroom under a north hall:

That an attack upon a Sanctuary is an attack upon the authority of every party to the agreement.

That every party to the agreement is **bound by Flame** to answer it.

That the answer is not optional, and not proportional, and does not require anybody in the Sanctuary to ask for it.

The morning went very quiet. Somewhere below, a Ketsueki shifted her weight. Somewhere above, on a wall, a Cloud did not. Down on the steps a child asked her mother what a Kotei was, in the piercing voice of the very young, and was shushed by four adults at once. Somewhere behind the treeline a bird went off. Ayame, who had been holding the same posture for twenty-two minutes, adjusted her grip on the edge of the cloth.

Mammon asked how many parties there were. It was the first thing they had said all morning that was not a translation, and three of their colleagues turned to look at them, and one did not.

Musashi looked at them for a moment with an expression that was almost kind.

"At the founding," she said, "every major house between here and the Chinese coast. Their successors inherit the bond. Some of those successors are now governments." She closed the box. "Most of them have forgotten us. None of them can *refuse* us. That is what a Flame bond is for."

Levi said that it was absurd, and he was right, and she told him so.

"Yes," Musashi agreed. "It has kept four hundred years of absurdly dangerous people out of a fishing town, so I have made my peace with it."

Xanxus had not moved for some time. *How many houses? Bound how? And why has nobody in Italy ever once mentioned that this exists?*

"Nobody attacked you," he said.

"Nobody has attacked us, no."

"Then we have a problem that does not exist yet."

"You have a *situation* you did not have an hour ago, which is different and more annoying, and I am aware that I have just made your morning considerably worse." Musashi put her hands flat on the table. "I did that deliberately, because I would rather make your morning worse than bury one of mine. Now. About the boy."

"What about him."

"He is not here. He is on a train." She said it plainly, without any attempt to make it sound like a strategy, which is why it landed as one. "He will arrive this evening, because he said the eleventh and he is very tiresome about that sort of thing. You may wait. You may wait *here*, if you like, and somebody will bring you tea, and when he arrives you may ask him yourself and he may tell you no."

"And what happens if the boy says yes?"

"Then he will go with you," said Sawada Musashi, "and I will be extremely unhappy, and the contract will not care, because he is a resident and residents may leave. We are a Sanctuary. We are not a prison."

She looked past him, down the steps, at sixty people who had not been asked to come.

"That is the part outsiders never believe," she said. "Nobody here is keeping him. That is precisely why he stays."

## 5.4

So why did it not end there? A great many people asked that afterwards, and answered it wrongly, because it was never going to end there and Hibari Indra had known that since the previous afternoon.

Her son had been on the shrine road since seven, and she had known he would be there since about four o'clock the previous afternoon, which was when he had stopped arguing.

That was the tell. Hibari Kyouya argued about everything, at length, with a vocabulary he had built specifically for the purpose. When he went quiet and agreeable it meant he had finished considering the matter and had reached a decision that he correctly expected other people to dislike.

He had not come up onto the steps, because he had been told not to by the only person in Namimori whose instructions he treated as data. He had instead positioned himself at the third turn of the road. The wall is high there on one side and the drop is real on the other, and a group coming down in a bad mood would be walking single file.

Indra had watched him choose that spot. She had said nothing, because saying something would have made it a negotiation, and because the boy was not wrong about the ground.

She had simply gone and stood four metres behind him, and stayed there for two hours.

He addressed her without turning round, in the flat register he reserved for people who were already wrong, and told her that she was in his way. She observed that she was standing four metres behind him.

"You are in my *way*," said Hibari Kyouya, with the terrible patience of a nine year old who has explained something twice, "because if I move, you will move first, and then they will look at you instead of at me, and then it will be you and not me."

Yasuragi came off her in a slow spread, the way it always did, and the boy's shoulders came down half a centimetre before he noticed and put them back up.

"Stop that."

"No," said Indra.

"That is *interfering*."

"That is my Flame, in my son's direction, on a road in my husband's territory. What exactly would you like me to call it instead?"

He did not have an answer to that. She had known he would not, which was exactly why she had asked it.

Here is what the contract required, and what every adult on that hill understood and the boy did not care about.

**The town could only defend itself if it was attacked.** Not provoked, and not merely approached… but attacked, first, by an outsider. In a way that could be described in one sentence, to a court of houses that had not thought about Namimori in a century.

And here is what Hibari Kyouya was.

A Getsufuji of eleven, a Cloud by the Italian word. Somebody who had spent four months being told, by the shape of a bond in his chest, that a thing of his had gone out of his territory and come back… and who was now standing above a road with a Storm, a Rain, a Lightning, a Mist and a Sky of the Vongola on the wrong side of his wall.

If he moved first, the town lost the clause.

Indra had explained this to him once, in the dark, the previous night, in eleven words. He had understood it perfectly. He had then explained back to her, in fewer, that he was aware, that he was not going to move first, and that this was, for the avoidance of doubt, the single most difficult thing anybody had ever asked him to do.

She had believed him. She had also gone and stood four metres behind him, because she was his mother and because belief is not a security arrangement.

There is a thing nobody in that family ever said out loud, and it is this: Hibari Indra had spent eleven years being the person who arrives forty minutes after you would like her to, and she had built an entire reputation on it, and there was exactly one person in the world for whom she had never once been late.

At nine forty, the Varia came down the road. They came down in a bad mood, single file, in the order Kyouya had predicted, and the boy did not move.

He did not move when Levi went past.

He did not move when Squalo went past and said something about the wall, loudly, in a language he did not speak, in a tone that needed no translation whatsoever.

He did not move when Xanxus went past without looking at him… which was worse than being looked at, and which Kyouya would remember in detail for a very long time, and which was the single closest that morning came to going the other way.

Belphegor stopped.

Nobody ever established afterwards why. Belphegor's own account varied by audience, and Mammon's was one sentence long and unhelpful.

The likeliest reading is the one Indra gave to the houses, and it is the simplest. The prince had been read to for eleven minutes by an old woman who was not afraid of him. Then he walked down a hill past a boy **two years older than himself** who was also not afraid of him… and something in him required an adjustment to the world.

"Hey," said Belphegor, in English, pleasantly. "Hey. Little thing. Look up."

The boy understood no English at all. He understood tone perfectly.

He threw three knives. He threw them at the boy, and he threw them well, and being the younger of the two had never once slowed him down in his life, and the outcome of that would have been the outcome of that.

Ayame had come down the road behind them at some distance, carrying tea, because Ruriko-sama had said somebody should bring them tea and Ayame was the person to whom things were said.

She was not fast. She had never been fast. What she was, and had been since she was nineteen, was a Ketsueki of the Sawada who had spent thirty years standing slightly in front of things. She was eight metres away, and she was moving before the second knife left his hand.

The first knife went into the wall. The second went through her left forearm and out the other side.

The third stopped four centimetres from the boy's throat and hung there, in the air, on nothing at all.

Down on the shrine steps a woman of seventy-four had put one hand flat on a table. She had been a Gen'ei for fifty-eight years. She had not needed to see the road to know what the road was doing.

Ayame sat down on the stones with her arm in her lap and said, quite clearly, in the tone of a person correcting an invoice:

"That was an attack by an outsider, on a resident, inside the boundary. Three witnesses."

Then she said, "Ow," in exactly the voice she used for dropping a ledger, and fell over.

Somebody screamed. *"Ayame-san! Ayame-san, no!"* It was one of the eleven patrolling children, who had not gone home when told to, and who was going to be a problem for her mother for the next several years and a great asset to this town for the forty after that.

Indra reached her son before he had finished deciding, took him by the back of the collar, and held on.

"*Let go!*"

"No."

"She is *bleeding*, they cut Ayame, let go of my arm…"

"Kyouya." Yasuragi came down over the whole road like a blanket over a fire, and the boy's knees went, and she held him up with one arm and did not let him fall. "You did it. You did not move first. Do you understand what you just bought this town?"

He said something. It was not language, and it was not Japanese, and the only word anywhere near it was the sound a Cloud makes when its territory has been opened.

"Then be angry *afterwards*," said Hibari Seiran, "because the clause is ours now, and if you move it stops being ours, and I am not letting go until you have finished being nine."

And the clock started. Nobody in the Varia felt it. That is the thing worth writing down: four of the five most dangerous people in the Vongola stood on a road in a fishing town and felt absolutely nothing at all.

What happened was this. In a house in Kōbe, an old man who had not thought about Namimori in forty years put down a cup and said a word his grandchildren had never heard him use. In Shanghai, a woman in a meeting stopped mid-sentence and looked at the wall. In three places along the Korean coast, and one in Taipei, and two in the mountains north of Kyoto, something woke up in the chest of a house head.

That Flame bond had been asleep a very long time. It knew exactly which direction it was facing. And it began, without anybody's permission, to pull.

Four hundred years is a long time for a Flame bond to lie quiet.

It had not gone thin.

The houses did not all react the same way, and the differences mattered later. Two of them assumed an error and checked. One of them, in Taipei, had a standing instruction dating from her grandfather and simply began moving without asking anybody anything. The old man in Kōbe spent eleven minutes on the telephone establishing that yes, it was Namimori, and yes, it was real, and then said a second word.

And one house, in the mountains north of Kyoto, did nothing at all for six hours, because its head was eighty-nine and asleep and nobody dared wake her… and when she did wake and was told, she asked only one question.

*"Is the Kohaku alive?"* Told yes, she said that in that case there was no hurry, and went to have breakfast. This is still quoted in three prefectures, usually by people who were not born when it happened.

## 5.5

Mammon felt it. They felt it in the feet, about a second before the sound, the way a building settles.

They turned around on a shrine road with a wounded woman on the stones behind them, a boy who still had not moved, and four colleagues who had no idea… and did the fastest piece of arithmetic of their professional life.

*Bond pull, seven distinct signatures, minimum. Distance: hundreds of kilometres. Time to arrival for the nearest, by air, if it is a house with aircraft, and they will be.*

*Four hours. Possibly three.* *And the clause said not optional and not proportional.*

Then the second number arrived, and it was worse, and it came with a face attached.

Mammon had read the registry printout at two in the morning. It carried a marginal notation in a hand nobody in the Vongola would fail to recognise, entered under a date eighteen months old.

It recorded that an inquiry regarding the Namimori instrument had been made, and answered.

The Vindice knew about this place.

The Vindice had *looked at* this place, recently, and had written it down, which meant that when seven house heads across East Asia began pulling in the same direction at the same moment, there was going to be a very short interval and then there were going to be chains.

"Boss," said Mammon.

"I *know* it's a mess, I have eyes, Belphegor you absolute…"

"Boss." They came down off the air and put their feet on the stones, which they did perhaps twice a decade. "Stop talking and look at me."

Xanxus stopped talking.

"We have between three and four hours," said Mammon, "and then this town is going to be answered for by every Flame house between here and the Chinese coast, under a treaty bond none of them can refuse, and after that the Vindice will arrive, because they have an open file on this place dated eighteen months ago. We are inside the boundary. We struck first. There were three witnesses and one of them wrote it into the air in a complete sentence before she passed out."

Squalo said something that began with "VOOOI" and ended in a question nobody answered. Nobody attended to it.

"Three hours," said Levi. "Boss. We can be at the airfield in ninety minutes if we go now…"

"You can be at the airfield in ninety minutes," said Mammon, "and it will not matter in the slightest, because the bond does not care where you are. It is not a manhunt. It is a *Flame finding*, and Flame does not need an address. They are not coming for you. They are coming because a treaty pulled seven Sky-rank houses out of their chairs, and when they arrive they will do whatever this town asks them to do, and this town has not asked them for anything yet."

There was a pause of a length that Squalo, afterwards, described as the worst four seconds of the year.

"Yet," said Xanxus.

Then, after a moment, without moving: "Say the last part again."

"We struck first, inside the boundary, in front of witnesses."

"Not that part. The other one."

Mammon paused for exactly as long as it takes to decide what a thing is worth.

"The Vindice have a file," they said. "Opened eighteen months ago. Regarding this town."

And that was one thing, and it was true, and it was the thing the boss needed.

Mammon did not mention the second thing.

The marginal notation had been entered in response to an inquiry from *outside* the Vindice. That inquiry had come from within the Sanctuary. Somebody in this fishing town had written to the Warden of Vendicare eighteen months ago… and had been answered.

And Mammon did not mention the third thing, which was still sitting in a column in a ledger in their room at the inn, four helpful arrivals in a line, three of them predating an investigation and one of them predating a massacre.

A complete answer is spent when it is delivered. An incomplete one goes on earning for years, and Mammon had been in this business a very long time, and had priced both.

And underneath the arithmetic, in a place they did not examine at all, there was something that was not an item to be withheld and not a calculation either. Nobody writes to Bermuda von Veckenschtein. Nobody is answered.

Somebody in this fishing town had a channel to the one authority in the world that Mammon had never once been able to buy a hearing from… and eleven years is a long time to have been trying.

"So what is it you actually want me to do," said Xanxus.

"I want you to do the only thing available, which you are not going to enjoy." Mammon looked up the hill, at the steps, where sixty people were standing very still and an old woman was being helped down toward a woman on the stones. "Go back up there and stop the clock."

"And how would I go about stopping a treaty?"

"You cannot. *She* can." A beat. "She has been able to since the moment he threw. She has not done it. Ask yourself why, and then go and be extremely polite."

Xanxus stood on a road in a fishing town for four seconds.

He was seventeen years old, and had been the head of the Vongola's independent squad for eleven months. He had never in his life been made to go back up a hill, and he was aware, with the part of him that would one day run a squad well rather than merely run it, that the alternative was watching four people he had chosen personally get folded into a treaty like laundry.

"Belphegor," he said, without heat, which was worse. "You are going to walk behind me and you are going to say nothing at all."

Then he turned around and went back up the hill.

## 5.6

Tsuna got off the train at six forty in the evening on the eleventh of January, one day and about nine hours later than he had meant to, with a canvas bag and no coat.

Nobody alive would ever hold that against him, because the date he had given his grandmother was the eleventh and he was standing on the eleventh. The tenth was the date he had given himself. He kept two, and only ever missed one of them, and had never once mentioned the second to anybody.

He knew before he reached the ticket gate.

It came at him the way weather does when you step out of a building. It was not the town being frightened, and it was not the town being hurt. It was seven Flame bonds lying across his home like cable, pulled taut, from directions that had no business pointing here… and the whole of Namimori sitting underneath them, going quietly about its evening.

"Oh no," he said, out loud, at the ticket gate, to a fox in a bag. "Oh, no, no, no."

He ran. He was seven, and he had been on trains and ferries for thirty-one hours, and he ran up that hill like something was on fire, which in a sense that had no smoke in it was exactly right.

He ran up the shrine road in the dark, past the third turn where somebody had washed the stones and not quite got it all, past a wall with a knife hole in it that somebody had already marked with a paper tag for the assessors, and he was most of the way up before his brain caught up with his legs and told him the thing he had been refusing to hear for ninety seconds.

*It is over.* There was no fighting. There was no cordon. There was a light on in the shrine office and the smell of somebody's dinner, and at the top of the steps, sitting on a cushion behind a low table with a box on it, his grandmother was drinking tea with the leader of the Varia.

They were not talking. They had, from the look of the pot, been not talking for some time.

Xanxus glanced at him once, took in the height and the bag and the state of his shoes, and went back to his tea.

Down at the bottom of the steps, three of the four he brought with him sat on the stone edging in the dark, not talking, the way people sit when they have been told to wait outside. The tall one rested his knees up under his chin. The small one was asleep against his shoulder, and nobody seemed willing to move him.

Tsuna looked at that for slightly longer than he meant to.

"You are late," said Musashi.

"The ferry…" He stopped. He put the bag down. He looked at the table, and the box on it, and understood what the box was, and that it had been open, and roughly what that meant.

"Ayame," he said. "Where is Ayame, is she…"

"Through and out. Nothing important cut. She has been intolerable about it for nine hours and has told four separate people that she got it in writing." Musashi turned her cup. "She asked me to tell you it was not your fault, and I said I would, and I am now discharging that obligation and adding that she is wrong and so are you."

Tsuna sat down on the step. He had not been invited to.

"Where is she now?"

"In her own bed, at the back of the house, with Tomoko-sensei and an opinion." Musashi refilled the pot without hurrying. "You may see her in the morning. You may not see her tonight, because it is nearly seven and she has finally stopped talking, and I am not having you undo that."

"Grandmother, please…"

"In the *morning*, Tenmei."

She only used that name when she wanted a thing to stop, and it stopped.

The seven lines were still there, humming somewhere above the treeline. He could feel the nearest one now that he was under it, a heavy old thing coming down out of the north-east, and if he put any attention on it at all he could feel the shape of the person on the other end, who was eighty-something and furious and already in the air.

"How long have the bonds been pulled?" he said.

"Nine hours, near enough."

"And nobody has released them…"

"No," said Musashi. "Because I have not released it, and I will not release it until I am satisfied, and everyone concerned understands that I am an old woman who is enjoying this."

Across the table, Xanxus made a sound that in a less dangerous man would have been a laugh.

Here is what Tsuna understood, sitting on a cold step with his heart going, and what he would still be thinking about at nineteen:

It had happened without him. All of it. The warning, the box, the reading, the eleven minutes, the clause said twice, a knife stopped four centimetres from a boy's throat by a woman who had not needed to see the road, and a Storm who had put her arm where the knife was going to be and then narrated the legal position of it while bleeding.

He had not planned one line of it. He had not been consulted. He had been on a train.

And the town had not fallen over.

He sat with that, on a cold step, in a coat he did not have.

It was not relief. Relief he knew; relief was a thing that arrived in the chest and left again. This sat somewhere lower down and did not move, and it had the unpleasant quality of a number coming out wrong after the money is already spent.

*What was I for, then,* he thought, *this whole time?*

He made himself do the honest version of the arithmetic, because he always did, and because the dishonest version was standing right there being much more comfortable.

The honest version: **the town had not been saved by the town alone.** It had been saved by an instrument four hundred years old, by a woman who had spent fifty-eight years being a Mist, by a Storm who put an arm where a knife was going, and by a nine year old Cloud who did the single hardest thing in his short life, which was *nothing*, for two hours, on a wall above a road.

None of that was luck. All of it was somebody having prepared.

*So they do plan,* he thought. *They have been planning for four hundred years. I have been planning for four.* And under that, the thing he could not get round: **not one of those preparations had needed him to be awake for it.**

And underneath that, quieter and much worse: *and if they did not need me for this… what have I been doing every morning since I was four?*

*How many of the five hundred and sixty seven assume I am the only one moving?* he thought. *How many of them are just… vanity with a filing system?*

*I built five hundred and sixty seven plans,* he thought, with a lurch that had nothing in it he could name, *on the assumption that if I did not do a thing, the thing would not get done.*

"Sawada."

He looked up.

He had not given that name. He had stood on a platform in Liguria and declined to give that exact name, and the man had let him decline, and had then gone and collected it somewhere else, off a town that says it out loud forty times a day without thinking about it… and had held on to it until now.

*That is not laziness,* Tsuna thought. *That is a receipt.*

Xanxus was watching him over the cup with an expression that had absolutely nothing friendly in it.

"You're the Cloud."

"I am the Cloud, yes."

"I came here to take you."

"I know why you came." Tsuna kept his voice level, which took most of what he had left. "Are you still planning to?"

"Your grandmother," said Xanxus, "read me a document."

"I heard."

"She read it twice." He set the cup down. "You're seven. You're a Cloud, and you're seven, and there is a treaty over this town that goes off if anybody breathes wrong, and you went to Italy alone and came back and said nothing to anybody." A pause. "I am going to want to know what you are, eventually."

"That is completely fair, and I would want to know too."

"I am not going to find out today."

"No," Tsuna agreed. "Today you're going to sign a ceasefire with a shrine, and then you're going to get on a plane before a very old man from Kōbe lands on this town, and then in about three years you're going to remember this conversation in a very bad moment and it is going to annoy you."

There was a silence, and it ran about two seconds too long, and Tsuna heard the shape of it and knew precisely what he had done.

He had been awake for thirty-one hours. He had spent the last ninety seconds being undone by a cup of tea. And somewhere in the gap between those two facts a sentence had walked out of him with a **date** in it.

Xanxus did not ask. That was the part that went cold. A man who merely wanted to know would have asked, and would have asked badly, and Tsuna had spent two lifetimes handling men who asked badly. Xanxus put the cup down instead, very carefully, like a man setting something on a shelf he means to come back to.

*He said eventually,* Tsuna thought. *I have just handed eventually a calendar.*

"Grandmother," he said, without looking away, "am I allowed to talk like that?"

"You are seven years old and you have been home for eleven minutes."

"Is that a no?"

"It is a *not in front of guests*," said Musashi, and poured him a cup.

The cup was the thing that undid him. He had not been expecting it, and he had to look at the steps for a while, and the fox in the canvas bag by his knee did not comment, which was the only mercy available.

She had come out of a storeroom with a four hundred year old instrument, read it twice to an assassination squad, stopped a knife at four centimetres from a boy's throat without turning her head, held seven house bonds pulled taut over her own town for nine hours because she was *enjoying it*… and she had still, at some point in the last ninety seconds, boiled water.

"Thank you," he said, to the cup. "For all of it. Is that allowed, or is that also not in front of guests?"

"Mm," said his grandmother.

---

The instrument of ceasefire was signed at nine that night, and it was witnessed, and because it was witnessed it had to be entered, and because it had to be entered it went into the registry, and the registry is not a private thing.

That is how a name travels. It went first to the seven houses. They stood down, were furious about it, and filed their own copies. From there it went into the prefectural record. And from there, because somebody in Kōbe wanted it on the books where the mafia could see it, into the ledger the Italian side keeps of incidents involving the independent squads.

And in that ledger, under a Monday in January, somebody wrote a name that had never been in it before.

The column was the one reserved for the party on whose behalf a Sanctuary invokes its bond.

Four months later, in a city on the other side of the world, a man who was not a man and who read everything put a finger on that line and stopped.

He read it twice, which was unusual for him, and which he would not have been able to explain if asked.

The line said very little. A Sanctuary in Mie had invoked its bond. Seven houses had answered and stood down. An independent squad had signed an instrument of ceasefire on the steps of a shrine, which was embarrassing, and which was going to be quietly funny in certain rooms in Palermo for about a year.

And in the column for the party on whose behalf the bond was invoked, a name, and after the name a title he did not recognise, and after the title an age.

**Seven.**

Then he went on to the next page, because the boy in question was seven years old and there was nothing to be done about a seven year old for another eleven years, and Reborn had a great deal on.

But he remembered the name.
