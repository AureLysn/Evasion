# Chapter 6: Local Carnivore Concludes The Small Animal Owns Nothing. Corrects This

Hibari Kyouya had been counting since he was four and the number was two hundred and eleven.

Two hundred and eleven was how many days it would take, at his current rate, to reach the point at which his mother would go. He worked the rate out himself, from the only data available, which was how long it took her to stop correcting a form once he learned it. He checked the arithmetic every month. The number came down from four thousand and something with a regularity that was the single most reassuring thing in his life.

He was not counting because he wanted her to go. That was a thing adults assumed and he had stopped bothering to correct.

He was counting because she had told him she would.

She said it when he was four, in the dojo, in the tone she used for facts. Namimori was not her territory. She came here for his father and stayed for him. And when he was strong enough to hold his own ground, she would go and find hers. She said it once. She did not soften it and she did not repeat it. Kyouya understood immediately that this was the greatest compliment anybody would ever pay him, because she told him the truth at four years old and did not check whether he could carry it.

So he counted. Two hundred and eleven days, and then his mother would leave, and he would hold Namimori, and that was the shape of the world and it had been the shape of the world for two years.

Then she knelt in the snow to somebody else.

  

Kyouya's world had four kinds of thing in it and the categories were not negotiable.

There were **carnivores**. Small number, and Flame had almost nothing to do with it, which had taken him a year to accept. His mother. Old Sawada, a *Gen'ei* who had never hit anybody in her life and who was the most dangerous person in the district and who he respected accordingly. A *Ketsueki* in Kobe he had seen once, from a distance, for about four seconds. Himself, eventually, at his current rate, in a little under a year.

There were **herbivores**, which was most people, and which was not an insult. Kyouya had read the books. A herd is not weak, and a herd is not Flame-negative either, which is a thing outsiders assume and which is wrong in Namimori by a wide margin. A herd has more mass than anything hunting it. It is very difficult to move. It will kill you if you get it wrong. Namimori is a herd of sixty thousand, and Kyouya had spent his whole life learning how it stood.

There were **scavengers**, which was the only category that carried contempt, and which contained everybody who came to this town looking for something that was not theirs.

And there was the small animal, who was in none of the three, and who had been an open file for two years.

  

He tried *prey* first, at four, and discarded it inside a week. Prey runs. The small animal did not run. Kyouya tested it properly, by coming over the wall at speed with a length of pipe, and it did not run then either. It turned round. It looked at him. And then, and this was the part he thought about for eleven months, it *stepped sideways*. Once. Precisely. So that the wisteria was between them. And waited to see what he did next.

Nothing that ran did that. Running is a decision you make with your legs. That had been made with the ground.

He tried *carnivore* next and it did not fit either, and the reason took him until he was five and a half to be able to say.

A carnivore holds ground. That is the whole of it. You take a territory. You mark it. You keep it. Everything you have is inside the line, and everything inside the line is yours.

The small animal did not have a line.

  

He established this over two years, by observation, because he did not ask questions of anybody about anything that mattered.

The shrine was not his. It was the shrine's. He served it. Old Sawada held it, and he would hold it after her as a caretaker holds a building.

The town was not his. He said so out loud. Kyouya had heard him say it four times, in four different rooms, to four different sets of adults who all went soft in the face when he did.

The plans were not his. Kyouya had never seen the book. He knew it existed and he knew roughly how thick it was. He had watched the small animal write in it for two years and had not once seen him look pleased about anything he wrote.

The *Yasuragi* was not his either, although the *Yasuragi* had knelt, and Kyouya had a great deal to work out about that.

Even the *fox* was not his. Kyouya had asked directly, which he did about animals, and had been told: *nothing. It doesn't work, it doesn't guard, it won't do a single thing anyone asks it to.*

So the strongest thing in Namimori owned nothing at all. This was not modesty. Kyouya could recognise modesty, and found it irritating, and there was none here. It was structural. The small animal had built a life in which every single object had somebody else's name on it.

Kyouya, at six, in a family that had held one gate for four centuries, looked at that and felt something he had no word for. The nearest was *vertigo*.

  

His mother knelt in the snow at the end of the winter and Kyouya was there and did not move a muscle.

He understood what he was watching. A *Yasuragi* of the main line going down in front of a *Kohaku* who was five. Nobody had explained the old form to him because nobody needed to. He found it in the house records at five, read it eleven times, and privately decided it was the only piece of paperwork in the world worth the ink.

She laid the old blade across her open hands. She said the words. The small animal, who was five, and who was wearing a coat that did not fit and had snow in his hair, put his hand on the flat of it.

And two hundred and eleven days stopped meaning anything.

  

Kyouya went home and sat on the roof for four hours in the cold and worked it out, and what he worked out was this:

She was not going.

Not *not yet*. Not *later*. She had put herself under another house's roof, in the old form, in front of witnesses. The old form has no way out of it that is not disgrace. His mother would sooner be killed than be a disgrace.

Therefore his mother was going to be in Namimori until she died.

He had spent two years counting down to a thing that was now never going to happen.

The correct emotion was relief. He established that fairly quickly and then set it aside, because it was not the emotion he was having.

The emotion he was having was that the countdown had been *his*. It was the one number in his life he had worked out himself, from data, without being told. The getting of it had been the whole shape of his childhood. Two hundred and eleven days. He had checked it that morning.

And somebody had reached into it and taken the bottom out, and it had not even been about him.

  

"You are angry," said Kusakabe Tetsuya, four days later, on the wall.

"I am not angry."

"You have not hit anything for four days."

"That is not a symptom."

"It is a symptom in *you*," said Tetsuya, Tetsuya was six. He had been the only sane person in Kyouya's orbit since they were three, and he was the reason Kyouya had never been actively dangerous to anybody smaller than himself. "I have counted. Four days. Last time it was nine days and your father had died."

Kyouya sat on the wall and looked at the Sawada roofline in the distance and did not say anything for a while.

"The number is gone," he said.

"…Which number?"

"The number."

Tetsuya thought about it, and got there, because he always got there, four seconds after everybody else and correctly.

"Ah," he said. "Because she is staying."

"Because she is staying."

"Is that bad?"

"No," said Kyouya, and discovered as he said it that this was true, and that the truth of it made the whole thing considerably worse rather than better. "It is not bad. It is *not mine*."

  

Here is what he did about it, over the following four months, and none of it was discussed with anybody.

He recalculated. Obviously. The threshold had been *strong enough to hold Namimori alone*. The threshold was now nothing at all. His mother was going to be standing on the east gate for another forty years, and the ground would not be his in any meaningful sense until he was middle-aged.

He hated that. He wrote it down, in the notebook he kept and which nobody had ever seen, in the hand of a small boy with excellent penmanship: **the ground is not available.**

Then he sat with it for six weeks and found the second thing, which was better.

If the ground was not available, then the *herd* was. Nobody held that. His mother held a gate. Old Sawada held a shrine. The small animal held the *Kotei* vault. Not one of them held the sixty thousand people underneath it in the sense Kyouya meant, which was knowing where they all were, what they were doing, and which of them was about to become a problem.

Nobody was doing that job. It was the largest unclaimed territory in the district and it had been sitting there his entire life.

He started the following Monday. He was six. It took him eleven years and by the end of it people called it the Disciplinary Committee and thought he had invented it out of a personality disorder.

  

The first thing he did with the herd was catch a scavenger, and it went badly, and he learned more from it than from anything else that year.

The man had been in Namimori nine days, and no house in the district had claimed him, and no *Chiyu* had treated him, which are the two ways a stranger normally surfaces here. Kyouya knew because Kyouya had started keeping a list on the Monday and the man was on it by Wednesday: mid-thirties, no house, no Flame that anybody could read, staying above the noodle place on Shibata street and asking about property.

"He is asking about *property*," Kyouya told Tetsuya.

"People do ask about property."

"Nobody asks about property four streets from a shrine with a *Kotei* over it. They ask about property where property is cheap." Kyouya was drawing on the wall in chalk, which he did, and which Tetsuya had given up objecting to. "Nine days. Four estate agents. He has not looked at a single building twice."

"So he is not buying."

"So he is *counting*," said Kyouya.

He went in on the eleventh day, alone, at two in the afternoon, which was a mistake, and he had worked out precisely which mistake it was inside about four seconds.

The man was a *Ketsueki*. A weak one, badly trained, from somewhere that did not teach Flame to children, and it made no difference whatsoever, because he was thirty-four years old and eighty kilos and Kyouya was six.

Kyouya got a broken finger, a cracked rib, and out of the room. He got out because the man did not want a dead child in a shrine town and not because Kyouya made him let go, and he knew the difference immediately, and it was the single most useful piece of information he acquired before he was ten.

  

His mother set the finger herself, in the kitchen, with a face like weather.

"Well?"

"He is a *Ketsueki*. Untrained. Naples or somewhere like it, the accent is wrong for Sicily." Kyouya held still, because holding still was free. "He is counting buildings. He has been at it eleven days and he is halfway up the east side."

"You went alone."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because I did not know he was a *Ketsueki* until I was in the room, and because I did not check, and because I have been telling myself for two years that I am not far off." He looked at the table. "I am extremely far off."

Indra finished the splint and did not say anything for a moment.

"That is the correct conclusion," she said. "I am going to make it worse. Do you want it now or in a week?"

"Now."

"You were not beaten by a strong man. You were beaten by an *average* one." She let that sit exactly as long as it needed. "There are perhaps four hundred men in this country who could do what he did to you this afternoon, and most of them are nobody, and you are going to meet a great many of them before you are twenty."

"…Hn."

"Do you want to know the useful part?"

"Yes."

"He stopped," said Indra. "He had you and he stopped, because he is in a town he is frightened of and he does not know why he is frightened of it, and the reason he does not know is that nobody has told him there is a *Kotei* vault over his head. A *Yasuragi* would have felt it in a day. He is a *Ketsueki* and he will never work it out." She stood up. "That is the town working. You were not saved by your own strength today, Kyouya, and you are going to be tempted to be ashamed about that, and it would be a waste."

  

He took Tetsuya and Kojima and the Fuwa twins the next time, and it took eleven minutes and nobody got hurt, and the man was on a train by the evening.

That was the Monday he started the list properly.

It is worth recording what the list actually was, because in eleven years people would attach a great deal of nonsense to it. It was a school exercise book. It had names, and where they lived, and what Flame they carried if any, and it had a column at the right-hand side that Kyouya had labelled with a single character and which nobody else ever understood, and which meant: *would this person be missed*.

He filled four pages the first month. Names, streets, trades, Flame. Eleven *Ketsueki*, six *Chiyu*, four *Hogo*, one *Getsufuji* who turned out to be visiting his mother. By the spring he had two hundred names and had learned that the Torii *Chiyu* line saw more of this town in a week than the shrine saw in a year, and had started, with enormous reluctance, to talk to people.

  

The courtship was a separate file and it had been open since he was four.

Nobody had taught him this either, which was becoming a theme. The Hibari have done it the same way for a long time. Three gifts, escalating, presented without comment. Then a written petition to the head of the other house. The Hibari consider the petition to be the romantic part, and everybody else considers this to be evidence of something.

His mother received a letter at nineteen that listed her faults. She married the man. Kyouya found the letter at five, in a box, read it four times, and regarded it as the family's finest surviving document.

The first gift had been a rabbit, at four. Correct and unremarkable.

The second gift was a bird, at six. Kyouya spent two months on it. He presented it to a room containing nine people, one of whom was not a person, and the small animal said *thank you* in the voice of somebody who had been thanking people for dead animals for some months and had got good at it.

That bothered him. It bothered him for four days, and then he understood why, and the understanding was the beginning of the whole thing.

**The gifts were not landing.**

Not because they were unwelcome. Because they were *food*. A gift of food says *I can feed you*. The small animal did not need feeding, had never needed feeding, and had in fact spent six years feeding an entire district.

Kyouya had been offering the wrong currency for two years. It was like offering money to somebody who has no use for money and is too polite to say so.

  

He put the third gift down for eleven months, which was the longest he had ever deliberately not done something.

Tetsuya asked about it twice and got nothing. His mother asked once, obliquely, over dinner, and got less. Old Sawada, who was the only person in the district Kyouya was straightforwardly afraid of, asked him directly in her own hallway in the spring.

"There has not been a third."

"No."

"May I ask why?"

"No," said Kyouya, and then, because it was her, and because she had never once talked to him as though he were six, "the first two were wrong."

Old Sawada looked at him for a moment.

"Yes," she said. "They were."

"You knew."

"I have known since the rabbit."

"Then why did you not say?"

"Because you would have got there," said Old Sawada, "and you would have got there better than I could have told you, and because I have spent sixty-three years watching people be handed answers they had not finished the question for." She started walking again. "Also I wanted to see how long it would take. Eleven months. I had money on fourteen."

"You had *money* on it?"

"Azuma has money on nine and is going to be extremely annoyed."

  

What he worked out, in the eleven months, he worked out in three pieces.

The first piece was the fox.

Kyouya had asked what it did and had been told *nothing*, and had said *good*, and had meant it, and had then spent four months noticing that he had been the only person in that house who had said *good*. Everybody else in that building had been trying, in their own way, to find the animal a job. The apprentice had a list. The Storm woman had a schedule. The small animal himself had made three separate attempts and had lost his temper on the third, which Kyouya had heard about from Tetsuya, who had heard about it from the gardener, and which had been the single most interesting piece of information to reach him that year.

They could not leave it alone. A thing with no use, in that house, was an *open item*.

And the small animal had gone on keeping it anyway. He had failed to give it a job and had kept it, and had written it into no plan, and had said out loud to a six-year-old apprentice that it was here because it wanted to be.

**So he could do it.** He could keep a thing for no reason. He had proved it, once, with a fox.

He simply could not do it with himself.

  

The second piece was the field.

There is a piece of ground on the north slope above the shrine that is nine hundred square metres and belongs to nobody. Kyouya established this properly, which for a Hibari means going into the house records, the district register, and the shrine's own land roll… and finding all three disagreeing.

It was Sawada land four centuries ago and got signed over to a branch family who died out in the Meiji period. The district never took it up, because a shrine's old ground is nobody's business. The shrine never reclaimed it, because Old Sawada's grandfather had an argument about it with somebody, refused on principle for nineteen years, and then died.

It was useless. That was the point of it and it took Kyouya four months to understand that this was the point of it. Too steep for building. Wrong aspect for the winter trees, which the gardener confirmed at length without being asked twice. No water. No road. Nine hundred square metres of north slope with three rocks on it grass, and one very old plum tree that nobody planted.

Nobody could use it for anything. Nobody had, for a hundred and forty years.

  

The third piece was the hardest and it broke something of his.

Nine hundred square metres on the north slope is not defensible by a six-year-old. Kyouya did the survey himself over eleven days. He was thorough, and he did not flinch from the conclusion. The ground had three approaches, two of them outside the shrine's wards. Holding it would require four people, minimum.

He had four people. That was not the problem.

The problem was that he had been going to wait.

That was in the notebook, and it had been in the notebook since he was five, in the same hand: **do not take ground you cannot hold alone.** It was the first rule he had written for himself and he had derived it from watching two families in this district lose everything by overreaching, and it was correct, and he believed it, and he had intended to wait until he was eleven or twelve.

He sat on the north slope in the rain in the eleventh month and looked at nine hundred square metres of useless grass and understood that he was going to break his own rule.

Not because the rule was wrong. Because the small animal was six. He had not slept properly in three years. A physician from India had asked him three questions and got no answer to the third. And he was going to spend the next five years being managed within an inch of his life by six people who adored him, and who all, every one of them, needed something from him.

*He does not have five years to wait for me to be eleven.*

Kyouya crossed out **do not take ground you cannot hold alone** and wrote underneath it, without any softening, because he did not soften things even for himself:

**I am not strong enough. Doing it anyway. Tetsuya, Kojima, the Fuwa twins.**

Then he went and asked them, which was the second thing he had never done before, and all four said yes inside a minute, and Tetsuya said yes before Kyouya had finished the sentence and then had the decency not to look pleased about it.

  

Getting the signature took forty minutes and he had not exaggerated about it.

He went to the dojo, because that was where she was on a Tuesday evening, and he waited until she finished, because interrupting her was a thing he had done exactly once at the age of three.

"I need your signature."

"On what?"

"A land transfer."

Indra put the sword away and turned round properly, which she did about four times a year.

"Whose land?"

"Ours. The north slope parcel, the one above the shrine, the nine hundred square metres that has been in dispute since the Meiji period." He had it in his hand and did not offer it yet. "It came to us in 1911 through the Kuroki settlement and nobody has ever done anything with it and the register is a mess. I have had it cleaned up."

"You have had it *cleaned up*."

"There was a solicitor in Yokkaichi, a *Hogo*, retired. He asked four questions and then he stopped asking questions, which I paid extra for."

"You *paid* him?"

"I have money," said Kyouya. "I have had money since I was five. You gave it to me."

His mother folded down onto the floor, which she never did in the dojo, and looked at her son for what felt to him like an extremely long time.

"To whom is it being transferred?"

"Sawada Tsunayoshi. Personally. Grandmother as trustee until he is twenty. The shrine explicitly excluded."

"Kyouya. That is Hibari ground."

"Yes."

"It has been Hibari ground for a hundred and fourteen years."

"It has been *nothing* for a hundred and fourteen years!" It came out louder than he intended and he did not take it back. "Nobody has walked on it. Nobody has cut it. No *Chiyu* has ever been asked to do anything to it. There is a plum tree up there that we did not plant and did not know about, and I found it because I went and looked, and none of you have been up there in my lifetime or yours."

  

She did not answer that for a while.

"Why?" she said.

"That is the fourth time somebody has asked me that this month and I am not going to get better at answering it."

"Try."

Kyouya stood in his mother's dojo with a land transfer in his hand and discovered that he did not have a sentence, and that the absence of the sentence was not the same as the absence of the reason, and that this was going to be a problem for the rest of his life.

"He does not own anything," he said.

"He owns a…"

"He does not own *anything*." He heard his own voice do something and stopped, and started again flatter. "I have been checking for two years. Everything in that house has somebody else's name on it and he arranged that himself and nobody has noticed, and if I wait until I am eleven he will have arranged four more years of it and it will be harder to undo."

"So you are giving him ground."

"I am giving him ground that cannot be used for anything," said Kyouya, "because if it could be used he would find the use and give it away by the spring."

Indra put her hand out for the paper.

She read it. She read the fourth paragraph twice, which everybody did, and then she got up and went to the box where the seal was kept and signed it against the wall standing up, which is not how a head of house signs anything.

"There," she said. "Kyouya."

"Mm."

"You will not tell anybody I did this without an argument."

"No."

"Good, because I am going to spend eleven years pretending I made you justify it." She handed it back. "Also I want you to know that I looked at that parcel when I was twenty-six and decided it was worthless, and I was correct, and you have just done something with it that I would not have thought of in forty years."

  

He presented the third gift on an ordinary Thursday in the autumn with no ceremony whatsoever, because ceremony was for the petition and the gifts were supposed to be silent.

He came in over the east wall, which was the only door he acknowledged. He walked up to the engawa where the small animal was sitting with ledgers and a fox and a cup of tea that had gone cold in the way that woman was always complaining about. He put a piece of paper down on the boards.

It was a land transfer. He had it drawn up by a solicitor in Yokkaichi who asked no questions after the first four. It was correct in every particular. It was signed by Hibari Indra as head of house, because a child cannot convey land and Kyouya had had to go and ask his mother for a signature, which had been the worst forty minutes of his life to date and about which he would say nothing to anybody ever.

The small animal read it.

He read it twice. Kyouya watched him do it, and watched his face do nothing at all, and then watched him go back and read the third paragraph a third time.

"This is the north slope."

"Yes."

"This is nine hundred square metres of the north slope, transferred to me. Not to the shrine. To *me*, personally, as a natural person, with the shrine explicitly excluded." He looked up. "Kyouya, I am six. I cannot hold title."

"You can hold it in trust with your grandmother as trustee until you are twenty. It is in the fourth paragraph. The solicitor said it four times."

"…So it is." The small animal read the fourth paragraph. "Why?"

  

Kyouya had prepared for this and had thrown the preparation away that morning, because it had read, on the page, like something a herbivore would say.

"You do not own anything," he said.

"I own a great deal. I have a house. I have a whole…"

"You have a *house* that is the shrine's. You have a town that you tell people is not yours. Four times! I counted. You have a fox that you tried to give a job to three times." Kyouya did not raise his voice, because he never did, and because the flatness was the point. "You have five hundred and something plans and every one of them is for somebody else." He did not raise his voice, because he never did. "I have been looking for two years. There is nothing in your life that is yours."

The small animal had gone very still.

"So." Kyouya put one finger on the paper. "This is yours. It has no water and no road and it is the wrong aspect for anything and it is too steep to build on. You cannot use it. Nobody can use it. There is a plum tree on it that nobody planted." He straightened. "You are not permitted to give it to the shrine. You are not permitted to put it in a plan. If you find a use for it I will take it back."

There was a silence of a length that Kyouya had not budgeted for.

"Kyouya."

"Mm."

"Are you aware," said the small animal, in a voice that had gone strange, "that you have just given me a fox?"

  

Kyouya did not understand that for about nine seconds and then he did, and he was extremely annoyed about it, and it did not change anything.

"I am aware that it is *useless*!" he said. "That is the entire point. Do not make it clever."

"I am not making it clever. I am telling you that you are the second person to do this and the first one was a man who is not a person, and neither of you explained it, and I have spent four months failing to understand why the fox helped and I think I have just…"

He stopped.

Kyouya waited, because he had learned in two years that the small animal stopped talking at precisely the moments something real was happening and that filling the gap was how the adults in that house lost their information.

"I have never had a thing that was for nothing," said the small animal.

"I know," said Kyouya. "I have been watching you not have one for two years. It is *extremely irritating*!"

  

He did the petition eleven days later and he did it properly.

Formal clothes, which he loathed. The right hour. The right door, which was the north one and not the one everybody used. A letter in his own hand, four drafts, listing the reasons a Hibari of the main line sought a formal understanding with the Sawada house. Listed in the family manner, which is to say that the third paragraph was a list of the other party's faults.

He put *undervalues himself to a degree that constitutes a structural risk to this district* as fault number one.

He put *does not sleep* as fault number two, on the authority of a physician's report he was not supposed to have seen.

He put *will not stop being useful* as fault number three, and then read it back and did not change a word of it, and sealed it.

Old Sawada received him in the small hall in full formal dress with Azuma Maro sitting behind her as witness, which was more than the occasion required and which he understood, correctly, to be a courtesy.

She read the letter.

She read it a second time. When she got to the third paragraph her face did something she did not permit it to finish. Azuma Maro made a noise behind her and was ignored.

"Hibari Kyouya of the main line," said Old Sawada. "The Sawada house declines."

"I know."

"On the grounds that both parties are six years old."

"I know."

"And that the petition may be re-presented."

"Annually," said Kyouya. "I have written the dates in the back. There are fourteen of them."

Old Sawada looked at the back of the letter, where he had in fact written fourteen dates. She put it down. Then she pressed her fingers against her eyes for a moment, in a way he had never seen her do.

"Fourteen," she said.

"You are sixty-three. I have allowed for eighty-two, which is the average for the women of your line over the last two hundred years, and I have added three because you are annoyed more than they were and it appears to be preservative."

Azuma Maro laughed so hard she had to be helped out of the room.

  

His mother was waiting for him outside and had clearly been there some time.

"Well?"

"Declined."

"Naturally."

"Annually," said Kyouya.

"Of course annually." She fell in beside him and they went down the shrine steps together. They had done that perhaps four hundred times. He had never once found it awkward until this year. "Kyouya."

"Mm."

"You asked me for a signature."

"Yes."

"You have never asked me for anything," said his mother, "and I want to be careful with this, so I am going to say one thing and then I am going to stop." She looked straight ahead. "The rule you crossed out was a good rule. I saw the notebook. I was not looking for it and I am not going to apologise."

Kyouya kept walking.

"It was correct at five and it was correct at six and it will be correct again at twenty," she said, "and you broke it on purpose, for a reason you could state, having done the survey first. That is not the same thing as being reckless. It took me until I was twenty-six to learn the difference and I learned it badly and it cost me a great deal."

"…Hn."

"I am also aware," she said, "that you have been counting down to my departure since you were four, and that I ended it in the snow without warning you, and that it took something from you that was yours."

Kyouya stopped on the steps.

"You knew about the number?"

"You checked it on the last day of every month for two years. In the dojo. On the same board. And you thought you rubbed it out," said Indra. "I am your mother and I am a *Yasuragi*. I have known about the number for eighteen months and I have never once mentioned it." She stopped walking. "I am mentioning it now because you have just given away nine hundred square metres of the north slope to a child who owns nothing. And I would like you to know that I noticed what it cost, and what it was for."

  

They stood on the steps for a while.

"I am not sorry," said Indra.

"I did not ask you to be."

"No. You have not asked me for one thing in six years except a signature, which is a problem I intend to work on and which you are going to find extremely unpleasant." She started down again. "I stayed because I found something worth being still for. That is all it was. It was not about you and it was not against you, and if I had waited to explain it properly I would never have knelt, and there was a great deal riding on my kneeling that day that I am not going to discuss with a boy of six."

"Seven in the spring."

"Seven in the spring," she agreed. "Fine. I will discuss it with you when you are ten."

"Nine."

"Ten, and if you push me to eleven I will take the dojo boards up."

  

He went up the north slope that evening, on his own, in the last of the light, because he wanted to see it belonging to somebody.

It looked exactly the same. That was correct and he had expected it and he was nevertheless slightly disappointed, which he noted and dismissed.

Nine hundred square metres of grass on the wrong aspect. Three rocks, one of which a *Gen'ei* would have moved and Old Sawada never had. A plum tree nobody planted, which the *Chiyu* gardener said was probably a bird's fault about a century ago, and which was going to outlive all of them.

Kyouya walked the boundary. He did it properly, in the old way, along all three approaches. He worked out for the fourth time where the four of them would have to stand, and how bad the north-east corner was.

Then he sat down on the largest rock and looked down at the town.

The *Kotei* vault was up. It always was. Sixty thousand people under a *Kotei* ceiling that four hundred of them knew about. A herd he was going to spend the next eleven years learning to hold. And somewhere down there a small boy with a fox in his collar who had, as of eleven days ago, one useless field.

*Small animal,* Kyouya thought, in the flat internal voice he used for classifications, *you are not a keystone species.*

He had thought that for a year and a half and had been rather pleased with it. A keystone species holds a system up. Take it away and everything collapses. That had seemed exactly right, and it was wrong in a way that took him two years and a piece of paper to see.

A keystone is a *function*. It is a thing defined entirely by what happens without it.

He got up off the rock and brushed his coat off and started down.

*You are a carnivore,* he thought. *You have been a carnivore the entire time and nobody in that house has noticed, including you, and you have no ground, and I have given you some, and you are going to be extremely bad at owning it.*

  

The classification problem came back in the winter and he settled it on the north slope in the snow.

He had been carrying four categories since he was four and the small animal had been outside all of them for two years, and Kyouya had a rule about that too: a taxonomy with a permanent exception is a broken taxonomy. Either you find the box, or you rebuild the boxes.

He went up on a Sunday to think about it and found the small animal already there, sitting on the largest rock with a fox in his collar, in a coat that fitted this time, with the *Kotei* going gold over the whole valley behind him.

Kyouya did not say anything. He sat down on the second rock, at two and a half paces, and they looked at the town for about eleven minutes.

"You are working on something," said the small animal.

"Yes."

"Is it me?"

"Yes."

"Is it the classification, or is it the ground?"

"Both. Do you want the ground first?"

"…No. Do the classification. I have been waiting two years for it and I have theories."

"Ah." He sounded, of all things, pleased. "Would it help to have it out loud? I am told I am useful for that. Grandmother says I am the only person in the house who does not fill a silence, and she says it as a complaint."

  

"You are not a keystone species," said Kyouya.

"…That is what I have been demoted to?"

"It is not a demotion, it is a *correction*, and you are going to have to stop doing that or I will stop telling you things."

"Sorry. Go on."

"A keystone species is a function." Kyouya kept his eyes on the town, because it was easier and because the *Kotei* vault was doing the thing it did in low sun. "You take it out and the system falls over. That is all it is. It has no properties of its own. It is defined entirely by the hole it leaves."

The small animal did not say anything.

"That is what everybody in that house thinks you are," said Kyouya. "The Storm woman. The gardener. The apprentice. My mother, who would deny it. All of them are extremely fond of you and every single one of them is describing a *hole they do not want*."

"…That is a very unpleasant thing to be told on a Sunday."

"Yes." Kyouya turned his head. "It is also wrong, which is what I came up here to establish."

  

"A carnivore holds ground," he said. "You have ground now. I gave it to you and you have not given it away, which I checked with the solicitor in November, and which I want you to know I checked."

"You *checked*?"

"Of course I checked! You had four months and a great many opportunities." Kyouya went back to the town. "You are a carnivore. You have been one the entire time. You do not hunt and you do not fight and you would give away your own coat, and none of that is the definition. The definition is that you decide where the line is and then nothing moves it."

"Kyouya, I have spent six years letting people move it."

"You have spent six years letting people move *you*," said Kyouya. "That is not the same thing, and I have watched you for two years, and I have never once seen the line move. The town is inside it. Every person in that house is inside it. My mother is inside it and she does not know how she got there and neither do I." A pause. "You are not in it. That is a fault and I have written it down and Old Sawada has a copy."

The small animal put his hand over his face.

"You wrote it in the *petition*."

"Fault number one. Undervalues himself to a degree that constitutes a structural risk to this district." Kyouya stood up and brushed the snow off. "Azuma Maro read it out at dinner. Twice."

"She *what*?"

"I have been informed that it is now being quoted in three houses and that the Kurokawa have a version with the wording improved," said Kyouya, "and that there is nothing whatsoever either of us can do about it."

  

The four of them walked the ground every week from the day of the transfer and Kyouya wrote it up like a gate, because it was one.

That was the part nobody outside the four of them understood, then or later. A piece of ground with a name on it is not a gift until somebody stands on it. Nine hundred square metres on the north slope with three approaches and two of them outside the shrine's wards is, in the plain language of this district, **an opening**, and Kyouya had made it on purpose and had then had to close it.

So: rotation. Kojima took the north-east corner, which was the bad one, because Kojima was a *Hogo* and could hold a line longer than any of them. The Fuwa twins took the two western approaches, which was overkill and which Kyouya had decided he did not care about. Tetsuya took whatever was left and the schedule, because Tetsuya was the only one of them who could be trusted with a schedule.

Kyouya took the ridge, alone, most nights, which was not tactically necessary and which nobody mentioned.

It cost them a winter. The twins' mother came to the house about it in January and had a conversation with Indra that Kyouya was not permitted to hear and that ended with the twins continuing. Kojima got frostnip in the north-east corner in February, refused a *Chiyu* for it on grounds of pride, and was extremely pleased about the whole business. And the whole arrangement was, by any measure Kyouya had been taught, a poor use of four capable children on ground with no water, no road, and nothing on it.

"You are guarding a field," said his mother, once, in passing, without stopping.

"Yes."

"Good," said Indra, and kept walking, and Kyouya stood in the corridor and worked out about nine seconds later that he had just been told he was correct.

  

The plum tree came into flower in February and nobody had expected it to, because nobody had been paying attention to it for a hundred and forty years.

Kyouya went up to look. So did the *Chiyu* gardener, who cried, which was embarrassing for everybody. So did the small *Gen'ei* apprentice, who counted the blossoms and reported the number at dinner and was taken seriously.

The small animal went up on the second day, alone, and stayed up there for two hours.

Kyouya knew because he was on the wall, at two and a half paces of vertical equivalent, which was the distance he had settled on the previous spring and had never explained and never would.

Nothing happened. The small animal sat under a plum tree on nine hundred square metres of useless ground that had his name on it, and did not write anything down. After two hours he came down and went and ate what Ayame put in front of him.

Kyouya, on the wall, in the cold, with his coat wrong and his arithmetic entirely rebuilt from the ground up, watched him come and marked the file closed.

  

The petition went into the shrine's record in the spring and the district found out inside nine days, which was four days faster than Kyouya had budgeted for and which he blamed, correctly, on Azuma Maro.

He had expected to mind. He did not mind, and he spent a week establishing why, and the answer was that a formal understanding between the Hibari main line and the Sawada shrine was, in the language of this district, a *statement about the next fifty years*.

It said: the *Yasuragi* pillar and the *Gen'ei* pillar have agreed to stop being two things.

It said: whoever comes for Namimori after this comes for both.

And it said, to four districts under shelter and to a great many people further out who were paying more attention than Namimori realised, that the *Kohaku* who had been asleep for six years had somebody standing on his ground who was not a Guardian and not a servant and not an adult managing him.

The Kurokawa sent a formal congratulation, a very ugly vase, and two *Ketsueki* daughters to deliver it, which everybody understood.

The Miura sent nothing, which Kyouya recorded.

Fukuda of Yokkaichi sent a letter, three lines long, in a bad *Ketsueki* hand, addressed to Kyouya personally rather than to the house, which said that a *Ketsueki* of his line held the inward gate now and would hold it for the Sawada and for whoever the Sawada were understood with, and Kyouya read it four times and put it in the box with his father's letter.

  

The list turned into something else in the second year and Kyouya has never once explained how.

What happened, mechanically, was that the *Chiyu* started telling him things. The Torii line sees everything in a town: who is drinking, who is being hit, whose grandmother has stopped eating, which house has a *Ketsueki* child coming in too fast and no one competent to steady him. They see it because they are called, and they say nothing, because a *Chiyu* who talks is a *Chiyu* nobody calls.

They started telling Kyouya. Not everything, and not the confidences, and the grandmother set the boundary herself in one sentence on the first afternoon: *we will tell you where to stand, boy, and we will never tell you why.*

It was the best arrangement he ever made and he did not negotiate it. She offered it.

He asked her, years later, why.

"Because you came and asked what I *needed*," said the Torii grandmother. "Not what I knew. Eleven years I have been treating this district and a Hibari has never once come through that door except to ask me what I knew."

  

By the end of that second year there were four of them, then six, then eleven, and none of it had a name.

The name came later and came from outside, and Kyouya disliked it for about a decade and then stopped caring. What it actually was, in the beginning, on paper, in an exercise book kept by a seven-year-old, was a list of two hundred people and one column at the right-hand side asking whether each of them would be missed.

He never changed the column. Forty years later it was still there, in a different book, in a worse hand.

  

"You are pleased," said Tetsuya.

"I am not pleased."

"You have hit four things this week and none of them were people. That is what pleased looks like on you and I have been keeping records since we were three."

Kyouya considered denying it and did not bother.

They were on the north wall, in the spring, with the *Kotei* vault up over the whole town and a plum tree in flower nine hundred metres away on ground that belonged to a seven-year-old.

"Tetsuya."

"Mm."

"When I asked you and Kojima and the twins. About the ground."

"Yes."

"You said yes before I finished the sentence."

"I did."

"Why?"

Kusakabe Tetsuya, who was seven, and who would spend the next fifty years being the only person alive who could tell Hibari Kyouya he was being an idiot, considered it with the whole of his face, which is how he considered everything.

"Because you asked," he said.

"That is not a reason!"

"It is the only reason I have got, and it is a very good one, and you are not going to like it." Tetsuya swung his legs. "You have never asked anybody for anything. Not once, not since we were three. You take, or you do it yourself, or you go without, and I have watched you go without a great many times."

"That is not…"

"So when you asked," said Tetsuya, "I said yes. Immediately. Before you finished. Because I have been waiting four years for you to ask me for something and I was not going to make you say the whole sentence."

Kyouya sat on the wall in the spring and did not have anything to say to that, and Tetsuya, who knew him, let it alone and looked at the plum tree.

Kyouya is six! Everybody keeps forgetting and then he does something like this.

2. Carnivores, herbivores, scavengers. He is not being metaphorical and he is not being cruel. He has read the books, he includes himself in the first category, and a herd is not an insult to him. Aurel is right that this is peer-level classification from inside his own taxonomy and not a person looking down at an animal, and I have written it that way throughout.
3. Two and a half paces is the escort distance. Kusakabe has measured it. It has a vertical equivalent, which is worse.
4. Hibari courtship: three gifts, escalating, presented in silence, then a written petition to the head of house. The petition traditionally includes a list of the other party's faults, which the Hibari regard as the romantic part, and which is the single most Hibari thing in this fic.
5. Indra's letter at nineteen listed her faults and she married him. Kyouya found it in a box at five. It is the family's finest surviving document and he is not wrong.
6. The rule he crossed out was a good rule. His mother says so. She also says breaking it having done the survey first is not the same as recklessness, and she is talking about herself at twenty-six, and that is the whole of what she is willing to say about it in this chapter.
7. The north slope is real in the sense that this kind of orphaned parcel is extremely real. Four hundred years of records, three registers disagreeing, and an ancestor who refused on principle for nineteen years and then died. Ask anybody who has tried to buy old shrine land.
8. Maro had money on nine months. Musashi had money on fourteen. Neither of them has ever told Kyouya what the stake was and both of them paid up.
9. The keystone species reclassification is the only "change" in this chapter and it happens after the decision, not before it. He does not act because he understands. He acts, and then understands, and Aurel called this in the outline eleven weeks ago.
