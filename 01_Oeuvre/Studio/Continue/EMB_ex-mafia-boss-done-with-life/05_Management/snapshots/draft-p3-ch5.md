# Chapter 5: Visiting Physician Asks The Patient Three Ordinary Questions. Gets No Answer To Any Of Them

Vayaskara Devan had been a physician for twenty-six years and had learned to distrust the letter that says *there is no urgency*.

Amba's niece had written it. Not Amba, which was itself information. Amba wrote to him roughly four times a year, with instructions and criticism and no news whatsoever. This was Indra, in her flat clean Malayalam that had got worse in eleven years abroad. A child in the household she now served had a chronic condition of six years' standing. The condition had recently and inexplicably improved. There was no urgency. She would be grateful if he came.

He booked the flight the same afternoon.

He was fifty-one. He had trained in the eight-limbed tradition his family had practised for nine generations, in a district where the nearest hospital was ninety minutes away until he was thirty. Then he had spent two decades doing something his grandfather would not have recognised. He moved between the two systems and refused to pretend either of them was complete. He had a licence from Thiruvananthapuram and a licence from Osaka and a great many opinions about both.

He had also, eleven years ago, come to this town to wake a woman who had been asleep for four months, and had done it, and had never fully understood how.

  

Indra met him at the station and he did not recognise her for about a second and a half.

That was not the years. He had seen her in the intervening time, twice, and she had not changed in any way a photograph would show. It was that she was *standing still*.

Indra Amba-mol. At eleven she had been physically incapable of remaining in a chair through a meal. Tutors beat her for it twice and she went on doing it. Once she walked eleven kilometres in the dark because she could not sleep, and did not think to mention it to anybody. She stood on a provincial Japanese platform with her hands at her sides. She waited for him to cross forty metres of concrete. She did not fidget once.

"You have got old," she said, in Malayalam.

"You have got *still*."

"Ah." Something crossed her face. "Yes. People keep saying that."

"Amma will want a full report and I intend to give her one."

"Amma has had a full report for eleven years and has drawn her own conclusions. They are wrong. I have stopped correcting them." She took his bag, which he objected to and lost. "You will stay at the Hibari house. You will eat there and you will complain about the food and I will pretend not to hear it, as before."

  

The briefing took an hour and was, medically speaking, almost useless.

He got it from the grandmother, in a small hall at the shrine, with green tea he did not want and a woman he liked immediately.

Sawada Musashi was sixty-three and had the sharpness of somebody who has spent a long life being right about people. She gave him a history that was fluent, well-ordered, and chronologically precise. It was also full of holes, and he could feel the edges of every one of them.

Episodes since infancy, in a *Kohaku* whose *Kotei* opened at birth. Roughly twice a month. Duration ten minutes to four hours. Presentation: sudden onset, altered awareness, unresponsive but not unconscious. No seizure activity. No incontinence. No post-episode confusion. Full recall afterwards. Six years of a chart with the frequency plotted on it. And in the last eleven weeks, nothing.

"What changed eleven weeks ago?"

"An animal came into the house."

Devan put his tea down.

"Sawada-san."

"…I am aware of how that sounds."

"You are aware of how it sounds and you have said it anyway. Which tells me you have decided to be honest with me about the parts you *can* be honest about." He got his notebook out, which he did not usually do in front of families. "So let me ask this properly. Is the animal the cause, or is the animal the thing you have decided to say instead of the cause?"

Musashi looked at him for a moment with an expression he could not read and would learn, over the following days, to recognise as the beginning of respect.

"Both," she said.

  

Here is what he could get, in the end, over three days, from four different people.

The condition was Flame-related, which in this country is a sentence that closes a discussion rather than opening one. Everybody said so, in the way people say a thing that has stopped being a hypothesis and become a piece of furniture. Nobody could tell him the mechanism. At least two of them were lying about not being able to.

The condition was not treatable. Not by *Chiyu*, not by *Hogo*, not by anything the Torii line had in it. Everybody said this too. They said it in a tone he had heard in villages, in teaching hospitals, and in exactly one Vongola clinic in Naples. The tone is always the same and it always means *we stopped looking*.

The child was extraordinary. Everybody said this most of all. They said it about his *Kotei*, about his memory, about his handwriting. One of them used the word *Tenmei* and then looked at the floor. About the fact that four districts had petitioned into this town's shelter since he was three. The priestess Azuma said it with her hands. The gardener said it while explaining a persimmon.

And in three days of talking to the entire household about a six-year-old, not one person told him whether the child slept.

  

On the third day he went to see the *Chiyu*, because he wanted to know what medicine looked like in this country's flame society before he opened his mouth about it.

The Namimori *Chiyu* line is called Torii. There are eleven of them and they are very good, and two of them are strong enough that a *Ketsueki* household four districts away will send for one rather than use its own. He spent a morning at their house being shown things, most of which he had read about and none of which he had watched, and he came away with the clearest understanding of the problem he had had in twenty-six years.

A *Chiyu* closes a wound. Not a *Ketsueki*, not a *Hogo*, not any of the others: only the *Chiyu*, and only that one thing. Not metaphorically. Devan watched a woman of about forty put her hand over a four-centimetre laceration on a builder's forearm and hold it there for eleven minutes, and the wound closed, and the builder went back to work, and there was no scar and no infection and no discussion. He had seen the same thing in Naples from a Sun, which is what the Italians call a *Chiyu*, and had assumed some of it was theatre. None of it was theatre.

A *Chiyu* accelerates. That is the actual mechanism as far as anybody in that house could describe it: the flame does not heal, it makes the body heal faster, and everything downstream of that follows. Broken bones in nine days. Pneumonia in three. A *Hogo* who took four hundred volts through both hands and was at work on the Monday. The Torii grandmother told him, with some pride, that Namimori had not lost a child to fever in forty years.

And then Devan asked what a *Chiyu* does about a heart murmur, and the room went slightly wrong.

  

"It does nothing," said the grandmother.

"Nothing at all?"

"A *Chiyu* speeds a body up. A murmur is not a thing a body is trying to finish." She was not defensive about it. She was reciting something she had clearly recited before. "We can do nothing for a bad heart, a bad joint, a bad eye. A *Chiyu* is not a *Kotei*, doctor, we do not make things whole, we make them *hurry*. We can do nothing for anything the body has decided is *finished*, correctly or otherwise."

"And a child who does not sleep?"

"Nothing," she said. "That is not an injury… it is a *condition*, and we do not have those here."

Devan sat in a stranger's house in a foreign country and had the thought he had been circling for three days, and it arrived complete.

**Flame society has extraordinary emergency medicine and no medicine at all.**

Everything a *Chiyu* does is acute. Wounds, fevers, breaks, poison, birth. A *Chiyu* for the body and a *Gen'ei* for the pain and a *Hogo* to keep a heart going for the eleven minutes it takes the *Chiyu* to arrive. Everything acute is solved so well and so fast that the entire culture has stopped noticing the existence of the other kind. There is no chronic care here. There is no concept of a workup. Nobody takes a history, because a history is what you take when you cannot simply put your hand on the thing.

And so a child with an episodic condition of six years' standing, in the wealthiest and best-connected shrine household in the district, had never once had a physician sit down and ask him what his week was like.

Not because they did not care. Because nobody in the entire system had the *shape of the question*.

  

"Have you examined him?" he asked the Torii grandmother.

"Tenmei-sama? Four times." She counted on her fingers. "A cut. A fever at three. A fall from the wisteria at four, which was the wisteria's fault. And once in the winter when he was very small and something was extremely wrong and nobody would tell us what."

"What did you find that last time?"

The old woman looked at her hands.

"I put my *Chiyu* into a *Kotei* core and it went straight through," she said. "Like putting your hand into a doorway. There was nothing to accelerate because there was nothing *there*, and I have not spoken about it for six years, and Sawada-san has never asked me not to, which I have always thought was worse."

Devan wrote none of that down. He carried it back to the shrine and put it in the part of the file that stays in a physician's head.

He examined him on the fourth morning, in a room with good north light, with the grandmother present because the child was six and Devan did not examine children alone.

"Good morning," he said. "My name is Vayaskara Devan. I am a physician. Do you know why I am here?"

"Because my grandmother asked you to come, and because you woke her up eleven years ago, and because you are the only doctor in this country she has ever said a kind word about." The boy sat with his palms flat on his knees. "Also because you trained with Indra-san when you were children, and she has mentioned it twice, which for Indra-san is a great deal."

"…That is a very complete answer."

"You asked a question. I answered it."

"Mm," said Devan, and wrote nothing down, and began.

The examination took ninety minutes and he did all of it. Pulse in three positions. His grandfather taught him that. He spent his thirties defending it to Japanese colleagues and his forties quietly noticing that it was right more often than it had any business being. Tongue. Eyes. Reflexes. Coordination. None of it touched the *Kotei* and none of it was meant to. The neurological screen from the Osaka side, all of it, including the parts that make children laugh.

The boy did not laugh. He complied. He complied so smoothly that it took Devan until the fifty-minute mark to notice he was being *assisted*. The child anticipated each instruction. He presented the limb before it was asked for. He held still before he was told.

Nobody has to teach a six-year-old to do that. Something has.

  

The findings, in order, as he wrote them that night.

Underweight. Not badly. Eleven per cent below where he wanted him and consistent with a child who eats when reminded and forgets when not, which the entire household had told him about in the tone of an affectionate family joke.

Sleep. He got this out of the boy in four questions and it took him a further hour to stop being angry about it. Four to five hours a night, by the child's own estimate. The estimate was precise, because he had been recording it. Onset difficulty rather than maintenance. Waking at four most days and getting up rather than lying there. It had been going on for approximately three years. It had never been raised with a physician, because nobody in this house had known.

They had not known because he did not sleep in a room anybody passed.

Vigilance. This was the one that put the pen down. The boy tracked the door. Not nervously and not obviously. Devan missed it for the first forty minutes because it was so well done. Then he moved his own chair four inches, and watched a small boy's attention update the room in a way he had last seen in a man of forty who had spent nine years in the wrong part of Naples.

Musculoskeletal. Nothing. Good tone, good coordination, no tremor.

Cognitive. He did not have a scale that fitted and he wrote down that he did not have a scale that fitted, which was more honest than anything else in the file.

The episodes. He could find no neurological cause. That was not a finding of nothing. Devan had spent twenty-six years learning to distinguish *I have found nothing* from *there is nothing*. This was the first. He wrote it in those words: **no organic cause identified within the limits of what I have been permitted to examine. The *Kotei* is outside my competence and I decline to guess at it.**

  

He raised the last part with the grandmother on the fifth evening.

"There is a piece you are not giving me."

"Yes."

"May I have it?"

"No," said Musashi.

"Then I would like to know whether it is a piece that would change my management. If it is, we have a different conversation. If it is not, I will stop asking, and we can both be more comfortable."

She thought about that for longer than he expected.

"It would not change what you would do," she said. "It would change what you would *hope*. I have watched three people take that information and go quiet for a month. I would rather have you working."

"That is an extraordinarily manipulative answer."

"It is also true."

"Those are not in conflict, Sawada-san, and you know it." He sat back. "Fine. I will work in the dark. But I am going to tell you what I have found in the light, and you are not going to like it, and I need you to understand before I start that none of it is about the episodes."

  

He gave her three things.

"He does not sleep. Four hours a night for three years. Nobody in this house knew, and I want to be plain that this is not a failure of love, it is a failure of *architecture*, because you have housed a child in a room on a corridor nobody uses and he has been awake in it alone since he was three."

Musashi did not move.

"Second. He eats when instructed. He does not eat when not instructed. He is eleven per cent underweight, and every person in this house has told me about it as a charming personal quirk. Including the woman who cooks. She has built an entire system of reminders around it. She is very proud of the system and has never once asked why it is needed."

"Ayame will take that badly."

"Ayame should take that badly, and then Ayame should keep doing exactly what she is doing, because it is working and it is the only thing in this house that is." He turned a page. "Third, and this is the one I would like you to sit with. He watches the door. He watched it for ninety minutes while I examined him. He did it while being spoken to. He did it lying down with his eyes shut, which is a thing I did not know a child could do."

"He is a *Kotei*," said Musashi. "They are aware of rooms."

"Sawada-san." Devan put the notebook down. "I have examined four Skies. Two in Naples, one in Kobe, one here. They are aware of rooms. They are not *braced* in them."

  

She was quiet for a while. Then she said, "What do you want to do?"

"I want to ask him three questions, and none of them are about his *Kotei*."

"Which are?"

"Whether he sleeps. Whether anything hurts. And whether he is frightened."

Musashi laughed. It was not a happy sound.

"You will get nothing."

"I usually do."

"You will get nothing from *him*," she said. "Doctor, I have been asking that child questions for six years. I am extremely good at it. I got the sleeping out of him in the first year and he told me it was normal and I believed him, and I have had four years to be sick about that." She turned her cup. "He answers what he is asked. He answers it accurately and completely and immediately, and you come away with a full notebook and nothing in it, and it takes you about two years to work out how it is done."

"How is it done?"

"He answers questions about what he will *do*," said Musashi. "Every time. You ask him how he is and he tells you what he is going to do about how he is, and it is such a good answer, and it arrives so fast, that you do not notice the substitution until you are in the corridor."

  

He did a second examination on the eighth day, with Maro in the room, and it was the piece of work he was proudest of in twenty-six years and it produced almost nothing.

The idea was simple and nobody had ever tried it. She would take her *Kotei* readings on her eleven descriptors, and he would take his findings on his, and they would do it simultaneously, on the same child, in the same hour, and then they would sit down and try to build a table.

It took four hours and most of it was arguing about vocabulary.

"*Settling*," said Devan. "You have used that word nine times. Define it."

"It is how much the *Kotei* is willing to be where it is."

"That is not a definition, that is a *poem*."

"Then give me a better one!" Maro slapped the mat. "You have a word, doctor, I have watched you use it. When you put three fingers on his wrist and made the face. What is the word?"

"…Tone," said Devan slowly. "I would call it tone. In my tradition it is a property of tissue, not of a flame, and it describes how a thing sits when nothing is being asked of it."

The two of them looked at each other.

"Write that down," said Maro.

  

What they built by the end of the afternoon was four rows long and it was the first document of its kind that either of them had heard of.

*Settling* mapped, roughly and with argument, onto tone. *Reach* mapped onto nothing at all in his system and he wrote *no equivalent* and underlined it. What Maro called the *bank* is how much a *Kotei* holds in reserve rather than expressing. That one corresponded disturbingly well with a thing Devan measured in the third pulse position, and had been taught to call, in a phrase that translated badly, *the held breath of the organ*.

And the fourth row was the one that stopped them.

Maro's readings said the boy's *Kotei* had been at the same value for six years, with the saw superimposed on it, and that the saw had gone and the value had not moved.

Devan's findings said the boy's body had been in a state of low continuous alert for at least three years, that this had not changed in eleven weeks, and that the two things had almost certainly never been connected.

"So the animal fixed the flame," said Maro.

"The animal fixed *your* half," said Devan. "Mine is exactly where it was, and mine is the half that is going to kill him at fifty."

  

They sat with that.

"You said the *Chiyu* cannot touch it," Devan said.

"The Torii woman told you the truth. A *Chiyu* accelerates a body toward something it is already trying to reach." Maro's hands had stopped moving, which he had learned in a week meant she was being serious. "There is no *Chiyu* in this country that can make a child stop watching a door. There is no *Gen'ei* that can do it either, and if there were I would burn the house down before I let anyone use it on him, and you may write *that* in your report."

"I intend to."

"Good." She got up, and got stuck halfway, and let him help her, and was rude about it. "Doctor. Before you go. You have not asked me the question you have been sitting on since Tuesday."

"Which is?"

"Whether the child knows he is being watched by me. On the chart. Twice a month for six years." She straightened her sleeves. "The answer is yes, he has known since he was four, and he has never once asked to see the numbers, and I have never once offered them." A pause. "We are both extremely proud of this arrangement and I have recently begun to suspect it is the stupidest thing either of us has ever done."

"…Why are you telling me?"

"Because you are leaving on Sunday," said Maro, "and everybody tells the doctor who is leaving on Sunday. Surely you knew that."

  

He asked anyway. Of course he asked.

It was the sixth day and he had arranged it badly on purpose. Not in the examination room, and not with the grandmother present, and not at a time that had been put in anybody's schedule. He found the boy on the west veranda at seven in the morning, with a bowl of something going cold and the animal asleep in his sleeve. He sat down on the boards without asking. His knees informed him about it.

"May I ask you three questions?"

"Yes."

"They are not medical questions in the sense you are expecting."

"All right."

"Do you sleep?"

"About four and a half hours," said the boy. "I have been keeping a record since I was four and I can show you if that would help. It is not distressing. I have tried the obvious things, including the ones your tradition recommends, which I read about last week in preparation for your visit. Warm milk is useless and I would like somebody to say so publicly."

Devan wrote nothing.

"Second question. Does anything hurt?"

"No."

"Nothing at all?"

"Nothing that a person would call pain." The boy considered. "There is a pulling, sometimes. It is not painful. It is closer to a pressure, and it is not in a place I can point to, and I have decided it is not useful to report because there is nothing to be done about it and it upsets people."

"You have decided that."

"Yes."

"You are six."

"I am aware," said the boy, "of the shape of that argument."

  

Devan looked at the garden for a while.

He had a reputation, in two countries, for being a slow physician. Colleagues meant it as a criticism and patients did not, and he had stopped defending it around the age of forty. The whole of his usefulness was in the pause. Eleven seconds after a person finishes answering, a certain kind of patient starts talking again, because the silence has not been filled and they cannot leave it alone.

He gave it eleven seconds. Then twenty. Then a full minute.

The child sat beside him with the cold bowl and did not fill it, and Devan understood that he was sitting next to somebody who was extremely comfortable with silence, and revised his estimate of the last six years downwards again.

"Third question," he said.

"Yes."

"Are you frightened?"

  

He would think about the next fifteen seconds for a long while.

The boy opened his mouth. Devan watched him do it. He watched a six-year-old start to produce an answer, and he watched the answer arrive, ready, fluent, and complete, the way the last two had. It was there. It was on the way out.

And then it stopped.

Not because the child chose to stop it. Devan had seen a great many people decline to answer a question and it does not look like that. This was the other thing. The thing you see in stroke, and in certain kinds of exhaustion, where the machinery reaches for a component and the component is not on the shelf.

The boy shut his mouth. He looked at the garden.

"I do not know," he said.

"That is all right."

"No, I…" He stopped again. His hands had gone to the edge of the bowl and stayed there. "That was not modesty. I am not being careful with you. I went to answer and there was nothing there. I have an answer for the other two. I have had an answer for the other two for years, and I have given them to my grandmother and to Maro-sama and once to a very persistent woman from Suzuka."

"And this one?"

"I have never…" He stopped. "Nobody has asked me."

  

Fuyu came out of his sleeve.

It came out slowly, in the manner of something that had been listening. It went up the front of the boy's kimono and sat inside his collar with its head under his jaw. The boy put two fingers against its side without appearing to notice that he had done it.

Devan had been given a great deal of contradictory information about this animal over six days. He looked at it and did the only thing his tradition really insists on, which is to observe what is in front of you before deciding what it means.

What was in front of him was a child self-soothing with an animal, competently, in a pattern that had clearly been running for weeks, and not knowing that he was doing it.

He wrote that down. It was the most useful thing in the file.

  

"May I tell you what I think?" he said.

"Please."

"I think your episodes have a cause I have not been permitted to find, and I have made my peace with that, and I am not going to spend the rest of this visit picking at it." He shifted, because the boards were doing something to his hip. "And I think the episodes are not the thing that is wrong with you."

The boy turned his head.

"I have examined you for six days. Here is my finding. You are a healthy, underweight, chronically sleep-deprived six-year-old with a condition I cannot characterise and a level of vigilance I would expect in an adult with a history I do not want to imagine." Devan kept his voice entirely flat, because the content was doing enough. "And when a stranger asked whether you were frightened, you reached for the answer and it was not there. In six years, in a house full of people who love you, nobody has ever put that question in front of you. So you have never had occasion to build one."

"…That is a lot to take from one silence."

"It is… and I may be wrong. I am wrong perhaps a third of the time and I write it in the file when I am." He got his stick and put it across his knees. "But I have been doing this for twenty-six years, and I have learned that when a household tells me about a child for three days without once mentioning how the child sleeps, the household is not hiding it. The household has stopped seeing it. And what a household stops seeing is generally the thing the child has arranged for them to stop seeing."

  

Azuma Maro cornered him in the corridor on the fifth day and talked at him for forty minutes about *Kotei* density, and it was the most useful conversation of the visit and she did not know it.

"You are the physician. Explain something to me."

"I am extremely tired, Azuma-sama."

"Then you will not argue, which suits me!" She produced a folder from somewhere. "Six years of readings. Twice a month. I have never shown these to a doctor because no doctor has ever been interested, and you have been in this house four days and you have asked *four people* what the boy eats, which means you are either a fool or the first serious person to walk in here since the war."

He took the folder. He could not read the notation.

"Teach me."

"*Really?*" She sat down so fast her knees cracked. "Nobody has ever said that to me."

It took her two hours and he understood perhaps a third of it, and the third he understood was worth the flight. She measured a *Kotei* as he measured a pulse: not for size but for *quality*, in a vocabulary of eleven descriptors, four of which had no equivalent in any language he owned. Density. Reach. *Bank*. What she called *settling*, which she demonstrated by putting her hand flat on the table and lifting it very slowly, and which he eventually decided was something like tone.

And her chart showed exactly what her chart showed. Six years of a saw. Then eleven weeks flat.

"You want to know what I think," said Maro.

"I want to know what you have *measured*. I can get opinions from anybody in this house and I have."

"Ha!" She stabbed a finger at him. "Yes. Good. Then: I have measured that the thing which stopped is not the thing that was wrong. The *Kotei* is exactly where it was. Nothing has moved. Something has come and stood in front of it, and it weighs about as much as a cucumber, and it is currently asleep in a child's collar."

"That is not a measurement."

"No," Maro agreed cheerfully, "it is not, and I have written it in the record anyway with a note saying it is not, which is more than most of your colleagues do."

Devan decided, then, that he liked this town enormously and that it was going to be very bad for him professionally.

  

Indra found him in the Hibari garden that evening and he had a great deal to say and said none of it.

"Well?"

"I am not going to discuss a patient with you."

"I am his Guardian."

"You are a woman who sat on a wall at eleven refusing to come down with a fever of thirty-nine. I am not going to discuss a patient with you," said Devan. "And I would like you to notice that you asked."

Indra sat down. There was a long pause in which he became aware that she was not going to argue, which was new.

"Ask me instead," she said.

"About what?"

"About whether I know. You have been in this house four days and you have wanted to ask me something since the second one, and it is not about him, and you have been too polite, and it is extremely irritating."

Devan laughed, which surprised them both.

"Fine. Amba-mol… what happened to you?"

"Be precise."

"You are *still*," he said. "You were the least still human being I have ever met. Your mother treated it as a condition and gave you a regimen, and I watched you follow that regimen for six years while hating it in a way I found genuinely alarming, and I assumed that when you left you would drop it inside a month and spend your life moving." He spread his hands. "You have been in one town for eleven years. You have knelt in the snow to a child. You stood on a platform on Tuesday for a full minute without moving your feet. What happened?"

  

"I found something worth being still for," said Indra.

"That is a line from a poem, and you have never read a poem in your life."

"I have read several and disliked all of them." She was quiet a moment. "…You want the real answer."

"I have come four thousand kilometres and eaten your mother-in-law's cooking. Yes."

"The regimen was never for stillness. Amma told me it was, and Amma was wrong, and I have never said so to her because she was wrong for the right reasons and it worked." Indra looked out at the dark. "It was for *choosing*. An Inverse Rain does not want to move towards anything, Devan, it simply wants to move, and if you do not build something that can hold you in place then your whole life is decided by whatever you happened to be near." A pause. "I did the hour every morning for twenty-five years and it did nothing for me except one thing." She looked at him. "It meant that when I found something I wanted to stay for, I was *able* to stay."

"…Ah."

"Yes."

"I am going to write that to your mother…"

"You are *not*!"

"I am fifty-one years old and I have wanted to win an argument with that woman since I was nine," said Devan, "and I am going to use this, and you cannot stop me because you are here and she is there."

  

The household reacted to the report in four different ways over eleven days and he stayed to watch all of them, which he had not planned and which turned out to be the real work.

Ayame took it worst, exactly as predicted, and took it in the most useful direction available. She did not apologise and she did not cry. She came and found him in the corridor with the paper in her hand and said, "Where does the food go?"

"Sorry?"

"He eats what I put in front of him when I stand there. He does not eat when I do not stand there. I have known that for four years and I built a system and I have been *proud* of the system." Her *Ketsueki* was up around her, not dangerously, but visible in the way heat is visible over a road. "You have written that I never asked why it was needed. That is correct and I have been awake since three about it. So I am asking. Where does the food go?"

"I do not know."

"Then find out!"

"Ayame-san." He put his hand up. "I have a guess and it is not medical and you will not like it. A person who has decided he is a cost will eat when somebody else has gone to the trouble, because refusing would waste the trouble. And he will not eat when nobody is watching, because when nobody is watching there is no trouble to waste."

She stood in the corridor for a while.

"Then I will stand there," she said, "every meal, for years, and I will make it look like nagging, and nobody in this house will ever know why, including him."

"That is not a treatment."

"You said there was no treatment! You wrote it in your own report, I have read it four times!" She was already going. "So it is not a treatment. It is a *woman standing there*. Go and have your tea, doctor."

Devan went and had his tea, and thought that he had rarely been so comprehensively out-argued by somebody agreeing with him.

  

Kazuki moved the room.

Nobody asked him to. The recommendation was three lines in a four-page report and it said *move the child to a corridor with traffic*, and Kazuki read it, and did not discuss it with anybody, and spent two days doing something at the east end of the house with lumber.

What he built was a small room off the kitchen passage. It had a window onto the winter garden and it was directly under the corridor Ayame used at five in the morning and it was, structurally speaking, the noisiest place in the building.

"He will hate it," said Musashi.

"He will hate it for a *week*," said Kazuki, going scarlet, and then, in the loudest voice Devan heard from him in eleven days, "and then he will be able to hear people!"

  

Maro did nothing at all, which Devan came to understand was the most sophisticated response in the house.

"You are not going to change anything?"

"I am going to change *nothing*," said Maro. "Everybody else in this house is now going to descend on that child with schedules and rooms and standing about, all of which is correct, and by the end of the month he will be managed within an inch of his life by six people who love him." She poured. "So I am going to be the one who does not. I am going to go on shouting at him about the Kurokawa and losing my eggs to that animal and telling him his handwriting is deteriorating, which it is. Somebody has to be the room where nothing is being done to him."

Devan looked at a woman he had dismissed on day one as a noisy old scholar.

"Azuma-sama, that is genuinely excellent clinical judgement."

"Yes," said Maro. "I have been doing this since before you were weaned. Drink your tea."

  

And Musashi read the report eleven times and did the thing nobody expected, which was nothing visible at all for nine days, and then one entirely ordinary evening she went and sat on the boards outside her grandson's new room without knocking and without a reason, and stayed there for an hour, and did it again the next night, and the night after that.

She never told him why. She never mentioned the report. She did it for eleven years.

  

He wrote the report on the seventh day and it was four pages and only one of them was about the episodes.

The rest was the other thing. A sleep protocol, which he did not expect to be followed and which he wrote in detail anyway. A recommendation on the room: move the child to a corridor with traffic on it, immediately, and do not explain why to the child. A feeding schedule that formalised what Ayame was already doing, so that it would survive Ayame having a bad week. And a paragraph he rewrote four times about vigilance. He could not treat it. It would not resolve on its own. The only thing known to help was a period of years in which nothing the child was braced for actually happened.

At the end he wrote a line that Musashi would read eleven times over the following year, and would eventually read out loud to Indra in the small hall, and would keep in the lacquer box with two hundred and eight seating charts:

**This child has been examined by many people for what he can do. To my knowledge he has not previously been examined for how he is. I recommend the second be done annually, by someone who does not need him.**

  

He went to look at the tree on the last afternoon, because Indra took him, and because he had been in this town eleven days and nobody had mentioned it once.

It stood behind the shrine and it was enormous and it was, to his eye, a sakaki like any other sakaki, and the whole of Namimori arranged itself around it in a way that became obvious the moment you knew to look. Every path bent. Every roofline stopped short.

"What is it?"

"A tree."

"Amba-mol."

"It is a tree, Devan. It is older than the town and it is the reason the town is here, and that is all I am able to say to you." Indra put her hands behind her back. "You have felt it every day since you arrived. You will have thought it was the weather."

He had, in fact, thought it was the weather. Eleven days of a faint high pressure over everything, the kind he associated with a coming storm that never came, and he had put it down to the flight and to being fifty-one.

"That is the *Kotei* vault," said Indra. "It covers the whole town. It has for six years. Every person who lives here is standing under it and about four hundred of them know, and the rest of them have simply decided that Namimori has good weather."

"Under a *Kotei*?"

"Under a Sky." She said it flatly, in Malayalam, which made it worse rather than better. "You have spent eleven days examining a child. That is the child, doctor. That is what he is doing while he is not sleeping."

Devan stood under four hundred years of tree with his hands in his pockets.

"I want to say something and I want you to let me say it badly."

"Go on."

"In my whole career I have had two patients I could not put down." He watched the branches. "One was a woman of thirty in Kozhikode with four children and a diagnosis, and she asked me how long, and I told her, and she said *good, that is enough time* and went home and organised everything. I think about her about once a month and it has been nineteen years."

"And the other is a six-year-old you met last Tuesday."

"And the other is a six-year-old I met last Tuesday," Devan agreed. "Because she was thirty and she had had a life, Amba-mol, and she had things in it that were hers. And I have spent eleven days in a house full of people who adore that child, and I have not been able to find one single thing in his life that exists for no reason except that he wanted it."

Indra was quiet a moment.

"There is one," she said.

"…Is there?"

"It has no use whatsoever. It steals eggs, it hates water, it will not take an instruction from anybody, and he spent ninety minutes with nine people in a room getting it a name." She was almost smiling, which on Indra was extraordinary. "You have written four pages of recommendations, doctor. Would it help you to know that the single most effective intervention in that house arrived by itself, in a bamboo tube, and nobody prescribed it?"

"…It would help me *enormously*," said Devan, "and I resent it."

  

On the last morning he did one more thing, which was not in the report and which he told nobody about.

He went and found the *Chiyu* gardener in the winter garden at six, because he had a question that had been sitting in him since the Torii house.

"Kazuki-san. When you put your *Chiyu* into a plant, what are you doing?"

Kazuki went red, as he did whenever anybody asked him anything. "I am… hurrying it. Toward what it is trying to do anyway."

"And if the plant is not trying to do anything?"

"Then nothing happens." He thought about it, with his hands full of something. "You cannot *Chiyu* a dead cutting, doctor. People try. It does not take. The flame goes in and there is nothing on the other end of it to hurry."

"That is exactly what the Torii grandmother told me about a bad heart."

"Then she is right, and she would be pleased to hear it, and I am not going to be the one who tells her!" said Kazuki, in a small panic, and then, more quietly, "It is why I grow the winter ones. A *fuyugaki* is trying to do something in January. Most things are not. So there is somewhere for the *Chiyu* to go."

Devan stood in a cold garden and understood the whole of the last eleven days at once.

*A Chiyu cannot start something. It can only hurry a thing that has already begun.*

A child who has never been asked whether he is frightened has not begun. There is nothing in him for anything to accelerate, and no flame in this country and no medicine in his own will put it there, and the only intervention available is the one Ayame had worked out in a corridor by herself: **somebody standing there, for years, until the thing begins on its own.**

He wrote that on the train. It went into the report as one sentence and everybody who read it thought it was a metaphor.

  

He said goodbye on the platform where he had arrived, and the whole household came, which he had not expected and which took twenty minutes.

The grandmother said something formal and correct and shook his hand for slightly too long. The *Ketsueki* woman gave him food for the journey and would not be argued with. The *Chiyu* gardener gave him four persimmons and an explanation of the persimmons that lasted until the announcement. The small *Gen'ei* apprentice bowed to precisely the correct depth and said eleven words.

The boy came last.

"Thank you for the report."

"You have not read the report."

"I have read the report," said the boy. "It was on my grandmother's desk and she left it there. She does not leave things on desks. So I have decided she left it there on purpose, and I am not going to embarrass either of us by asking."

"Mm," said Devan.

They stood on the platform in the cold and neither of them filled it, which was, Devan thought, the first entirely comfortable silence of the visit.

"Doctor. The third question."

"Yes."

"I have been working on it for six days and I have not got anywhere. I want you to know that I *have* been working on it. In case you thought I had put it down."

Devan looked at a small boy on a provincial platform, in a coat that was too good and a face that was too still, holding an animal that had no use.

"Sawada-san," he said. "That is the correct amount of progress."

"…That is not a real answer."

"No," Devan agreed, and picked up his bag, and got on the train. "But it is a true one, and you will have several years to disagree with me, and I intend to come back every one of them."

  

He came back in the spring, and the spring after that, and every spring for nineteen years, and the file got thick.

The sleep improved in the second year and never became normal. The weight came up four per cent and stayed there. The vigilance did not move at all for eleven years and then moved, all at once, for reasons that had nothing to do with medicine and that Devan recorded in three lines without comment because it was not his to write about.

He never did characterise the episodes. He wrote *no organic cause identified* nineteen times, in nineteen consecutive annual reports, in handwriting that got worse every year, and he never once softened it and never once pretended.

And every single visit, at some point, usually on the veranda, usually early, he asked the third question.

The first year: nothing.

The second year: *I have thought about it and I do not think the word applies to me.* Which Devan wrote down verbatim and underlined twice.

The fourth year: *Yes. Not of anything you could put in a file.*

The eleventh year he got a real answer, and it took four minutes to arrive, and it was the only time in twenty-six years of practice that Vayaskara Devan had to leave a room before he had finished taking a history.

He wrote that one down too, afterwards, in the corridor, on his knee, in Malayalam, so that nobody in that house could read it.

I have been waiting to write this chapter since the fox turned up. Sorry it took five.

1. Vayaskara is one of the eight Ashtavaidyan families of Kerala, hereditary physicians in the eight-limbed Ayurvedic tradition, which is a real lineage system and not one I invented. Devan is the first in his line to hold two licences and his grandfather went to his grave regarding this as a moral failure.
2. Amba is Indra's mother and taught them both. Devan was the promising nephew. Indra was the disaster with the Flame. Amba treated a Rain as a patient because she had no other framework, and it was the correct call for the wrong reason, which Indra has known for thirty years and has never told her.
3. Amba-mol - "mol" is an affectionate Malayalam diminutive for a daughter. He has called her that since she was six and she has never once told him to stop.
4. Three pulse positions is standard in the tradition and Devan has spent his career being condescended to about it in two languages.
5. The eleven-second pause is a real clinical technique and it works on almost everybody. It does not work on Tsuna, and the reason it does not work is the whole chapter.
6. Nobody in this fic has previously asked Tsuna whether he is frightened. I went back and checked. Twenty-six chapters of people asking a small terrifying child what he intends to do about things.
7. The room recommendation is the single most effective thing in the report and it costs nothing and nobody thought of it for six years, which is what a blind spot is.
8. Yes, Musashi left the report on the desk on purpose. Yes, he knows. Yes, she knows he knows. This family communicates almost exclusively by leaving objects in places.
9. Devan is not a Guardian, is not flame-active in any significant degree, has no role in any prophecy, and will never fight anybody. He is here because a child needed a doctor and somebody thought to call one. That is the entire point of him.
10. He does write to Amba. She replies in four lines and three of them are about his handwriting.
