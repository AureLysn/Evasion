# Chapter 7: Local Sky Officially Wakes Up At Seven. Town Reacts Poorly. Nobody Was Prepared For How Small He Is

The town had been getting ready for eleven months and had told itself, the entire time, that it was doing something else.

The Kurokawa repainted their gate. They said it was overdue, which was true, and they had said it was overdue for nine years. The Miura settled a boundary dispute that had been running since 1974. They settled it badly, in a hurry, in a way that cost them, and nobody in either family would admit why. The noodle place on Shibata street replaced its awning. Four households on the east side put in orders for formal dress for children who had grown out of theirs. The woman who did the alterations worked from January and did not take a day off. When anybody asked, she said she was busy. When they asked why, she said she was *busy*.

Namimori is a town of sixty thousand people and roughly four hundred of them know what is over their heads. That is the arrangement. It has held for six years, and it holds because it is not a secret in the ordinary sense. It is a shape the town makes together, as a crowd opens a lane for an ambulance without anybody deciding to.

The remaining fifty-nine thousand six hundred know something. They could not tell you what. They know the shrine matters. They know the weather here is good. They know the town has been full of people from elsewhere for six years and that none of them cause trouble. And they know that when the Hibari boy is on the street, the street behaves.

And they knew, by the spring, that something was coming, because their neighbours had started painting things.

  

The formal notice went out on the fourth of March and it was four lines long.

It said that Sawada Tsunayoshi, grandson of the priestess of the Sawada shrine, had recovered from the long illness of his infancy. It said the shrine would hold the rite of *Kotei* recognition on the nineteenth. It said all households of Namimori and the four sheltered districts were called, and that the shrine would provide.

That last clause is not decorative. It is four centuries old and it means the shrine feeds everybody who comes. In the week before the nineteenth the Sawada household bought out three fishmongers, both rice merchants, and the entire winter storage of a farm in Suzuka. The owner cried when the order came through.

Ayame ran it. She had eleven weeks, four hundred kilos of rice, and a household of nine. She recruited fourteen women from the district by the simple method of writing to them and telling them what time to arrive.

None of them declined. Two of them had not spoken to each other since 1981 and worked side by side for eleven days.

  

Here is what the town understood about the rite, in ascending order of how many people understood it.

Everybody knew there would be food.

Most people knew you wore your best and that the shrine would be full and that it would take most of a day.

The Flame-active households, about four hundred of them, knew this was a *Kotei* recognition. They knew the last one in this valley was four centuries ago. They knew the woman it was held for had founded the town, and that the records of it run to three pages and are mostly about seating.

And perhaps forty people knew the rest. The heads of houses. The two priestesses. The physician who flew in from Kerala on the sixteenth. They knew the boy had not in fact been ill, and that the six years were a thing the town did on purpose. What was happening on the nineteenth was this. A district of sixty thousand people was being formally given permission to look at a child. It had spent his entire life protecting him without looking at him.

  

The Kurokawa household argued about it for three days and this is a fair sample of what happened in a hundred houses that month.

"He is *seven*!" said the head of the house, a *Ketsueki* of fifty-one and who had been against the whole business since February. "What is a seven-year-old going to do for this district? Answer me that."

"Nothing," said his aunt. "He is going to do nothing. That is what the position *is*, and you have been told four times."

"Then why are we going?"

"Because the shelter petitions came through him. Yokkaichi is in. Suzuka is in. Neither of those has happened in ninety years, and both of them happened since he was three." She was doing something to a sleeve and did not look up. "Because your daughter's *Chiyu* came in badly at nine and the Torii woman fixed it in an afternoon, and the Torii woman is in this district because this district has been worth being in for six years, and you have never once asked yourself why."

"That is not the boy! That is the shrine."

"It is *both*. You know it is both. You are being difficult because you are frightened."

"I am not frightened!"

"You have painted a gate that did not need painting," said his aunt, "and you have been out to look at it twice a day since Tuesday. Sit down and let me do your collar."

  

There was a second kind of argument and it happened in the houses with no Flame in them at all, and it went the other way, and it was worse.

Nobody had told those households anything. The notice said *recovered from the long illness of his infancy* and that is all fifty-nine thousand people had. So the question in those kitchens was not whether a seven-year-old could hold a district. It was: **why are we invited?**

The answer, given at the door by the shrine's own people, was that all households of Namimori were called.

*All* households. Not the flame-active ones. Not the old families. Not the four hundred who knew. The clause is four centuries old and says *all households of Namimori*, and Musashi had read it in January, and had considered the modern practice, which was to send the notice to the families that mattered, and had gone and looked at the original and had found that the modern practice was about a hundred and forty years old and was not in the text.

She sent it to everybody. Sixty thousand people. It cost the shrine eleven weeks of work and a sum of money that Maro described out loud as *obscene*.

Four thousand people came who had no idea what a Flame was, and four hundred came who knew exactly, and the *Kotei* vault was up over every one of them, and only the second group could feel it, and the first group had been living under it their whole lives and calling it *good weather*.

  

He came out at the eleventh hour of the morning and the town made a noise.

Not a cheer. Namimori does not cheer. It was the sound a crowd makes when it has been holding a breath it did not know it was holding. It went across the forecourt in a wave. Afterwards nobody could agree on who started it.

He was in the twelve layers.

That was the first shock, and it was a shock even to the people who had known it was coming, because the *junihitoe* [1] is not a garment a modern person sees. It is eleven or twelve robes worn one over another, each showing a hand's width at the collar and the sleeve. The colours are read as a chart is read. The whole thing weighs between eleven and fifteen kilos. The shrine had one. It had been remade four times in four centuries. The outermost layer was rewoven the previous autumn by a workshop in Kyoto that asked no questions and charged accordingly.

The second shock was the mask. It was white. It covered the upper face. It had no expression at all, because it is not supposed to have one. A *Kohaku* under recognition is not a person that day. He is a position, and the mask is there to make that literal, and the rule that goes with it is the one everybody in that forecourt had been told twice: **you do not look at his eyes.**

The third shock was that he was extremely small.

  

That was what did it. Not the silk and not the mask.

They had been told he was seven. Everybody had been told he was seven. It was in the notice. And there is a great deal of difference between being told a number and watching a child in fifteen kilos of ceremonial silk cross a forecourt without staggering, while ninety households go down on the stones.

The parents did the worst. They went first and they went hardest. A woman near the front, a Kurokawa *Ketsueki* with a boy of eight at home, put her hand over her mouth and had to be steadied by her husband. Two of the Miura *Hogo* had come with their jaws set, because their family had lost a boundary over this. They looked at the top of the dais and forgot entirely what they were annoyed about.

Somebody at the back said, out loud, into the silence, "*He's tiny*."

It carried. It carried the whole width of the forecourt, in the acoustics that shrine has. About four hundred people heard it. Not one of them laughed. The priestess conducting did not turn her head. And afterwards it was generally agreed that whoever it was had said what everybody was thinking, and should not be identified.

The parents among them were entirely certain that their own children had never been that small.

  

The rite itself takes four hours and most of it is not interesting.

There is a reading of the line. It took forty minutes and covered eleven generations, and Azuma Maro delivered it from memory. The shrine's second priestess has done it from memory since the Muromachi period, and she was not going to be the one to break that.

There is the recitation of the shelter, in which the four petitioning districts stand and are named and answer. Yokkaichi answered second. It answered in the voice of a man in a wheelchair whose brother had pushed him four hundred metres up a shrine approach not built for it. His *Ketsueki* came up so hard when he spoke that two people in the row behind him sat down.

There is the naming, and it goes through all four columns of the table, and it is the only occasion in a person's life when all four are said aloud together. That is the part outsiders think is the whole thing.

*Sawada Tsunayoshi.* The name his father's line gave him, from a shogun.

*Sawada Masaya.* The name his mother's line gave him, with the eight in it. The shrine has been putting that eight in its children's names for four centuries and nobody outside the family has ever asked about it.

*Kohaku.* What he is. The word had not been said aloud in that forecourt in four centuries and about ninety people flinched.

*Tenmei.* What the district will call him from that day, in formal settings, for the rest of his life.

And then the last one. It is not a name and it is not in the written order of service. The priestess adds it or does not add it at her discretion, and it had not been added in this valley since the fourteenth century.

Sawada Musashi, sixty-four years old, in full formal dress, in front of ninety households and four districts and a man who was not a person, said:

"**And the Sky of Namimori.**"

  

The town lost its composure at that point and did not recover it for about eleven minutes.

  

*

  

Musashi had been conducting rites for thirty-two years, and had carried a *Gen'ei* through every one of them, and she had never once, in all of it, been unable to feel her own hands.

She got through the naming, with her *Gen'ei* laid flat across the forecourt the whole four hours, which is a thing no one thanked her for and no one noticed except Maro and the man who was not a person. She got through the shelter and the reading and the four hours. She did it in a voice that carried to the back wall. By any measure available to anybody in that forecourt, she conducted the finest rite any of them would see in their lives.

She was watching her grandson the entire time and what she saw was ruining her.

He was *fine*.

That was the whole of it and it took her until the third hour to let herself put it in those words. He was fine. Fifteen kilos of silk on a seven-year-old frame. Four hours on his feet. Ninety households on the stones in front of him. A mask he could not see the edges of. And he carried all of it as a competent adult carries a difficult morning.

His *Kotei* was even. His breathing was even. His hands were correct. His *Kotei* did not so much as ripple, and Maro was reading it from the second row and told Musashi afterwards that it had been flatter than her own. When the Suzuka woman fumbled the response, he waited exactly the right length of time and then moved. It looked like grace. Musashi knew from thirty-two years of doing this that it was not grace. It was *judgement*, and judgement of that kind takes decades to build.

  

She had thought she was prepared.

She had a physician's report in a lacquer box and she had read it eleven times in a year. She had sat outside a door on the kitchen passage most nights since the autumn. And right up until eleven o'clock that morning she had believed she understood the thing Vayaskara Devan wrote.

She had understood it as a fact about the past. *Nobody has asked him how he is.* A failure, hers, correctable, being corrected.

Standing in her own forecourt in the third hour of the rite she understood it as a fact about the present tense and it went through her like cold water.

Because a seven-year-old **cannot do this.**

That is not an opinion. Musashi had put four children through preliminary rites in her career, none of them under eleven, and all four had cried, and two had been sick, and one had run, and every single one of it had been *normal*. A child in that much silk, with that many people watching, gets frightened. The ritual has room for it. There are four separate points in the order of service that exist purely to let a frightened child stop for a moment without it being a failure.

He used none of them.

He did not use them because he did not need them, and he did not need them because somewhere, somehow, at some point she could not see and had not been present for, her grandson had learned to do this.

*Where*, she thought, in the third hour, with ninety households in front of her. *Where did you learn it. You have been in this house every day of your life.*

  

She found Devan afterwards, in the corridor behind the hall, sitting on a step with his stick across his knees, looking like somebody who had been awake since four.

"You saw it."

"I saw a child conduct himself extremely well at a long ceremony," said Devan. "That is what I put in the file this morning and it is what I will put in the annual, and I would like to be asked what I actually think before I say it, because I have been wrong about this family before."

"I am asking."

"Then: yes. I saw it." He rubbed his face. "Sawada-san, I have been coming here a year and I have written four pages and one sentence at the end of them that you have apparently read a great many times, and I have thought about that sentence every week since I wrote it, and I want to revise it."

"Revise it."

"I wrote that he had not been examined for how he is." Devan looked up at her. "That was too kind. I was being a guest. What I should have written is that your grandson has been *competent* since before he could walk, and that nobody in this house has ever asked where a person that age gets competence from, because it has been useful to all of you and because it looks like a gift."

  

Musashi stood in the corridor with four hours of silk and incense on her, and took her time about answering.

"Say the rest of it."

"I do not know the rest of it. That is the honest position and I intend to hold it." He got up, badly. "But I will tell you what I know from a great many years of practice, and it is this. Competence in a child is a *response*. It is not a temperament and it is not an inheritance, whatever the four hundred people out there think. Nobody is born able to hold a room."

"He was born able to hold a room."

"Then something happened that I do not know about," said Devan, "and I have decided that it is not mine, and that it is possibly not yours either, and I am going to go on treating the sleep and the weight and the vigilance for the next twenty years and never once ask, and I want you to understand that this is a *decision* and not an omission."

"Doctor."

"Mm."

"I have been asking that question for seven years."

"I know," said Devan. "I have watched you do it. May I say something extremely rude and then go and lie down?"

"You may."

"You are the only person in that forecourt who did not enjoy this morning," he said, "and you have been carrying that alone since about eleven o'clock, and there are four other people in this town who saw exactly what you saw, and you have never once compared notes with any of them because every one of you thinks she is the only one who noticed."

  

He was correct and it took Musashi four days to act on it and she did it badly.

She sent four letters. Indra, who replied inside the hour with two sentences and came in person that evening. Azuma Maro, who came without replying and arrived first. The Hibari boy, who was seven, and who received a formal letter from the head of the Sawada house and answered it in his own hand in four lines and turned up in his good coat.

And Kawahira, who did not reply, and who was in the cold seat under the north shutter when she arrived, having got there first, as he did.

They sat in a room in the middle of March.

"I have called you here," Musashi said, and then stopped, because it sounded absurd, and because Maro had already begun to laugh at her.

"You have called us here," said Indra, "because you watched him do four hours in the *junihitoe* without a wobble and it frightened you, and you have realised that this is not a thing you can fix by improving the house."

Musashi put her hands flat on the table.

"…Yes."

"Good. It frightened me in the winter of his fifth year and I have been carrying it alone since, which was extremely stupid of me." Indra looked round the table. "Who else."

"Since the rabbit," said Kyouya.

"Since the first time he ever said thank you to me," said Maro, and was not laughing any more.

Kawahira held still a moment. Then: "Before I met him."

  

That is the conversation that would matter later and almost none of it was useful at the time.

They established that all five of them had seen it. They established that not one of them had raised it with another, over six years, in a town where everybody knows everybody's business, and they spent some time on why, and the answer was not comfortable.

Maro got there first, because Maro always got to the unpleasant things first and enjoyed them.

"Because he is *load-bearing*," she said. "Say it properly, all of you. This town has four districts in shelter, a vault over its head, two hundred families that would be somewhere else, and a Hibari main line that has stopped counting down to leaving. Every single one of those things rests on a seven-year-old, and every one of us has known for years that something is wrong with how he came by what he is, and not one of us has pulled on it."

"That is not why I did not pull on it," said Indra.

"No," Maro agreed. "It is why *I* did not. I am telling you what mine was. You tell me yours."

"Mine was that he was frightened when I got close, and I decided I would rather not know than frighten him twice."

"Mine was the same," said Musashi.

"Mine," said Kyouya, "was that it is his."

Everybody looked at him.

"You are all discussing whether to open something that belongs to him," said Kyouya, seven years old, in his good coat, with his palms flat on his knees. "He has one field, one fox, and one thing he has never told anybody. I gave him the field. I am not taking the third one."

  

They spent an hour on what to actually do and got nowhere, which Musashi had expected and which was still worse than expecting it.

"We could ask him," said Maro.

"Who is *we*?" said Indra.

"One of us. Any of us. It is not complicated, Yasuragi-san, you sit down and you say *what happened to you*, and then you deal with whatever comes out."

"I have done that." Musashi did not look up. "Twice. He was four the first time and five the second. Do you want to know what happened?"

"Yes."

"He answered." Musashi moved her cup a few degrees, which is a thing she does. "Both times. Immediately, completely, in detail. And both answers were *true*, and both of them were about something else, and I did not work out what he had done until the following week, and the second time it took me a month."

"Then you ask again," said Maro. "You are the head of this house."

"I am also the woman whose *Gen'ei* he has been living under since he was born," said Musashi, "and I want everybody in this room to sit with that for a moment, because it is the part I have been sitting with since eleven o'clock. If I put a question to that child and hold it there, he will answer it. I am extremely good and he is seven and I would get something." She put the cup down. "And then I would have taken it, rather than been given it, and he would never hand me anything again as long as I live."

There was a silence with a great deal in it.

"That is the correct analysis," said Indra, "and I hate it."

  

"What about the physician?" said Maro. "He is outside all of this. He is not flame society, he has no house, he wants nothing, and he asks that question every spring and writes down that he got nothing. Let him keep asking."

"He is already doing that."

"Then let him do it for twenty years!" Maro slapped the mat. "You are all looking for something to *do*, and there is nothing to do, and there is a man from Kerala who has already found the only thing that works and none of you have noticed because it is slow and it is boring and it does not feel like helping."

"It is not helping," said Kyouya.

"It is a…"

"It is not *helping*, it is *waiting*, and those are different, and I would like somebody in this room to say the true version out loud." He had not raised his voice. He did not, ever. "None of you can fix it. He has to. All any of us can do is make sure that when he starts, there is somewhere for it to go."

Azuma Maro, seventy-one years old, looked at a boy of seven and did not say anything clever, which for her is an event.

"When did you get like this?" she said.

"February," said Kyouya.

  

Musashi asked the question she had been holding since the corridor.

"Where did he learn it?"

Four people looked at her.

"That is the thing I cannot put down. He was *competent* at four. Not clever, competent, and there is a difference and I have spent a career learning it." She spread her hands on the table. "A child learns to hold a room by being in rooms he cannot leave. That is the only way it is done. And he has been in this house every single day of his life, and I have been in it with him, and I would have *seen* it."

"Unless it did not happen here," said Indra.

"He has never been anywhere."

"He has never been anywhere *in six years*," said Kawahira.

The room went quiet in a way that Musashi would think about for a long time afterwards, and Kawahira looked at his cup, and Maro's ring went cold on her hand, which she did not mention until the following week.

"That is a strange sentence," said Musashi.

"It is a precise one," said Kawahira, "and it is the last one I am going to say on the subject."

  

Kawahira spoke last, and he spoke, for once, without any of the machinery.

"You have all made the correct decision for four different reasons and it does not matter," he said, "because the thing you are circling is not a secret you can decide to open. It is a *shape*. It is going to come out on its own, in about eleven years, in the worst possible way, in front of somebody who has not earned it."

"Then we should…"

"You should do exactly what you are already doing," said Kawahira. "Ruriko-san puts a room on a busy corridor. Ayame-san stands there at meals. The Hibari child hands over ground that cannot be used. The physician comes every spring and asks his three questions and writes down that he got nothing." He turned his cup. "None of that touches the shape. All of it is building the only thing that will matter when the shape arrives, which is a house he can afford to be seen in."

There was a long pause.

"That is the most useful thing you have ever said," said Maro.

"Yes," said Kawahira. "I have been saving it."

Inside the mask it was very warm and smelled of new lacquer, and Tsuna spent the first hour working out how much of the forecourt he could see, because that is what you do.

Eleven degrees to either side. Nothing above about four metres. Nothing at all below his own knees, which was the dangerous one and which he had allowed for by counting the steps in the dark the night before, four times, until Ayame came out and asked him what he was doing and he told her a lie.

He had done this before. Not this rite; there is no version of this rite in the life he remembered, and the differences kept catching him. But the *shape* of it, a room full of people who need you to be a fixed point for four hours, he had done perhaps two hundred times, and the muscle for it had come back inside about nine minutes and he had felt it come back and had been quietly sick about that for the rest of the morning.

That was the thing nobody in the forecourt could have known and which he could not have explained to anybody in it.

He was not being brave. He was not enduring anything. He was doing an extremely familiar job in unfamiliar clothes, and the only part of it that cost him anything was the fifteen kilos, and the fifteen kilos were a *physical* problem, and physical problems are the easy kind.

  

He got through the reading by doing arithmetic.

He got through the shelter by looking at Fukuda's brother's hands on the chair, which he had not known would be there, and which cost him about four seconds in the middle of a response and which nobody noticed except his grandmother.

He got through the naming by not listening to it.

And then his grandmother said *and the Sky of Namimori*, which was not in the order of service, which he had read, and he had read the fourteenth-century precedent too because of course he had, and the forecourt made a noise that he felt in the boards through fifteen kilos of silk.

That one got through the mask.

He had prepared for the whole morning. He had four contingencies for a stumble and two for being sick and one, which he had not told anybody about, for the possibility that his core would slip in front of four hundred people. He had not prepared for four hundred people making that sound, because it had not occurred to him that they would, because in every version of his life that he could remember a room full of people had wanted something from him and this room did not want anything at all.

Behind the mask, where nobody could see and nobody was permitted to look, Sawada Tsunayoshi stood absolutely still for eleven seconds and had no idea whatsoever what to do with his face.

  

The feeding took until dark and it is the part the town remembered.

Four hundred kilos of rice. Fourteen women and nine household staff and, by the middle of the afternoon, about sixty volunteers who had simply started helping and could not be stopped. Four *Ketsueki* on the fires, two *Chiyu* on the cuts, and a *Getsufuji* nobody had invited who turned out to be extremely good at carrying. The Miura men, who had come angry, carried tables for three hours. A *Hogo* boy of about twelve ran the water and refused to be relieved, and by evening had a burn on one wrist that two separate *Chiyu* offered to close and that he would not let them touch, because he wanted it.

Ayame stood in the middle of it and directed with a ladle and did not sit down once between six in the morning and nine at night, and at some point in the evening a woman from Suzuka asked her whether she was the head of the house.

"No," said Ayame.

"Then who *are* you?"

"I stand there," said Ayame, "while he eats," and went back to work, and the Suzuka woman told that story for thirty years and never once understood it.

  

Devan watched the whole thing from a step with a plate he did not want and made notes he never showed anybody.

Indra found him there in the late afternoon.

"Well?"

"Four thousand people who do not know what a Flame is have spent a day being fed by a shrine and looking at a child," said Devan. "Do you know what that is, Amba-mol? In my profession we would call it a *public health intervention* and we would be very pleased with ourselves."

"It is a rite. It is four centuries old."

"It is a rite that says *all households* and has been quietly meaning *the good families* for a hundred and forty years, and a woman of sixty-four went and read the original in January and put it back." He ate something off the plate. "That is not liturgy. That is somebody deciding, in the twelfth month of preparing the largest event of her life, to make it four times harder on purpose."

"…And you are saying this to me why?"

"Because you will not tell her and neither will I," said Devan, "and one of us should have said it out loud somewhere."

  

Inside the mask, in the fourth hour, he did the thing he had been putting off.

He counted the room.

Not the people. He had done the people in the first hour, out of habit, the way he had counted every room he had walked into for two lifetimes: exits, sightlines, who is armed, who is frightened, who will move first. Four hundred Flame-active in a forecourt is a serious quantity. The *Ketsueki* alone would have been a district-level problem if anything had gone wrong, and there were eleven *Getsufuji* in the third row who could have made it a regional one.

Nothing was going to go wrong. He had established that by the second hour and had not known what to do with himself since.

So in the fourth hour he counted the other thing, which was: **how many of these people are here because I asked.**

The answer was none.

He went through it twice because he did not believe it. The four districts had petitioned. Had he gone to them? No. They wrote to his grandmother, and one of them wrote twice before anybody replied. The families had come because the shrine called them and the shrine had called them because a four-century-old clause said so. Ayame had recruited fourteen women and he had not known about it until eleven days beforehand. The Hibari were on the walls because a seven-year-old decided they would be. Had anybody asked for that? They had not. Was there a single *Yasuragi* or *Getsufuji* on that perimeter he had personally placed? Not one. Kyouya had not consulted him. Nobody had consulted him about any of it.

Sawada Tsunayoshi stood on a dais in fifteen kilos of silk in the fourth hour of his own recognition and discovered that he had not organised it.

He had organised everything since he was three. That was not an exaggeration and he had the book to prove it. And here were four thousand people in a forecourt, and a rite four centuries old, and four hundred kilos of rice, and not one line of it was in the five hundred and sixty-eight.

*I am not load-bearing here,* he thought, behind a mask nobody was permitted to look at. *This would have happened without me.*

He had spent six years terrified of being unnecessary and had built an entire life around not being, and the thought arrived without any of the fear attached to it, and he stood there and turned it over and could not find the fear anywhere.

He would spend eleven weeks looking for it. He never did find it. It was, he decided much later, the single strangest afternoon of either of his lives.

  

He went up the north slope in the evening, still in most of it, because getting out of the *junihitoe* takes two people and forty minutes and he had escaped after the fifth layer.

The plum was over. It had been over for a fortnight and the ground under it was a mess and nobody had swept it because nobody was allowed to.

He sat down on the largest rock in four remaining layers of ceremonial silk with the mask on the grass beside him. Fuyu poured out of the collar and went and did something in the leaf litter. Down below, the town was eating four hundred kilos of rice.

*Plan 569*, he thought. *Nothing. Deliberately. See what happens.*

He had written that in the winter and had felt ridiculous and had not crossed it out, and he had come up here perhaps eleven times since and had not once managed to do nothing for longer than four minutes, and he was aware that this was funny and had not yet decided whether it was funny at his expense.

Today he managed about forty.

  

Kyouya came up the north slope at dusk, over the fence rather than by the path, because the path would have meant announcing himself.

"You left."

"I left after the fifth layer. It takes two people and forty minutes and I did not have forty minutes in me." Tsuna did not turn round. "Are you here to tell me off?"

"No. I am here because this is my rotation." Kyouya sat down on the second rock at two and a half paces, which he had never once explained. "Kojima has the north-east until eleven. It is his birthday and he would not swap."

"You are guarding a field on the day of a district rite."

"I am guarding a field *every* day. Today is not different." A pause. "Did you eat?"

"…Not yet."

"*Four hundred kilos of rice*," said Kyouya, with feeling, "and the person it was for has not eaten. Go down."

"In a minute."

"You have said in a minute for forty minutes. I have been on the wall since seven." Kyouya produced something from inside his coat, which turned out to be a *rice ball wrapped badly in a napkin*, and put it on the rock between them without comment, and looked at the town.

Tsuna looked at the rice ball for slightly too long.

"Kyouya."

"Do not."

"I was only going to say…"

"I know what you were going to say and I have decided that this is not a fourth gift," said Kyouya. "There are three. The system has three. If you make this a fourth I will take the field back and you will have nothing again, and then where will we be?"

"…Ruined," said Tsuna. "Both of us. Structurally."

"Yes." Kyouya's chin came up about four millimetres. "Eat the rice."

  

He ate the rice.

That is worth recording because Ayame had spent a year standing in doorways and had not managed it once without being present, and a seven-year-old with a bruise on his jaw did it in eleven seconds with a napkin and a threat, and neither of them noticed, and Fuyu did.

  

Nagi found him because Nagi always found him, and sat down without being told, which had taken four months and which he still noticed every time.

"Masaya-sama."

"Nagi-chan."

"Are you all right?"

He opened his mouth to say yes, and got as far as the shape of it, and then stopped, because he had spent a year now being asked a version of that question by a physician from Kerala and had four times failed to produce anything at all, and had started, in the last few months, to be able to tell when the answer he was about to give was the wrong kind.

Nagi waited. She was seven and she had learned it from him and neither of them knew that.

"I don't know," he said.

"That's all right."

"…Is it?"

"You said it to me," said Nagi, "when I asked you what the fox was for. You said *I don't know* and you didn't put anything after it." She was picking at the grass. "It was the best thing anybody had ever said to me and I have not told you that until now because it seemed like a lot."

  

There was one more thing that day and it happened in the last hour and only three people knew about it.

Kawahira came up the slope, which he did not usually do, and did not sit down, which he also did not usually do.

"Sawada-san."

"Kawahira-san."

"Your grandmother said a thing at the naming that is not in the order of service."

"I noticed."

"You will not have noticed all of it." He put his hands in his sleeves. "The fourteenth-century precedent you read is not the last time it was said. It is the last time it was said *in this valley*. It has been said four times elsewhere since, and I was present for two of them, and on both occasions the person it was said about was dead within nine years."

Tsuna went very still.

"Is that a warning?"

"No," said Kawahira. "It is an *observation*, and I have been sitting on it since eleven o'clock this morning deciding whether to make it, and I have decided to make it because you are the only person in this district who will do the correct thing with it, which is nothing at all."

"…That is a great deal of confidence in a seven-year-old."

"It is no confidence whatsoever in a seven-year-old. It is a considerable amount in *you*, which is a different quantity, and one that this town has been carefully not measuring for six years." He turned to go. "Both of them were killed by people who wanted what they held. Nobody wants what you hold, Sawada-san, because nobody outside four hundred people knows you hold anything, and that arrangement ended at eleven o'clock this morning in front of four thousand witnesses."

"Kawahira-san."

"Mm."

"Did my grandmother know that?"

Kawahira stopped on the slope.

"She read the fourteenth-century precedent in January," he said. "The other four are in a Kyoto archive that has not been open to the public since 1946." A pause. "So: no. And I am not going to tell her, and neither are you, because she said it for the correct reason at the correct moment and it was the finest thing I have watched a human being do in about ninety years, and I am not going to be the one who spoils it."

  

Down in the town the lanterns were going up, and somewhere on the east wall a seven-year-old was sitting at two and a half paces of vertical equivalent with a bruise on his jaw from something entirely unrelated. Ayame was fighting the boiler. Kazuki was explaining a persimmon to a woman from Suzuka who had made the mistake of asking. Maro had gone home. Indra had not.

And in the small hall, two *Gen'ei* and a *Yasuragi* and a *Getsufuji* of seven and a man who was not a person had spent an evening establishing that all five of them had known for years and that not one of them was going to say anything, and had gone away having agreed to keep doing exactly what they had been doing, which is the least dramatic decision in this entire account and the only one that would turn out to matter.

Nobody told him.

He would find out in eleven years, in the worst possible way, in front of somebody who had not earned it, exactly as predicted.

But that is not this arc.

  

Nagi asked one more thing before it got fully dark and it was the last question anybody put to him that day.

"Masaya-sama. What is a *Kohaku* for?"

He looked at her.

"Sensei says a *Kohaku* is the person and *Kotei* is the Flame, and I know the whole table," said Nagi, in a rush, because she had clearly been holding it for hours. "*Ketsueki* Kogyoku Arashi. *Yasuragi* Seigyoku Shigure. *Chiyu*, *Hogo*, *Getsufuji*, *Gen'ei*, *Kotei*. All seven, both columns." She counted them off. "I did it for Maro-sama in February and she gave me a sweet, But that is what it *is*. Everybody at the rite today knew what it is. Nobody said what it is *for*."

The *Kotei* vault was going the colour it went at that hour, over sixty thousand people, four hundred of whom could see it.

"I have been trying to answer that for a very long time," said Tsuna.

"How long?"

"Longer than you would believe if I told you."

"And?"

"And every answer I have come up with has been a *job*," he said. "Hold the town. Hold the *Kotei* vault. Keep four districts in shelter. Be useful enough that the arrangement holds." He turned the mask over on the grass with one finger. "They are all true. Every one of them is true and I do them and I am going to go on doing them. And I have started to suspect, in about the last four months, that not one of them is the answer to your question, and that I have been answering a different question for six years because I could not do yours."

Nagi thought about it with the enormous seriousness she brought to everything.

"That is like the fox," she said.

"…Yes. That is exactly like the fox."

"Fuyu is not *for* anything and Fuyu is still here." She was picking grass again. "So maybe a *Kohaku* is not for anything either, and everybody just needs one, and they are embarrassed about it so they give it jobs."

Sawada Tsunayoshi, aged seven, sat on nine hundred square metres of useless ground and was comprehensively overtaken for the second time that year by a small child.

"Nagi-chan."

"Yes?"

"Would you write that down for me?"

"…Why?"

"Because I am going to want it in eleven years," said Tsuna, "and I will not believe it if it is in my own handwriting."

  

"Nagi-chan."

"Mm."

"Did you eat?"

"*Yes.*" She sounded so offended that Fuyu surfaced out of the leaf litter to look at her. "Ayame-san has been standing there. She has been standing there for a year. She stands there for *everybody* now, it is not just you, the whole house has to eat in front of her like a *prison*."

"…She does that on purpose, you know."

"I *know* she does it on purpose!"

"Good," said Tsuna. "Don't tell her I told you."

  

They sat there while it got dark, on nine hundred square metres of useless ground with a dead plum blossom all over it, and below them a town that had spent six years not looking at him ate its dinner and talked about how small he was.

He did not have an answer to the third question.

He had a field, a fox, a room on a noisy corridor, a physician arriving every spring, a seven-year-old who asked him if he was all right, and one line in a book that said *nothing, deliberately, see what happens*, and not one of those was in any of the five hundred and sixty-eight.

It was not a solution. He was extremely clear about that, and would remain extremely clear about it for another four years, and would be wrong about it the entire time.

  

There is an epilogue to the day and it is one line in the shrine's record, in Musashi's hand, entered on the twentieth of March.

Under *attendance*, where four hundred years of priestesses have written a number of households, she wrote a number of **people**.

**4,211.**

Nobody has ever asked her why she changed the unit. Maro noticed inside a week and did not mention it for nineteen years, and when she finally did, over tea, in a small hall, Musashi said only that a household is a thing you count when you are counting *who matters*, and that she had gone off the practice.

  

The four sheltered districts became six within two years.

The Torii *Chiyu* line took two apprentices from outside the family for the first time since the war, one of them a *Chiyu* girl from Yokkaichi whose father held a gate from a chair.

The Kurokawa head of house, who had argued for three days and painted a gate that did not need it, wrote to the shrine in the autumn asking whether the *Kohaku* might be willing to look at his daughter's *Chiyu*, which had come in badly at nine and never quite settled. He did not use the word *ask*. He used a construction four hundred years old that means *I am putting my house in your hands and you may say no*, and Musashi read it out loud to Indra twice.

The answer was yes. It was always going to be yes and everybody in that room knew it, which is precisely the thing the five of them had spent an evening in March failing to work out what to do about.

  

And in the spring of the following year, in the annual, under *findings*, Vayaskara Devan wrote four lines.

Sleep: improved, from four and a half hours to five and a half. Not adequate. Continue.

Core: outside my competence. The *Kotei* readings are Azuma-sama's and are appended. She reports no change in six years and considerable change in eleven weeks, and declines to explain either, and I have stopped asking.

Weight: up two per cent. Continue.

Vigilance: unchanged.

And then, in the space where a physician records the patient's own account, in handwriting that had got worse and would keep getting worse for nineteen more years:

*Asked the third question. Patient said: "I have thought about it and I do not think the word applies to me."*

*This is the first answer of any kind in two years. It is wrong. I have written it down exactly as given.*
