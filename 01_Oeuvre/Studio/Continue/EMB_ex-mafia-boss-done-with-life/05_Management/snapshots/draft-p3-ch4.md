# Chapter 4: Local Rain Guardian Escorts Her Sky To A Negotiation. Watches Him Be Wrong. Has Feelings About It

Hibari Indra had been awake since four, which was not unusual, and had spent the first hour of it not moving, which was.

The not moving was the discipline. Her mother taught it to her at six, in a courtyard in Kerala, in a family that had produced physicians for nine generations and exactly one Rain. Amba looked at her daughter's flame and understood inside a week what she was looking at. A pure *Yasuragi* [1] is a gift. In nine generations the family had produced two *Chiyu*, a *Hogo*, and a great many people with no Flame at all who were better physicians than any of them. A pure *Yasuragi* running backwards is not a gift. It is a weapon with no handle.

So: the discipline. An hour a day, every day, for thirty-one years. Lie still. Do not fix the blanket. Do not get up when the mind says get up. Let the thing in you that wants to *move* stand there wanting.

People in Namimori thought she was calm. Musashi thought she was calm, and Musashi had known her eleven years and was the second most perceptive person Indra had ever met. Her own son did not think she was calm, because her own son had inherited the engine without the brakes and could see the family resemblance from the inside.

At five she got up and did the forms in the dark. At six she wrote to Junpei about the Yokkaichi escort, and to the *Hogo* who ran the eastern road about a lorry, and to her son's tutor about a matter she did not intend to discuss with her son. At seven she stood in the Sawada kitchen and watched a six-year-old refuse breakfast, which was a thing she had watched perhaps two hundred times and had never once got used to.

  

"You are going to eat that."

"I am going to eat some of that."

"Masaya-sama." Ayame did not look up from the stock. "Yasuragi-san has been standing there for four minutes and she has a face on."

"I do not have a face on."

"You have the face," said Ayame.

Indra had, in fact, been standing there for four minutes with a face on, and she had done it on purpose, and she was mildly annoyed at being caught by a woman whose entire attention was on a pot. That was the thing about this household. It ran on a surveillance network so total and so affectionate that a person could not put a cup down wrong in it.

The boy ate about two thirds, which was a victory, and went to get his coat, and Indra went after him.

"Tell me about the man."

"In the car."

"Tell me now, and then tell me again in the car, and then I will have heard it twice."

He gave her a look over his shoulder that was much too old for the face it was on. She had catalogued it as *item four* some years back. About eleven weeks ago she had decided she was never going to ask about it.

  

Here is the decision she had made, and she wanted it clear in her own head, because a Hibari who cannot state her reasons is a Hibari who is about to do something stupid.

She had knelt in the snow at the end of the winter and sworn to a six-year-old. In the old form, with her mother's blade across her palms, in front of witnesses. Nobody had asked her to. Several people would have preferred she did not.

She had spent every day since auditing the decision. That was not doubt. That was maintenance. You check a bridge after you build it. You check it hardest in the first year. Anybody who tells you that checking means you regret the bridge has never built one.

The audit had produced a list of oddities. She kept it in her head, numbered, because writing it down would have been a betrayal and thinking it was not.

One: he did not want anything. She had spent thirty-one years in and around powerful people. Every one of them wanted something. Most wanted several things, and you could steer by it. He wanted the town to be safe. That was all he wanted. She had probed it four separate ways, including once by offering him something a *Kohaku* his age should not have been able to refuse, and it was not a cover for anything.

Two: he had never once asked her for a favour that was for him.

Three: he read a room like a man of sixty. Not like a clever child. She had known clever children. She had raised one. Clever children read rooms *fast* and *wrong*. He read them slowly and correctly, and he had done it since he was four.

Four: the look. Over the shoulder. The one that said *I have already had this conversation with somebody, possibly with you, possibly some time ago.*

Five, and this was the one she had put at the bottom because it was the one that mattered: he was frightened of something specific, constantly, and it was not any of the things a child in his position should be frightened of.

She had elected not to ask about any of them. She wanted the reasoning stated plainly somewhere, and her own conscience was the only place available. **She had not sworn to a set of facts. She had sworn to a person.** If the facts turned out to be strange, that was the facts' problem.

  

Yokkaichi is fifty minutes by road and about four hundred years by every other measure.

It had petitioned into Namimori's shelter three years ago, along with Mie and Suzuka and one other. Those petitions are why this town now has a rite that takes four hours. A district petitions when it cannot hold its own gates. Yokkaichi has two *Ketsueki* lines, one *Hogo* line, and a *Chiyu* family who do the district's healing and have never once been to the assembly. It gives up the right to settle its own disputes above a certain weight. In exchange it gets to say, when somebody comes for it, that it is under the Sawada shrine. For three years that sentence had been enough, which still astonished her.

The man they were going to see was called Fukuda. He was the head of the largest *Ketsueki* line in Yokkaichi. He had been obstructing the shelter agreement for eleven months. Nobody could work out why.

"He is buyable," said Tsuna, in the car.

"Go on."

"Not with money. He is buyable with *position*." He had the folder on his knees and was not looking at it, which meant he had stopped needing it some days ago. "He holds his family by fear and by being the only one who talks to outsiders. If we make him the channel, formally, in writing, he will sign inside a month. He will also make it look like a concession."

"You are very certain."

"I am."

"Masaya-sama," said Indra, "you are six."

"I am aware of the shape of the argument, Indra-san, and I would like to skip to the end of it if that is all right."

She let it go, because she had been letting it go for three years. The last four times he was this certain he had been correct in every particular. On one occasion he named the month a man would default and was out by six days.

  

The escort protocol is four hundred years old and about ninety per cent theatre, and Indra had never once been tempted to skip a line of it.

A Sky does not travel alone. That is the first rule and the only one anybody outside flame society has heard of, and outsiders assume it is about assassination. It is not. It is about *Kotei* [2]. A Sky in an unfamiliar district is a slope in a landscape that does not expect one, and the people around him start leaning without noticing they are leaning, and if there is nobody in the party whose job is to notice, somebody harmonises by accident in a stranger's front room and two families have a problem for a generation.

That was the Rain's job. Not the sword. The sword was for the eleven minutes a decade when the sword was for something.

So Indra sat in the front and kept a thin skin of *Yasuragi* over the car for fifty minutes, which is exhausting and which nobody thanks you for, and which she would have described, if anyone had asked, as holding a lid slightly above a pot without touching it.

She had done it for Musashi's husband, who was a *Hogo* and did not need it and was gracious about it anyway. She had done it twice for Musashi in the bad year, when a *Gen'ei* of that grade going unstable in a moving vehicle would have been a district matter. She had never done it for a passenger who *noticed*, and this one had noticed at four, and had said thank you once and never again, because she had told him after the first time that thanking the Rain for the Rain was like thanking a road for being flat.

He had thought about that for a week and then started saying it to other people about their own work, which was the single most irritating and most characteristic thing he had ever done to her.

  

They arrived at eleven. The house was old and badly kept and the garden was immaculate, which told her something before anybody spoke.

Fukuda came out himself, which was wrong. He was about sixty and his *Ketsueki* was low and steady and tired. He bowed to a six-year-old for slightly longer than protocol asked. He apologised for the state of the entrance hall. None of it was performance.

Indra stood at the position, one pace and to the left, and read the room, and began quietly to revise.

The tea was good and came out fast, which meant somebody had been watching the road. The house held four people and three of them were young. Against the wall of the second room stood a folded wheelchair. It had been folded recently, and put where a guest would not have to look at it.

She did not say anything. It was not her negotiation.

  

The offer took nine minutes and Indra could have recited it, because she had heard four versions and had argued him out of two.

It was elegant. The shelter agreement had eleven clauses and ten of them were signed, and the eleventh named a channel: one person, from Yokkaichi, formally recognised by the Sawada shrine, through whom all district business above a certain weight would pass. It carried a stipend. It carried a seat at the winter assembly, which meant a box on Musashi's chart, which meant four hundred years of standing order rearranged to fit a man in.

For a *Ketsueki* line in decline it was the best offer anybody had made Yokkaichi in a century, better than anything the Kurokawa had ever put to them and better than what Mie had signed, and Tsuna had costed it at eleven months of resistance collapsing inside four weeks.

Fukuda listened to all nine minutes with his hands on his knees. He did not interrupt. He did not look at the folder. Twice he looked at the door of the second room, which Indra saw and Tsuna, facing the other way, did not.

It went wrong in the eleventh minute.

Tsuna made the offer. He made it well and in the correct order. The whole approach rested on one assumption: that the man in front of him wanted to be the door between Yokkaichi and the world.

Fukuda listened to all of it with his hands on his knees.

Then he said, "I do not want to be the channel."

"…Ah," said Tsuna.

"I want to stop being the channel. That is the entire reason I have been holding up your agreement for eleven months, Tenmei-sama, and I have written to your grandmother about it four times, and I assume the letters did not reach you."

Indra, from one pace and to the left, watched the back of a six-year-old's neck and saw something she had not seen in three years of standing behind him.

He went still. Not the polite stillness he used with district heads, and not the terrible flat one he had used on the Estraneo. This was neither. This was a man walking into a room he had walked into a hundred times and finding the furniture moved.

  

"The letters reached me," said Tsuna.

"Then I do not understand the offer."

"No. Neither, at present, do I." He put the folder down on the mats and did not pick it up again for the rest of the morning. "Fukuda-san, I am going to ask you to start at the beginning, and I am going to ask you to assume that I know nothing, because it appears that I do not."

That was the moment Indra decided she would follow this person into anything.

She thought about it afterwards for a long time and could never improve on it. It was not the swearing in the snow. The swearing in the snow had been a judgement, and judgements can be revised. This was the other thing. She had spent her whole life among people who, when the ground moved, reached for whatever story let them keep standing where they were. She had never once, in thirty-one years, watched somebody put the folder down.

  

The beginning took forty minutes and it was not complicated. That was what made it bad.

Fukuda's line had held the Yokkaichi gate for two hundred years and it had been dying for thirty of them. Three sons. The eldest went to Nagoya and does not answer letters. The second came into no Flame at all and married out. The third was in the second room, and had been in the second room since a road accident nine years ago. A *Ketsueki* of considerable strength who could not hold a brush.

There was nobody to hand the gate to. There had not been anybody for nine years. The man spent those nine years being the only one who talked to outsiders, not because it gave him power, but because there was nobody else left to do it… and he had never been able to say so out loud.

"I did not obstruct your agreement," he said. "I obstructed the clause about the channel, because the clause about the channel required a name, and I do not have a name to put in it, and I could not think of a way to write that down that did not read as *my house is finished*."

The room held still for a moment.

Then Fukuda said, "I have been told that the shrine is thorough. I assumed you knew all of this and were being delicate."

"I was not being delicate," said Tsuna. "I was being wrong."

  

She got him out at half past one with an agreement that was worse than the one he had come for and real in a way the other one had not been.

He had improvised it in about eleven minutes, on the mats, without a folder, and Indra had watched the whole thing with her hands folded and her face doing nothing and a great deal going on behind it.

She had watched him negotiate perhaps thirty times and she had never watched him do it without a plan, and it turned out to be a completely different animal.

The planned version was quiet. He would put a thing down, wait, put a second thing down beside it, and the other party would arrive at the conclusion on their own and feel clever about it. It was slow and it was extremely effective and Musashi had once described it as *being bullied by furniture*.

This was not that. This was fast and audible and slightly reckless. He asked eleven questions in nine minutes. He got two of them wrong and said so out loud, both times, without stopping. Twice he started a sentence, cut it off mid-clause, and started a different one, which she had not once heard from him and which she found she liked enormously.

And he gave things away.

He gave up the channel clause entirely. He gave Yokkaichi a shared gate, which had no precedent and which was going to give Musashi a headache lasting into the following year. The Sawada shrine would hold the outward-facing half until such time as the Fukuda line named a successor. No deadline. He put in no deadline at all, which cost him something and which the old man noticed.

And then he asked about the third son.

He asked badly. He asked like somebody who had decided to ask and then discovered he had not planned the sentence. She had never heard that from him before. What came out was: "Does he still have the Flame?"

"He has all of it," said Fukuda. "Every scrap. The *Chiyu* woman who comes on Thursdays says it is the strongest Flame she has ever put a hand on and she has been saying it for nine years. It has nowhere to go."

"Then he can hold the inward half."

The room stopped.

"He cannot write."

"I did not say write. I said *hold*." Tsuna had gone forward about six inches, which she had learned to watch for. "A gate is not paperwork, Fukuda-san, and you and I both know it. This country has spent four hundred years pretending otherwise, and that is a separate argument, and I am too young to win it. Somebody in a house has to be the thing the house is anchored to. That is a *Ketsueki* standing in a doorway saying no. It does not require a brush."

  

On the way out she saw him.

The door of the second room was open about a hand's width, which it had not been when they arrived, and there was a man in his thirties in the chair behind it, and he was looking at them go past with an expression Indra recognised immediately and did not enjoy recognising.

She had worn it herself for eight years in this country. It is the face of somebody watching a conversation about their own life happen in another room.

His *Ketsueki* was enormous. Not loud. Enormous, in the way the sea is enormous when it is flat.

That was the part she took home with her. It was banked right down, the way a household banks a fire it has no intention of using, and it was still the strongest Storm-side flame she had felt outside the Kurokawa main line. Nine years in a back room. Nobody had come to test him, because his father could not bring himself to ask anybody to, and because a district in decline does not invite the neighbours in to look at what it has left.

Tsuna stopped.

He did not go in, which she was glad of, because going in would have been the six-year-old's move and would have cost the old man something he could not afford. He stopped in the corridor, and turned, and bowed. Properly. The full one, the one a Sky gives to a household's flame-holder, which he had used perhaps eleven times in three years and never once to somebody who had not been introduced.

The man in the chair did not move for a second. Then he got his hand up off the armrest, about four inches, which was clearly the whole of what was available to him, and Tsuna straightened and went out.

Nobody in that house said anything about it. Fukuda saw it. Fukuda's face did something and got put away.

  

They were nine minutes down the road before he said anything.

"I had that entirely wrong."

"Yes."

"I have never…" He stopped. He was looking out of the window at nothing, and his hands were in his lap, and he was six years old and he looked, for the first time in her acquaintance, exactly six years old. "I was certain. Indra-san, I was not guessing. I was *certain*. I have been certain about eleven things this year and right about eleven of them, and I built four months of work on this one."

"You were wrong about a man's household," said Indra. "It happens."

"It does not happen to me."

He said it without any arrogance whatsoever, which was what made it land. He said it the way a person reports a symptom.

Indra drove for a while. She was aware that the correct thing to do, the thing Musashi would have done, was to ask a gentle question and open a door and wait. She was also aware that she was not Musashi, and had never once successfully done that in her life. The boy had a nose for a door being opened and would go around it.

So she said, "Good."

He turned his head.

"…Sorry?"

"I said *good*." She did not take her eyes off the road. "You have been wrong once, that I have seen, in three years. Do you have any idea how frightening that is to stand next to?"

  

"I am not following."

"Then I will be plain, and I will only do this once, so attend." She changed down for the hill and did not hurry it. "There are two kinds of people who are never wrong, and I have married into one and buried three of the other. The first kind is not really never wrong. They are simply never *checked*, and everybody around them spends a lifetime doing the checking in silence. I have met perhaps forty of those and I have buried three."

"And the second?"

"The second is the one that frightens me," said Indra. "The second kind is right so often that the people around them stop bringing them things. Why would you? He has thought of it. He thinks of everything. You come to him with a worry and it turns out he has a plan for it filed under a number. You go away feeling foolish. The next time, you do not come at all." She took the corner. "And then one day the thing he did not think of arrives, and there is nobody standing near him who is in the habit of speaking."

The car went along for some time.

"That is a very specific description," said Tsuna.

"It is my late husband's family in four sentences, and I married into it at twenty and I have been dismantling it ever since!" It came out considerably louder than she had intended, and she heard it, and she did not apologise, because the discipline was for four in the morning and not for this. "Do you know what my son does? He *asks me things*. Constantly. Terrible questions, at meals, in front of guests. He is six and he asks me whether I was frightened. I answer him. And every single time I answer him I am rebuilding something my husband's grandfather spent forty years knocking down."

  

He was quiet for a while after that. She let him be.

Then he said, "You are not calm at all, are you."

"No."

"How long have you been not calm?"

"Thirty-one years," said Indra. "An hour every morning, lying still, letting it want to move. My mother started me at six. She had nine generations of physicians behind her and no idea what to do with a Rain. So she treated it as a condition and gave me a regimen. It was the correct decision and I have never told her so."

"Does it stop?"

"It does not stop. It becomes work you can do." She glanced at him, once, and back to the road. "Everybody in Namimori thinks I am a serene woman. Your grandmother thinks I am a serene woman, and she reads people for a living. It is the single most useful lie in my possession, and I did not tell it. They arrived at it themselves. I have let them."

"Why are you telling me?"

"Because you were wrong in front of me this morning," said Indra, "and you have been sitting in this car for twenty minutes deciding what it means about you, and I would like to get in front of the answer you are building."

  

The rain got heavier. She put the lights on early, which she did not usually do, and which was a small concession to a road she had driven perhaps four hundred times.

"Eight years," she said.

"Sorry?"

"You asked me once, when you were four, why I did not like the Kurokawa. I told you it was a long story and gave you a piece of fruit, which I would like to apologise for now, because it was the sort of thing that is done to children and I do not do that." She changed lanes. "Eight years. That is how long it took this town to stop treating me as something the Hibari had brought back from abroad."

"…Ah."

"My *Yasuragi* is the purest in this district and it is rare in the Hibari line, and both of those facts were used against me for eight years by people who could not have held a veil for four minutes." Her hands were correct on the wheel, which was the discipline, and her voice was not, which was not. "I was a curiosity. Then I was a scandal. Then Keita died and I was a widow with a two-year-old and a great deal of authority I had not been given permission to have, and that was the worst of the three, and it lasted nineteen months."

"What ended it?"

"I stopped waiting for it to end," said Indra. "I put four of them in their place in one afternoon in front of witnesses, and I did it in a way that could not be undone or apologised for, and it was extremely satisfying and I would do it again tomorrow!" She heard herself, and let it stand. "Your grandmother told me afterwards that she had been about to do it for me, and that she was glad she had not, because it had to be me. She was right. She is right about that sort of thing roughly ninety per cent of the time and it is unbearable."

The wipers went. The boy was quiet in the way that meant he was working.

"Indra-san."

"Mm."

"Why are you telling me the bad ones?"

"Because you only ever hear the finished versions of people," said Indra, "and you are building a model of this town out of finished versions, and you are going to make a mistake with it one day that will be much larger than a Yokkaichi channel clause."

He did not say anything to that for so long that she checked on him in the mirror.

"Indra-san."

"Mm."

"When I was wrong. In the room." He was picking at the seam of his coat, which was a thing he never did. "What did you see?"

She thought about it properly, because he had asked properly, and because he was going to hold whatever she said for a very long time and she wanted it to be worth holding.

"I saw a person put a folder down," she said.

"That is not an answer."

"It is the entire answer, and you will understand it in about eleven years." She put the wipers on, because it had started, in the way it does on that road. "I have been in rooms with men who were wrong. Every one of them reached for the version of the story where they were not. Every single one, Masaya-sama. Including me. Including twice this year." A pause. "You put it down and asked him to start at the beginning. I have never seen it. I have been looking for thirty-one years and never once seen it. I saw it this morning from a person who does not come up to my elbow."

  

"That is a very kind reading of a man discovering he does not know anything."

"It is not kind. Kind would be telling you it did not matter." She indicated, and turned, and the rain came on properly. "It mattered. You lost the channel clause, you gave away a deadline you should have kept, and the shared gate is going to cost the shrine two years of arguments with the Kurokawa, who will be *delighted*."

"…Yes."

"And it was still the correct morning's work, and both of those are true, and you are going to have to learn to hold two true things at once because your position is going to be nothing else from here on."

He let out a short breath. She could not read it.

Then he said, "I have been told that twice this month," in a tone she did not follow at all, and put his head back against the seat, and did not explain, and she did not ask, because she had made a decision about that eleven weeks ago and she was a woman who kept her decisions.

  

Kyouya was on the wall when they got back, which meant he had been there some time, because Kyouya did not wait anywhere he could be seen waiting unless he wanted the waiting noticed.

He looked at the car. He looked at Tsuna getting out of it. He said, "You lost."

"I did not *lose*, Kyouya, it was a negotiation. Negotiations do not have a winner, they have a…"

"You lost," said Kyouya, with enormous satisfaction. He came down off the wall and fell into step at exactly the distance he had been maintaining for four months and had never once explained to anybody.

"How did you know?"

"You came back in the front of the car," said Kyouya, without turning round. "You sit in the back when you have won. You sit in the front when you are thinking."

Tsuna stopped walking.

"…How long have you known that?"

"Since you were five." Kyouya kept going, which meant Tsuna had to catch up, which Indra suspected was the entire design. "It is not a secret. It is a pattern. You have seven."

"*Seven?*"

"You do not know your own patterns. That is a strange thing about you and I have decided not to be annoyed by it." He held the door, which he did for nobody, and which he would have denied doing. "Tell me about the man."

"He was not what I thought."

"Good," said Kyouya. "The ones who are what you think are boring, and you are only interesting when you have been surprised, and you have not been surprised since the winter."

Indra watched her son escort a six-year-old across her Sky's grandmother's forecourt like a man supervising a valuable and slightly disappointing animal, and she thought, not for the first time, that she was going to have a great deal to answer for.

  

She reported to Musashi in the small hall, because that was the protocol and because she wanted to.

"He was wrong," she said.

"About Fukuda?"

"About all of it. The man is not obstructing, he is dying. His line is dying with him. And there is a *Ketsueki* in a back room who has not been outside in nine years!" She sat. "Masaya-sama built four months on a reading of that man that was wrong in every particular. When it came apart he put the folder down inside four seconds. Then he improvised something better and worse."

Musashi was quiet. Then she said, "How did he take it?"

"Badly. Not the failure. The being wrong."

"Ah."

"It frightened him, Gen'ei, and I do not know why, and I am not going to ask." Indra took the tea she had not requested and which had appeared anyway, because Ayame. "He said, *it does not happen to me*. Like a man reporting a fever."

Musashi turned her cup a quarter turn and did not drink from it.

"There is something he thinks he knows," she said. "There has been since he was four. It is why he is never wrong. I have never been able to get near it and I have stopped trying. The two times I got close he was so frightened that I decided I would rather not know than do that to him twice."

"Three times, in my case."

"You also?"

"I also."

The two of them sat in the small hall with their tea and their eleven years of acquaintance and their entirely separate sets of the same conclusion.

"He is going to have to be wrong more often," said Indra.

"I know."

"It is going to be very bad for him and very good for him and I have no intention of protecting him from it."

"I know that too," said Musashi. "I have been protecting him from it for six years, which is the single largest mistake I have made as his grandmother, and I have known it for about a fortnight." She put the cup down. "Do not stop."

  

The Kurokawa found out on Thursday, which was four days faster than Musashi had budgeted for, and were exactly as delighted as Indra had promised.

A shared gate has no precedent. That is not a small phrase in a system where the seating at the winter assembly is governed by a well nobody can find. It means that for the first time in four hundred years the Sawada shrine holds a piece of another district's gate directly, and every family in the shelter now has a question about what that implies for their own, and three of them have decided the answer is *very good* and two have decided it is *extremely bad*.

Musashi spent eleven days on it. Indra watched her do it and did not offer to help, because the help was not hers to give and because watching Gen'ei fight an administrative war is one of the genuine pleasures available in this town.

"I have written to the Miura," Musashi said, at the end of the second week. "I have told them that the arrangement is temporary, exceptional, and reflects the particular condition of the Fukuda line."

"Is it temporary?"

"It is *not*," said Musashi, with enormous satisfaction, "and by the time anybody establishes that, it will be nine years old and there will be a word for it."

There is a version of this town, Indra thought, where those two would have been unbearable.

She had thought that before and had never followed it anywhere, and she followed it now, in the small hall, over tea she had not asked for. A shrine that never made mistakes and a Sky who was never wrong and a Rain who could put a district to sleep. Sixty thousand people under a *Kotei* vault, held up by three women and a child, none of whom had ever been meaningfully told no.

It was not a comfortable thought. She had it anyway, because she was thirty-seven years old and had learned that the thoughts you refuse to have are the ones that make your decisions later.

  

She raised it with him a month afterwards, in the garden, with no preamble, because preamble was Musashi's instrument and not hers.

"Who tells you no?"

He looked up from the ledgers. "Sorry?"

"It is a simple question, Masaya-sama, and you are stalling, which means you heard it." She sat down on the stone, which was cold, which she elected to ignore. "Your grandmother does not, because she is frightened of frightening you. Maro does not, because she thinks arguing with you is a spectator sport. Ayame tells you to eat, which is not the same thing. Kyouya tells you no about approximately four subjects and none of them are policy."

"You tell me no."

"I have told you no four times in three years and I have been correct three of those times and you did not take the fourth," said Indra. "That is not a system. That is an accident, and it is currently the entire mechanism by which this town is prevented from doing whatever a six-year-old decides on a Tuesday."

He put the brush down.

"…That is a genuinely terrifying way to put it."

"I have been working on it for a month."

"It shows!"

"Thank you." She let it sit for a moment. "I am not asking you to build a council. You would build a council, and it would be an excellent council, and you would manage it so well that inside two years it would agree with you about everything and you would be exactly where you are now, except with more paperwork and a false sense of security."

"That is…" He stopped. "Yes. That is what I would do."

"I know. So do not." She stood up, because the stone really was cold. "Do the other thing."

"Which is?"

"Be wrong in front of people on purpose, and let them watch you survive it," said Indra, "and do it before it is expensive, because at the moment the only person in this town who has seen it is me, and I am going to die eventually and you are apparently not."

  

There was one more thing about that morning and she did not work it out for a month.

He had bowed to a man in a chair. Fully, the way a Sky bows to a household's flame-holder, in a corridor, to somebody who had not been introduced and who could not return it.

Indra had assumed at the time that it was courtesy, or pity dressed as courtesy, and had been slightly disappointed in him for it. She revised that in the second week and revised it again in the fourth, and what she landed on was this: he had not been bowing to a sick man. He had been bowing to a *Ketsueki* who held a room, which was a thing that was true and which nobody in that house had said out loud in nine years, and he had done it in the only sentence available to him, which was his body, in a corridor, in about two seconds, on his way out of a negotiation he had just lost.

That was not kindness. She wanted to be exact, because being inexact about him was how people got him wrong in both directions.

It was *accuracy*. He had looked at a household and seen who was actually holding it up, and had addressed that person correctly, and the fact that it was also the kindest thing anybody had done in that house for a decade was a side effect he had probably not noticed and would have been embarrassed by.

She thought about item one on her list. *He does not want anything.*

And she thought, in the small hall with her tea going cold: *no. He wants something. He wants everybody to be counted, and he does not include himself in everybody, and that is not a virtue, that is a fault line, and one day something is going to come through it.*

She wrote that one down. It was the first thing on the list she had ever written down, and she put it in the box with her mother's letters, where nobody would look for four hundred years.

  

She went back to Yokkaichi in the spring, alone, without telling anybody, because it was her gate and her judgement and she was not going to make a six-year-old responsible for it.

The arrangement said the Fukuda line would hold the inward half. It did not say who, and it did not say *how*, and the shrine had signed a document promising that a district's inner gate would be held by a man nobody outside that house had laid eyes on in nine years. That is not a formality. An inner gate is the thing that decides whether a family's *Ketsueki* stays a household matter or becomes a regional one, and the same is true of a *Hogo* line or a *Chiyu* one, and if it is not held, the first bad winter takes the whole line and takes two neighbouring ones with it.

So she went to find out, and she went the way the Hibari go, which is at ten in the morning, announced, in the good coat.

  

His name was Fukuda Ren and he was thirty-four and he had not spoken to anybody outside his family since the accident.

He received her in the second room, which had been cleared, and which had a scroll on the wall that had been hung that morning judging by the dust line. His father stayed in the doorway. Indra asked him, politely, to leave, and he did, and it took him four seconds longer than it should have.

"You are here to test me."

"Yes."

"My father thinks you are here to be gracious."

"Your father," said Indra, "is a decent man who has been alone with a problem for nine years, and he has stopped being able to imagine anybody arriving for a reason that is not pity. That is what nine years does. I do not hold it against him and neither should you." She sat down. "I am East Gate of Namimori. I hold the eastern approach of a town of sixty thousand and I have held it since before your accident. I am here because I signed a piece of paper that says you can do a version of my job and I would like to know whether I lied."

Something in his face came loose.

"Nobody has said it like that."

"Nobody has said it at all, which is the actual complaint, and I would like us both to notice that before we start."

  

The test is not complicated and nobody enjoys it.

She put out her *Yasuragi*. Not the thin skin she used in the car. The other one, the working one, laid down across the room the way she would lay it across a road, and she took it up to about a third, which was more than she had used on a living person in four years.

An Inverse Rain does not soothe. That is the entire content of the word and the reason her mother treated her like a patient. What Indra puts into a room is *pressure to move*. It gets into the space under the ribs where a person keeps the decision to stand up, and it pushes, and the effect on an untrained flame-active adult is that they get to their feet without knowing why and then feel foolish and then feel angry.

At a third, a strong *Ketsueki* should have been up and across the room.

Fukuda Ren went white, and his hands closed on the arms of the chair, and his *Ketsueki* came up around him like a wall going up in a hurry.

And he stayed exactly where he was.

Indra held it for eleven seconds. Then she took it off, all at once, which is the rudest way to do it and which is also the only honest way, because a gate that only holds while somebody is being gentle is not a gate.

He sat there breathing.

"Well," said Indra.

"*Well?*" He got it out on the second attempt. "You attacked me!"

"I tested you. I would have done exactly that to a nineteen-year-old Kurokawa in a courtyard with his whole family watching, and I have, and he cried." She was aware that she was enjoying this and elected not to correct it. "Do you know what you just did?"

"I did not move!"

"You could not have moved," said Indra. "That is the point. Every *Ketsueki* I have ever tested passes that by standing up, because standing up is what a Storm does, and it tells me nothing except that his flame works." She leaned forward. "You had nowhere to put it. So you put it into the room. Fukuda-san, I have been doing this for nineteen years and I have felt perhaps four flames that came up like that, and two of them were on the Hibari main line, and one of them was my husband."

  

She stayed until the middle of the afternoon and it took most of that for him to believe her.

That was the part she reported to nobody. Not the test, which went in the record, and not the finding, which went to Musashi that evening and into the shelter file by the end of the week. The part she kept was the forty minutes in the middle where a thirty-four-year-old man tried four separate times to explain to her why she must be mistaken, and got angrier each time, and finally stopped in the middle of a sentence and put his hand over his eyes.

"Nine years," he said.

"Yes."

"Nobody came."

"No."

"Why did *he* come? He is a *child*. My father has written to that shrine four times and got nothing, and then a six-year-old walks into my house and bows at me through a doorway and I have an office."

Indra thought about how to answer that, and discovered that she had been thinking about how to answer it since the car.

"Because he is building something," she said, "and I have watched him build it for three years and I still cannot tell you what it is. He does not want a network. He does not want owed favours, he has actively refused four. What he does is find the person in a house that the house has stopped counting, and he puts them back in the count, and then he goes away and does it somewhere else."

"That is not a strategy."

"No," Indra agreed. "It is not. I have tried very hard to read it as one and it does not read."

She stood, and put her coat on, and looked at a man in a chair with the strongest banked *Ketsueki* in three districts.

"He was wrong about your father, you know," she said. "Completely wrong. He came here with four months of work built on a man who does not exist and he had to throw all of it away on the mats in front of me." She got the door. "You are the thing he built out of being wrong. I would think about that, if I were you, on the days when it is hard to get up."

  

Indra went home at nine, and did the forms, and lay down at ten, and did not sleep for a while.

She thought about a folder on a mat. She thought about a man in Yokkaichi who had written four letters and had assumed the silence was tact. She thought about her mother in a courtyard in Kerala, taking her daughter's wrist and finding a pulse that would not settle. Amba had decided inside a week to treat a Flame as a patient.

Then she thought, because she was honest and because the hour was late: *I did not stay for Namimori.*

She had said she had found where home was, and it was true, and she had let everybody understand it as the town. The town was part of it. The town had been good to her eventually, after eight years of not being. She would burn for the town and had said so.

But she had knelt in the snow for a different reason. She had watched a five-year-old spend an entire winter making arrangements for the safety of people who did not know his name. She had watched him be embarrassed when anyone thanked him. And she had understood, with the whole of her *Yasuragi*, that she was looking at somebody who had decided at some point she could not see that he did not count.

And a person who does not count will not stop. Not ever. Not for anything.

So somebody was going to have to stand next to him and be extremely difficult about it, for a very long time. Indra had looked around the room that winter, and done the arithmetic, and knelt.

*Drench or drown*, she thought, and turned over, and slept.
