# Chapter 3: Local Sky Offers A Fox Gainful Employment. Is Turned Down Flat. Takes It Extremely Badly

The fox told him about the pear tree on a Tuesday, and Tsuna answered it out loud, and Nagi was in the room.

That was how he found out. Not through any careful process of observation. Not because anybody sat him down. He said "it did, actually, I remember the pears" to what appeared to everyone else in the room to be a length of rope asleep on his wrist. Then he looked up into the face of a six-year-old who had gone very carefully blank.

Nagi did not say anything about it. Nagi never said anything about anything, which was one of the four or five things about her that worried him and one of the two he had no idea how to approach. She looked at him. She looked at the fox. She wrote another line of her exercise, and her brush stayed steady. Tsuna sat with the sick little jolt of a man who has just found the shape of a hole he has been walking around for three months.

Everyone could see Fuyu. That was established in the first week, loudly, by Maro. Three separate texts and one very expensive letter from Kyoto had informed her that this was not how a *kudagitsune* behaved. She took it personally.

Nobody else could hear it.

  

He tested it that afternoon, because he was who he was and testing it was cheaper than wondering.

He did it badly. He was aware, even while doing it, that he was doing it badly. A man who ran an organisation of eleven thousand people for four decades ought to be able to design a cleaner experiment than *ask three people a stupid question and watch their faces*. But he was six. His hands were small and everything took longer than it should. There were days when the gap between what he could plan and what he could physically execute made him want to put his head through a wall.

He asked Ayame whether she had heard anything odd.

"Odder than usual?"

"Just… odd."

"Masaya-sama," said Ayame, without turning round, "you have a talking rope on your arm. Kazuki has started saying good morning to a persimmon tree by name. The Hibari boy left a *pheasant* on my step this morning." She put the lid on. "Define odd!"

"It doesn't talk."

"It squeaks."

"That's different," said Tsuna, and heard how it sounded, and let it go.

Kazuki said no, and asked whether it was the boiler again. Maro said yes immediately, at length, and turned out to mean the Kurokawa. Musashi looked at him for slightly too long. Then she said that if he wanted to tell her something he could tell her, and if he did not want to tell her something he could do that too. Either way she would like him to eat.

So: nobody. He had a *kudagitsune* that the entire household could see. It fed off the entire household's kitchen. It had been given a name by committee. And it spoke to exactly one person in the world.

He wrote this down. Then he looked at what he had written and did not know what to file it under. That was a sensation he had not had in a long time, and he did not enjoy it.

  

The whisper was not a voice. He wanted that clear in his own head, because the alternative had implications about the state of his mind that he did not currently have the resources to investigate.

It arrived like remembering. That was the closest he could get. A name comes back to you an hour after you needed it, complete and unbidden and in your own mental handwriting. It was like that, except that it came from outside, and it came about things he had never known.

The pear tree, for instance. There was a pear tree in the northeast corner of the Vongola grounds, planted the year Lambo turned twenty. It never fruited properly. Tsuna walked past it perhaps nine thousand times across forty years. He had not thought about it once since dying. He had not thought about it much before dying.

The fox thought about it. The fox had, apparently, liked it. In whatever way a Sky lion made out of a man's own flames can be said to like a tree. And it offered this up on a Tuesday afternoon like a thing making conversation.

*The pears were bad but the shade was good.*

"It did, actually," Tsuna said. "I remember the pears."

And that was all of it. A dead man's fruit tree. A child in the corner with a steady brush. And the discovery that he had been talking to something out loud in his own house for three months.

  

Here is what he could not make fit.

The fox knew the previous life. All of it, as far as he could establish, and he established a great deal over the following weeks with a thoroughness that would have embarrassed him if anyone had been watching.

It knew the layout of the Vongola mansion, including the two corridors rebuilt in his fifties. It knew his Guardians the way he knew them, which is to say by their habits and not their attributes. Hayato putting salt in his coffee when he was thinking. Takeshi never once sitting with his back to a door in fifty years. Kyouya out of the building for eleven of those years and in it, without being asked, on the day it mattered. It knew the name of the woman who ran the kitchens in Sicily for nineteen years. It also knew that Tsuna had never once asked her about her children. He had not. That arrived one evening without invitation and cost him most of a night.

It knew things he had forgotten. That was the part that unsettled him structurally rather than emotionally. He had assumed, without ever putting it into words, that his memory was the archive and everything else was commentary. It was not. There was a second copy, and the second copy was in some places better, and it was wound around his wrist eating stolen egg.

And it knew nothing at all about this one.

He tested that properly, because it mattered. He asked it about the Estraneo house, where he had personally been eleven weeks ago. Nothing. He asked it about Kyouya. Nothing, and not the nothing of refusal, but the flat blank nothing of a question landing in a room with no furniture. Did it know what a Cloud was? Yes, in the Italian sense, in the sense the Vongola had used the word. Did it know that the Japanese for it was *Getsufuji*, or that there was one over the east wall at this moment killing a pheasant? No. He asked it about the sakaki, and the fox looked at the sakaki when he pointed, and did not know it from any other tree.

*It is not an oracle*, he wrote, in the small book he kept for things he could not tell anyone. *It is a memory that walks. It ends where I ended.*

Then he sat and looked at that sentence and felt, for the first time in six years, actively frightened of something he had brought into his own house.

  

The drift took him eight days later, in the record room, in the middle of an inventory.

He knew the shape of them by now. There was a warning, if you could call it that. It was less a warning than a change of weather. The room going slightly further away, sound arriving a half-beat behind sight, his own hands reporting from somewhere down the corridor. Musashi called them the floaty episodes and had done since he was two. Maro called them *Kotei* slippage in the record and something ruder out loud. He called it Adjusting, mostly to himself, mostly because a word with a job in it was easier to hold than a word without one.

What actually happened was that his core, four hundred metres away and eleven centuries old, remembered that it was not where it should be. A *Kotei* core is not a reservoir. It is a place the rest of a person is hung from, and his was hung from a tree.

He put the brush down before it got bad, which was the whole of the technique and had taken him four years to learn. Then the floor went a long way off. The *Kotei* dome over Namimori pulled at something in his chest that was not in his chest. The ancient neutral weight of the sakaki leaned on him as weather leans on a roof.

And Tsuna went out.

Not unconscious. He was never unconscious. That would have been kinder and everybody in the house would have preferred it. He simply went *thin*, and stayed thin. The world carried on at a distance of some considerable metres. There was nothing whatsoever to be done except wait for his own body to finish arguing with itself.

They lasted between ten minutes and four hours. He had records.

This one lasted ninety seconds.

  

He came back as one comes out of deep water, all at once and wrong-footed, and found that he was on the floor of the record room with his cheek against the boards and a weight across his throat.

The fox had wound itself around his neck. Not tightly. It had gone twice around. It was pressed the length of his windpipe with its head under his jaw. And it was making a sound he had never heard it make, which was not the pleased squeak and was not the annoyed squeak. A low continuous thing, more felt than heard, like a cat pitched down into the bone.

And his core was quiet.

Not home. He wanted to be accurate about this later and he was accurate about it at the time, lying on the floor with his heart going and a fox on his throat. The core was not back in the crux. It was still out there in the sakaki, still bound, still four hundred metres away and holding up a *Kotei* vault over sixty thousand people.

But it had stopped *pulling*.

Something was standing between him and it, taking the strain, and it weighed about as much as a large cucumber.

Tsuna lay on the floorboards of his own record room and worked it out in the flat administrative part of his brain that never stopped no matter what else was happening.

A *kudagitsune* lives in a vessel outside its keeper's body. That is not a metaphor. It is a load-bearing fact about the animal.

He had been handed a second container for a thing that did not fit in the first one.

He said, to the ceiling, "…Oh, you absolute *bastard*."

He meant Kawahira. He was almost certain he meant Kawahira.

  

That was the last drift for a great while.

He did not believe it at first. He gave it two weeks, then a month, then six, and kept the records the way he had kept the records since he was three, and the column stayed empty. Six years of entries in a hand that got steadily better and then a page with nothing on it.

He should have been happy about it.

He was not happy about it. He spent an unreasonable amount of the following month working out why. The answer, when it arrived, was so unflattering that he wrote it in the small book in the smallest hand he had.

*I did not plan this. I did not build it, I did not negotiate for it, I did not trade anything for it. It arrived in a tube on the step and I did not even open it for two hours.*

*I do not know how to be given things.*

He looked at that for a while. Then he added, because it was true and because there was nobody to be embarrassed in front of:

*Reborn would have found this extremely funny.*

  

Musashi found out in the third week, because Maro told her, because Maro had been running the numbers.

That was the arrangement and had been since he was two. Maro kept the record of his *Kotei* the way a harbourmaster keeps tides: a column of figures taken twice a month at the second shrine, in a hand that got smaller every year, going back six years without a gap. She had never once told him what the figures were. He had never once asked, which was a courtesy in both directions and which they had never discussed.

She turned up on a Thursday and made him sit down on the mats in the small hall and did not say good morning.

"Twice a month for six years," she said. "Six years, Masaya-sama. Do you know what the variance looks like?"

"No."

"No, because you have never asked, which I have always thought was very restrained of you and which I have decided this morning was actually cowardice." She dropped a folded paper on the mats between them. "It looks like a saw. Up, down, up, down, with the down being every time the core remembered where it was supposed to be. Four hundred and eleven entries."

"And now?"

"And now it looks like a *table*," said Maro. "It has looked like a table for eleven weeks and I have been sitting on it for nine of them because I did not believe my own arithmetic, and I have had it checked, and it is correct, and I am extremely annoyed about all of it."

Musashi picked up the paper. She did not open it, which told Tsuna that she had already seen it.

"Explain the fox," she said.

  

He explained the fox.

He did it carefully and he did it incompletely, which was the shape of most conversations he had in this house, and he was aware while doing it that Musashi knew he was doing it and had elected to let him.

He told them the mechanism, because the mechanism was true and safe. What he left out was where the fox had come from, and what it had been, and what a *Kotei* flame does when it is made into a shape and then outlives the man who shaped it. A *kudagitsune* keeps itself in a vessel outside its keeper. His *Kotei* core was tethered out of his crux and had been since he was a few months old, and the pull of it was the thing that dropped him on the floor twice a month for six years. Something had come between the pull and him, and it was taking the strain, and it weighed almost nothing.

"That is not possible," said Maro, with great enjoyment.

"You are looking at four hundred and eleven entries and a flat line."

"Yes, and I am telling you it is not possible, which is a different statement from telling you it is not happening!" She was up on her knees now and going through the folder she had brought, which was substantial. "A vessel takes the Flame it is given. *Ketsueki*, *Chiyu*, *Hogo*, it does not matter, you pour it in and it sits there. It does not take the Flame of a *core*." She was jabbing the mats now. "A core is not a quantity, Masaya-sama, it is a *place*. You cannot put a place in a jar. Can you? Can anybody? Has anybody?"

"And yet."

"And yet," Maro agreed, and slapped the folder shut, and looked as happy as he had seen her in a year.

  

Musashi did the examination herself, which he had been expecting and dreading in roughly equal parts.

It went the way it always went. She put her hands on either side of his head and did not move them for a long time, and her *Gen'ei* came in around the edges of him like water finding the shape of a bowl. There is nothing pleasant about being read by a *Gen'ei* of that grade. It is not painful. It is the indignity of being a room somebody else walks through, opening cupboards, while you are the cupboards.

She had done it perhaps two hundred times. She had done it the first time when he was nine days old.

"Hold still."

"I am holding still."

"You are *narrating*, which is a thing you do when you are frightened, and I would like eleven seconds of quiet."

He gave her eleven seconds of quiet.

When she took her hands away she sat back on her heels and looked at him for long enough that he began composing something to fill the gap, which he had learned by six years old was always a mistake with her and which he did anyway, every time.

"Well?"

"Your crux is empty," said Musashi. "It has been empty since you were four months old. It is empty this morning. It will be empty in ten years. Nothing has moved and nothing has healed, and there is no *Chiyu* in this country that will move it, and I want you to stop looking at me like a person waiting for the second half of a sentence."

"I know."

"I have not finished." She said it without heat. "The *pull* is gone. Not reduced. Gone. There is a thing standing in the gap and it is not doing the work of a core, because it cannot, because it is a fox. It is doing the work of a *hand*." She looked at the animal, which was on the mats between them washing itself with total unconcern. "It is holding the rope so that the rope does not saw. Is that a *Gen'ei* thing? Is it a fox thing? Is it a you thing?" She sat back. "Three questions, and I expect an answer to none of them, which has been the general condition of my life since you were born."

"For how long?"

"I have no idea," said Musashi. "Neither has Maro. Neither has anybody, because nobody has ever done this, and I want you to hear me say the next part clearly." She waited until he looked at her. "It is not a cure. You are not better. When you are old enough to carry that core you will still have to go and get it, and that is still ten or twelve years away, and if the fox stops, everything comes back."

"Understood."

"Do you understand it, or have you filed it?"

Tsuna opened his mouth, and shut it again.

"That is what I thought," said Musashi.

  

Maro stayed for lunch and would not stop talking about it.

"There is a family in Nagano," she said, around Ayame's rice, "*Hogo* line, minor, no shrine, who kept them for nine generations. Nine. And the record says the same thing every time, which is that the animal chooses, and that it cannot be given and cannot be sold and cannot be inherited except by a bride, and that in four hundred years there is no recorded instance of one being *asked* to do something and doing it."

"None?"

"None! There are twelve recorded instances of one being asked to do something and the household subsequently losing a barn." She pointed at him with a chopstick, which Ayame saw and elected not to fight. "You will note that I am telling you this now, and not in three weeks, and you may draw your own conclusions about what I expect you to have already tried."

Tsuna, who had by that point tried nothing, and who would within eleven days try three times, said, "I have no idea what you mean."

"Mm," said Maro.

Musashi did not say anything at all, which was worse, and which he thought about for most of the afternoon.

  

So he did what he did, which was to try to give it a job.

He would like the record to show that he thought about it first. He thought about it for eleven days. He read what Maro got out of Kyoto. He read what Musashi had in the record room. He read the three pages of folklore that Kawahira produced from nowhere like a man who had not gone to any trouble. And out of all of it he built a proposal.

It was a good proposal. That was the thing he kept coming back to afterwards, sitting on the engawa with his ears burning. It was a *good* proposal.

A *kudagitsune* gathers and tells. That is the entire folkloric function: it goes about, it learns, it whispers to its keeper, it answers what it is asked. And Namimori had four gates. One of them had a Hibari on it. The other three had nothing.

He had a *Getsufuji* problem in Yokkaichi and an information problem everywhere. And here was a creature that could not be seen coming if it did not want to be, and that could not be made to talk by anybody else alive.

He put it to the fox on a bright cold morning in the north garden, carefully, at a level he thought a fox could follow.

The fox listened. He was fairly sure it listened. It sat on the stone lantern with its tail, if it was a tail, hanging down the side. It looked at him with its two black pinheads. When he finished, it made no sound at all.

Then it went and put itself in a patch of sun.

"That's a no?"

The fox put its chin down.

"Right," said Tsuna. "Well. It was only an idea."

  

He tried again nine days later, with a smaller job.

Not gates. Not information. Just Nagi. She was six, and getting good at *Gen'ei* faster than anybody was comfortable with, and she spent a great deal of her time alone. Somewhere in the first month she had decided that being unobtrusive was how she paid rent. He had not worked out how to fix that. He had a great many plans and not one of them was for that.

But a fox is a thing a child can have.

"You like her," he said. "You sit on her. You could just… be with her. That's all. That's the entire job."

The fox, from the crook of his elbow, made the pleased squeak.

"So that's a yes?"

The fox did not move.

The next morning it was on Kazuki's shoulder in the winter garden, where it had been most mornings for four months. It stayed there through breakfast. When Nagi came out to sweep, it did not go to her.

It went to her that afternoon, unprompted, and wound around her wrist for an hour while she practised. Then it left. It came back two days later. It went where it went, in an order that nobody in the household had ever been able to predict, and it declined absolutely and without any apparent malice to be scheduled.

  

The third time he lost his temper, and he had not lost his temper in this life, and he was not proud of it.

It was late. He had been at the Yokkaichi correspondence for six hours and it was going badly. It was going badly because he was six years old and the man on the other end knew it. Somewhere in the fifth hour he caught himself reaching for a memory of how the Yokkaichi families came in the first time. The memory produced nothing. In the first time they came in through Hibari Kyouya, in the year Tsuna was fifteen.

Hibari Kyouya was currently in the garden with a dead pheasant and a great deal of confidence.

He put the brush down.

The fox was on the corner of the desk, in the exact position it had occupied every night for a month. That was the one place where his sleeve would catch it if he reached across.

"You could do this," Tsuna said.

The fox blinked at him.

"You *could*! You could be in Yokkaichi and back before morning and nobody would see you. You would tell me who is actually holding that family together. I would know what to write. It would take you *one night*." He heard his own voice climbing and did not stop it. "That is a *use*. That is a good use! It is not a cruel use, I am not asking you to hurt anybody, I am asking you to *go and look at something and come back*, which is the thing you are *for*. Which is what you *are*, which is…"

He stopped.

The fox had not moved. It sat on the corner of the desk with its ridiculous fur going the wrong way in three places, and it looked at him. It did not run. It did not squeak. It did not do anything at all.

And Sawada Tsunayoshi, aged six, who had buried every person he ever loved and come back to a world that was not quite the same one, sat in a lamplit room at eleven at night. He understood exactly what he had just said out loud.

*The thing you are for.*

"…Ah," he said.

  

Kyouya came over the east wall two days after that, which was how Kyouya came anywhere, and found him on the engawa with the fox.

He stopped. He did not say hello, because he had never said hello in his life, and he looked at the animal on Tsuna's wrist with the particular attention he gave to about four things in the world.

"That is not a dog."

"No."

"It is not a weasel." Kyouya came closer, on the boards, and crouched, and got his face approximately eleven centimetres from a creature that could have gone up his sleeve. "It is not a marten. It has no shoulder."

"It is a fox. Sort of. It is a *kudagitsune*, which is a fox the way a shrine is a building."

"It is *not*," said Kyouya, with feeling, "a fox. I have seen foxes. Foxes have a skeleton." He put out one finger. The fox considered the finger with its two black pinheads, and then put its chin on it, and Kyouya went so completely still that Tsuna had to look away for a second. "What does it eat?"

"Eggs. Rice. Anything of Maro-sama's. It has views about *inari-zushi* and I have stopped fighting them."

"What does it do?"

And there it was, from a six-year-old, in four words, without any of the machinery Tsuna had been building around the question for four months.

"Nothing," said Tsuna.

Kyouya thought about this. He thought about it properly, the way he thought about everything, with his whole face involved and no hurry whatsoever.

"Good," he said.

"…Good?"

"The strongest thing in a place does not need a job." He said it flatly, as a matter of settled natural law, in the tone of a boy explaining that water goes downhill. "A thing with a job can be replaced by another thing with the same job. A thing with no job is *itself*." He took his finger back. The fox squeaked at him, the pleased one, and Kyouya's chin came up about four millimetres, which on Kyouya was a standing ovation. "It is like you."

Tsuna, who had spent one hundred and eleven days working towards that sentence from the other direction, sat on his own engawa and was overtaken by a six-year-old.

"That's not…" He stopped. "I do a great many jobs, Kyouya."

"Mm," said Kyouya. "So does the town."

Then he went back over the wall, because he had said what he came to say, and because he had a pheasant situation to attend to, and Tsuna sat where he was for some time with a fox on his wrist and his entire month rearranged.

"He is *six*," he said, out loud.

The whisper came, and it was not helpful, and he told it so.

He did not sleep. He went out and sat on the west engawa in the cold with a blanket that was not doing its job. This was becoming a habit that Ayame had opinions about. He made himself go through it properly rather than comfortably.

The fox had been called something before. It had run from the name.

He had known that on the night it happened and had put it away in the file marked *later*, and *later* had turned out to be four months, which was about standard for him.

Because here was the thing he had not let himself hold: Natsu was not a pet. Natsu had never been a pet. Natsu was *made out of him*. A piece of his own Sky given a shape, a name, and a job. The job was to be a lion. The name he chose at fourteen because it was the opposite of Reborn's *Leon*, and he had thought that was funny.

He had built a thing and given it a function and called the function its name. And then he died, and the thing came with him. Of course it did. It was *him*. It arrived in a world with no body waiting for it, went into the nearest small living creature it could find, and now it was a fox.

And it was not a lion. It was very specifically not a lion. It was small and boneless. It stole eggs. It hated water. It had gone up a wall in a straight line. None of that was Natsu, and all of it was the animal it had actually become.

But it knew the pear tree.

*It understands what Natsu understood. It knows what Natsu knew. And it does not feel that it is Natsu.*

*And I keep offering it work.*

  

Kawahira came at the beginning of the second month, as he now did, and sat in the draughty place by the north window that had a cushion in it.

Tsuna waited until the house was noisy enough. Then he said, "Your note."

"Which one?"

"You have written me one note. Seven days after the wedding. It said *this should help with your problem*."

"So it did."

"Which problem?"

Kawahira had a cup of something and was turning it, and did not appear to be attending to the conversation at all. By now Tsuna knew that to be the single most reliable indicator that he was.

"I have found, Sawada-san, that when a man is carrying two problems and you solve the one he told you about, he will thank you for a week and then get quietly worse. So I have made it a policy to be vague." He looked up. "You had a core problem. That is the one you told me about. It is the one you negotiated about. It is the one you costed, and you costed it very well. The figure you gave me was accurate to within about four per cent."

"And the other one?"

"You do not have a name for it," said Kawahira. "That is what makes it the other one."

Tsuna sat with that.

"You knew the drifts would stop."

"I thought it likely. A *kudagitsune* is a vessel that walks about; you had a core that would not sit in its crux. It is not sophisticated." He shrugged with about a tenth of one shoulder. "Any competent *Gen'ei* would have got there. There are, I grant you, not many competent *Gen'ei*."

"That is not the same as knowing what was in the tube."

"No," Kawahira agreed. "It is not."

"So you knew."

"Sawada-san," said Kawahira, "I have been alive for a length of time that would upset you. In all of it I have met exactly one creature that came back from somewhere it should not have been able to come back from." He set the cup down. "I have been watching that creature run around your grandmother's grounds for six years wearing a very small kimono."

He drank his tea.

"And then in the winter a second one turned up," he said, "with your Flame all over it and no idea what to do with itself. I did not know what it was. I recognised the *shape*. That is different, and I want to be honest with you about the difference. You are going to spend the next month deciding how frightened of me to be, and I would rather you did the arithmetic with correct figures."

  

"It won't take a job," Tsuna said.

He had not meant to say that either. It was becoming a pattern in this house and he did not care for it.

"No," said Kawahira. "I imagine not."

"You could have led with that."

"You would not have believed me. You would have gone and tried it three times." He said it entirely without malice, which was worse. "You have a habit, and I am going to name it once and then never mention it again, and you may do what you like with that. When you do not know what a thing is, you give it a function, and then you treat the function as though it were the thing. It is an extremely effective habit. It has kept this town alive for six years." A pause. "It is also how you talk about yourself, and I notice that nobody in this house has told you so."

Tsuna looked at the mats.

"My grandmother tried," he said. "Two weeks ago. On the steps behind the hall."

"Ah." Kawahira sounded, for a moment, almost pleased. "Then I withdraw the last part. This household is better than I gave it credit for, which has been the general trend of my acquaintance with it."

  

He tried, that night, to say it.

The fox was on the corner of the desk. He had stopped moving it. He had also, without deciding to, stopped putting anything in that corner. So there was a fox-shaped absence in his working space, which Nagi noticed within a day and said nothing about, because she never did.

"I'm not going to ask you again," he said.

The fox squeaked. The pleased one.

"That's not… I'm not doing it for a *reward*, don't do that." He rubbed his face. "I'm trying to say something and I'm doing it badly, and I would appreciate it if you didn't make it charming."

The fox waited.

"You're not him," Tsuna said.

It came out flatter than he meant. He tried again.

"You're not Natsu. I know that. I said it at you like an *idiot* on the second day and I've been apologising in my head ever since. I'm going to say it out loud once, so it's done. I'm sorry. That was careless. I am not usually careless and I would like you to know I'm aware of it."

The fox put its chin on the desk.

"But you were," he said. "That's the part I can't put down. You were him, and you're not him. Both of those are true at once and they don't resolve. I have been trying to make them resolve for four months, because that is what I do with things."

He stopped, because his throat had gone unhelpful, and because he was six and his body did that now without consulting him.

"And I don't know what to call it," he said. "Not what to call *you*. We did the name. The name is fine. Kazuki got it right in about nine seconds and I'd been working on it for three months, which tells you everything about me you need to know." The whisper came, quiet, and he shook his head at it. "No. No, that's not it. I'm not fishing. I mean I don't know what to call the *situation*. I've got a word for everything, that's the whole of me, that's my entire skill. And there isn't one for this."

The fox unwound itself off the desk and poured down the leg of it. It came up the front of his kimono and sat inside the collar, against his neck, with its head under his jaw.

It did not squeak.

"…Yes," said Tsuna. "That's about where I've got to as well."

  

There was one more thing, and it took him until the end of the month to be willing to look at it.

The fox knew him. Not the way the household knew him, which was as a small serious person who held up a sky and would not eat. It knew him as a man of seventy-one with bad knees and a habit of going quiet in meetings, and it had known that man for fifty-seven years, and it was the only thing in this world or the last one that had the full set.

Which meant it was in a position to compare.

He noticed it first over something absurd. He was doing the Suzuka figures, and he was doing them the way he did everything now, which was slowly and twice, and somewhere in the second pass the whisper arrived with the flat clarity it had when it was reporting rather than reminiscing.

*You used to make Hayato do this.*

"I did," said Tsuna. "He was better at it."

*You used to make everyone do everything.*

He put the brush down.

  

It was not an accusation. He wanted to be fair about that, and he made himself be fair about it for eleven days before he let himself have the other thought.

It was not an accusation because a *kudagitsune* does not make accusations. It gathers, and it tells, and what it told him was a fact with no weight attached, in the same tone it used for pear trees. The Vongola Decimo had delegated. Extensively, and by design, and it had been one of the two or three things he had been genuinely good at. He had built an organisation of eleven thousand people and he had not done eleven thousand people's work.

And here he was, six years old, running a shrine's correspondence with four districts, personally, at eleven at night, having just tried for the third time to give a job to an animal.

*You used to make everyone do everything.*

The old Tsuna had trusted people with things. He had put his Storm in charge of the accounts and his Cloud in charge of nothing whatsoever, on purpose, for nineteen years, and both arrangements had worked.

The new one had five hundred and sixty-eight plans and had shown them to nobody.

  

He said it out loud eventually, on the engawa, because there was nobody to say it to except the thing that already knew.

"I got worse."

The fox did not squeak.

"That's the actual finding, isn't it. Not that I came back wrong. Not that the world's shifted, although it has, and I'm going to be paying for that for years." He turned his hands over and looked at them, which were a child's hands, which was a joke he had stopped finding funny around year four. "I had a whole life to learn how to let other people carry things. And I did learn it. And then I got a second one, and the first thing I did was take it all back."

The whisper came, and it was not an answer, because it was not an oracle and had never once told him anything he did not already know.

"Yes," said Tsuna. "That's the bit."

He sat with it. The wisteria did what the wisteria did. Somewhere in the house Ayame was conducting a dispute with the boiler in which the boiler was losing.

"I have been six for a very short time," he said, "and seventy-one for quite a long one. I do not think I am going to be good at this."

The fox got up. It came the length of his arm and sat on his shoulder and put its head against his ear, and made the low sound, the one more felt than heard, the one that had been standing between him and four hundred metres of tether for four months without being asked and without being paid and without, as far as he could establish, wanting anything at all.

He stopped talking.

That, it turned out, was the entire point, and it had taken a fox, and he was going to be embarrassed about that for years.

  

He wrote nothing down that night, which for him was close to a religious observance.

In the morning he took the small book out and turned to the page where he kept the drift records, and looked at the empty column.

Then he turned three pages on, to the plans. There were five hundred and sixty-eight of them. He had begun them at four and had never once shown them to another living person.

He read the index. It took eleven minutes.

Every single one of them was a plan for something to *do*. Every one had a trigger, a cost, and a person attached. The person was always somebody else. The cost was always him. And there was not one entry in five hundred and sixty-eight that described a state he wanted to be in.

He turned to the end and found the last blank page and sat with the brush over it for a while.

Then he wrote, at the top, in his best hand, as a heading:

**569.**

And under it, after some time:

*Nothing. Deliberately. See what happens.*

He looked at it. He felt precisely as ridiculous as he had expected to feel, and he did not cross it out, and he shut the book.

The fox, from inside his collar, made the pleased squeak directly into his ear at a volume that was frankly excessive.

"Yes, all right," said Tsuna. "Don't get used to it."

  

Nagi found him on the engawa at the wrong end of the morning, which she did about twice a week now and which she still approached as though she expected to be sent away.

"Masaya-sama."

"Nagi-chan."

She sat down. She had got much better at sitting down. In the first month she hovered until told, every time. He made a point of never once telling her. It took four months and she got there on her own, and he counted it privately among the better things he had done with this life.

"You talk to it," she said.

He had wondered when.

"I do."

"It talks back."

"It does."

Nagi thought about this with the enormous seriousness she brought to everything, including breakfast.

"Nobody else hears it."

"No."

"Is that…" She stopped, and started again, which she did when she was going somewhere she had not planned. "Is that lonely? Or is it the other one?"

Tsuna turned his head and looked at her.

He had been asked a great many questions in two lifetimes. He had been asked by heads of families and by intelligence services and by a hitman who had made an art of it. He could not immediately recall being asked one that went in on the first attempt.

"It's the other one," he said.

"Oh. Good."

And she took out her exercise and got on with it, and did not ask a single follow-up question. The fox came out of his collar and went and lay along the top of her brush hand, where it interfered with her *Gen'ei* considerably, and where she let it stay.

  

He took it into the town at the end of the month, which he had been avoiding, and which turned out to be a mistake in a direction he had not predicted.

He went in the plain grey and the half-mask, which was the arrangement for a *Kohaku* who was officially still asleep: everybody knew, nobody said, and the mask meant that nobody had to be the person who said it. He had worn it perhaps forty times. It worked because the town had decided it worked, which was how most things in Namimori worked, and which he had stopped finding strange around year three.

The fox rode in his sleeve as far as the fish market and then came out and sat on his shoulder, in daylight, in front of about two hundred people.

The market did not go quiet. That was the first thing he registered, and he registered it with real relief, because a market going quiet is a market that has decided something. What it did instead was ripple. The woman at the second stall, a *Ketsueki* of the Kurokawa branch line whose name he knew and whose mother's name he also knew, looked at the animal on his shoulder for slightly too long and then went back to her fish with the fixed expression of somebody storing an anecdote. Two stalls down, a *Chiyu* who did the district's minor healing and who had once set Kazuki's thumb said, "*Tenmei-sama*," and bowed, and then said, to the fox, "and good morning to you," and bowed again, slightly lower, and Tsuna had to look at the ground for a moment.

By the time he reached the end of the row there were four separate people not-quite-watching him, and a boy of about nine had been sent by his mother on an errand that took him past twice, and the *Hogo* who ran the ice had wordlessly put an extra handful into the parcel, which was either a gift for a Sky or a gift for a fox and which Tsuna was never going to be able to establish.

Nobody asked what it was. Nobody asked what it was *for*, either, and he noticed himself noticing that, which was new.

  

Azuma Maro caught up with him at the corner by the second shrine, out of breath and delighted.

"You have *no idea* what you have just done."

"I bought fish."

"You walked a *kudagitsune* through the Thursday market at ten in the morning with your face half covered, Masaya-sama, and there are ninety households in this town who now believe the Sawada shrine has recovered a family spirit from the old records, and by Sunday it will be forty generations old and it will have a *name in the archive*." She was gleeful about it. "Two people have already asked me which line it came down. I said the Nagano line. I said it with a straight face and I am not sorry."

"Maro-sama."

"It is *free*," said Maro. "Do you understand how rarely a thing is free? A town that thinks the shrine has an inherited spirit is a town that stops asking why the Sawada Kohaku has been asleep for six years, and it cost you nothing, and you were not even trying, which is the part I want engraved somewhere."

Tsuna stood on the corner with a parcel of fish and an unearned reputation and a fox on his shoulder.

"I didn't plan that," he said.

"I *know*!"

"You are enjoying this a great deal."

"Gen'ei said the same thing to me on Tuesday and I told her what I am about to tell you, which is that I have been dealing with the two of you for a combined seventy years and I have earned this." She patted his shoulder, on the side without the fox, because even Maro had limits. "Go home. Eat something. Tell Ayame the *Hogo* boy gave you extra ice, she likes to know who is behaving."

  

That afternoon he went and stood in front of the sakaki for a while. He did not often do that. It made his chest strange, and there was nothing useful to be done there.

The tree was enormous and extremely old and entirely indifferent to him. His core was somewhere in it, four hundred metres from where it should be, holding up a vault of *Kotei* over sixty thousand people who mostly did not know it was there. A *Kohaku* Flame gets denser with age. He had died old. There was no version of this in which a six-year-old body took that weight, and Maro had told him so at four, and he had not believed her until he was five. It would be years before he could bring it home. A *Kohaku* flame gets denser with age, and he had died old, and there was no version of this where a six-year-old body took that weight. Ten, on his own estimate. Twelve, on Maro's, and Maro had never once been wrong about a number in her life and he had not told her he agreed with her.

He put his hand on the bark.

"I'm not going to be able to do anything about you for about a decade," he said. "I'd apologise, but you're a tree."

Nothing happened, because it was a tree.

Inside his collar the fox shifted, and the low sound came, the one more felt than heard. And the pull that had been in him since before he could walk stayed exactly where it had been for four months. Quiet. Standing about a hand's breadth off.

He had been given a thing.

He still had no idea what to do about that. He was fairly sure that not knowing was, for once, the correct answer. So he stood in the cold under four hundred years of tree and let it be a thing he had not planned.
