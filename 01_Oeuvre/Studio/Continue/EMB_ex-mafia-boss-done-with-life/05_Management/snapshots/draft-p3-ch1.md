# Chapter 1: Local Apprentice Counts The Uses Of Everything In The House. Comes Up One Short

Nagi had made a list.

She had not written it down, because writing it down would mean someone could find it, and someone finding it would mean questions. But she had made it, and she kept it in her head where it was safe, and she added to it most days.

It was a list of what everything in the house was for.

The *engawa* [1] outside the west rooms was for sitting on in the afternoon, when the sun came around and made the boards warm. The stone bowl in the north garden was for washing your hands before you went to the shrine, and also for the birds, who did not know that. The heavy iron kettle in the kitchen was for water. The lighter one was for water that had to be a different hotness, which Nagi did not understand yet but was learning.

The paper on the *fusuma* [2] in the third guest room had been replaced twice since she came, and both times Ayame-san had done it herself, and both times she had been in a bad mood about it afterwards.

Nagi thought that was probably also a use. Being in a bad mood about a thing meant the thing mattered.

She was six, or close to it. Nobody had told her exactly, and she had stopped waiting for someone to.

The point of the list was this: everything here had a reason. That was the difference between this house and every place before it. Before, things happened and then other things happened, and there was no line you could draw between them. Here there was always a line. You could follow it if you were patient.

Nagi was very patient.

She had a room with a window that faced the wisteria. She had three sets of clothes, and two of them were new. She had a wooden comb with a chip out of one tooth, which sensei had given her and which she had not asked about, in case asking made it go away.

And she had lessons, which were the best part, and which she would not have said out loud that she liked because saying so felt greedy.

So: the list.

The list was going well.

Then the fox arrived, and the list stopped working.

  

The fox had come in the seventh day after the wedding.

Nagi had not seen it arrive. She had seen the bamboo tube, once, sitting on the step by the side entrance where the deliveries came, and she had thought it was a delivery, because it was on the step where the deliveries came. That was the kind of thinking the list encouraged.

Then Masaya-sama had come out and picked it up and gone very still.

He had stood there in the cold with his sleeves hanging and looked at a piece of bamboo about as long as his forearm, and he had not moved for what felt to Nagi like a very long time. She had been on the walkway with an armful of folded cloth and she had not known whether to keep walking or to stop, so she had stopped, because stopping was quieter.

Then he had said, out loud, to nobody, "…Oh."

Just that.

And then he had taken the tube inside, and shut the door, and that was the seventh day after the wedding.

The fox came out of the tube sometime that night. Nobody had seen that either. In the morning it was simply there, wound twice around Masaya-sama's wrist like a very poor bracelet, and the household had begun the slow business of pretending it had always been there, which the household was extremely good at.

  

Describing the fox was hard.

Nagi had tried three times in her head and had not got it right. It was about as long as her arm and about as thick as two fingers, and it was the colour of the inside of an old scroll, yellow-white, warm-looking. Its fur went the wrong way in places. If you looked at it quickly you thought *rope*. If you looked at it slowly you thought *animal*, and then you noticed the eyes, which were black and round and no bigger than pinheads, and after that you could not go back to thinking *rope* ever again.

It poured. That was the only verb for it.

It squeaked. It squeaked when it was pleased, and it squeaked when it was not pleased, and the two squeaks were completely different and Nagi had learned to tell them apart within four days, which she was privately proud of. Maro-sama had taken eleven days. Nagi knew this because Maro-sama had announced it at dinner in the tone of a woman being robbed.

Everyone could see it. That was the part Nagi had needed explained.

"They aren't usually like that," sensei had said, when Nagi finally asked. Sensei had been doing the ledgers and had not looked up. "A *kudagitsune* [3] belongs to one person. Nobody else sees it. Nobody else is supposed to know it exists."

"But I see it."

"Yes."

"And Kazuki-san sees it."

"Kazuki-san feeds it," sensei said, which was a different thing and also true. "So does Ayame. So does everyone in this house who has ever put down a plate."

Nagi waited.

Sensei turned a page. "It has decided to be seen," she said. "That is all. Do not build anything on top of it."

Nagi had built something on top of it anyway, quietly, in the part of her head where the list lived. She had written: *the fox is not hiding because it does not want to*. And then she had looked at that for a while and had not known what to do with it, because it did not tell her what the fox was **for**.

  

Here is what the fox did, over the following weeks, in the order Nagi observed it:

It slept in Masaya-sama's sleeve during lessons and made a bulge that made him look like he had a broken arm.

It stole exactly one piece of *inari-zushi* [4] from the kitchen and was caught, and Ayame-san had chased it the length of two corridors with a serving spoon while it screamed, and then it had come back at dinner and sat on the table looking at her, and she had put a piece down in front of it without saying anything, and had gone on serving, and had dared anyone with her face to comment.

Nobody commented.

It hated the pond.

It liked Kazuki-san's hands and would ride on the back of one while he worked, which meant Kazuki-san worked one-handed for most of a month and did not once complain, and the persimmon grafts that year came out fine anyway, which Nagi thought was either luck or love.

It sat on Masaya-sama's shoulder during the winter airing of the storehouse and squeaked at every single box.

It did not, as far as Nagi could tell, do anything.

That was the problem.

  

Because everything here had a use.

Ayame-san's *Ketsueki* [5] lit the lanterns and warmed the bathwater on the days the boiler sulked and, once, sealed a cut on Nagi's palm so fast and so hot that Nagi had cried, which had been embarrassing. Kazuki-san's *Chiyu* [6] kept the winter garden alive past all reason. Maro-sama saw the whole town through a veil Nagi was not allowed to ask about. Sensei wove. Indra-sama did whatever Indra-sama did, which Nagi did not have a word for and did not want one.

And Masaya-sama.

Masaya-sama held up the sky. Nagi had been told this in the careful voice adults used for facts they did not expect a child to carry, and she had accepted it the way she accepted the tide tables, and later she had gone out at night by herself and looked up and understood that it was not a saying.

Everything had a use. Everything was *for* something.

The fox was for nothing.

It ate. It slept. It stole. It rode about on people's hands. And when it was pleased it squeaked, and Masaya-sama would put one finger against its side without looking up from whatever he was reading, and the fox would go quiet, and that was the whole of it.

Nagi turned this over for days and could not make it fit, and it bothered her more than she wanted to admit, because it was the first thing in this house that broke the line.

  

The thing about being taken in is that you spend a while afterwards trying to work out what you cost.

Nagi did not think about the before very often. She had a few pieces of it and they were not good pieces, and she had learned that if she did not pick them up they mostly stayed where they were. Cold. Loud. Being very hungry in a way that stopped being sharp and became a kind of ringing.

And then sensei's hands, and a coat that smelled of cedar, and Masaya-sama crouching down to be her height, which nobody had ever done, and saying something she could not remember one word of.

After that: food, and a room, and lessons.

So she had spent the first month working out what she cost, because things cost. That was one of the pieces she had kept.

She had decided, eventually, that she was for learning Mist. That sensei needed an apprentice and the timing had been good and she had been in the road at the right moment. It was not a warm thought but it was a solid one, and Nagi preferred solid to warm, because solid held still.

She had put herself on the list under *use: apprentice*, and she had felt better.

And then the fox came, and the fox had no use, and nobody minded, and Nagi's entire arrangement developed a crack straight down the middle of it.

  

"It wants something," she said.

She had not meant to say it out loud. She had been sitting on the warm boards of the west *engawa* with a bowl of *amazake* [7] going cold in her hands, and Ayame-san had been folding, and the fox had been in the middle of the walkway in a patch of sun, arranged in a shape that no animal with bones could make.

Ayame-san did not stop folding. "Everything wants something."

"No, but…" Nagi frowned into the bowl. "It wants something and it isn't getting it."

Now Ayame-san did stop.

"Go on."

Nagi had not expected to be taken seriously and had to scramble. "It squeaks at things. Not all things. Just some things." She pointed with her chin, because her hands were full. "It squeaks at the storehouse boxes. It squeaks at the persimmons. It squeaked at your comb once and you thought it wanted to eat it."

"It did want to eat it."

"It didn't," Nagi said, and then went red, because contradicting Ayame-san was not on any list she had. "Sorry."

Ayame-san looked at her for a moment. Then she set the cloth down, which she never did in the middle, and she came and sat on the boards next to Nagi with her knees together and her back straight, the way she did everything.

"Tell me the ones it squeaks at."

Nagi told her. It took a while, because Nagi had been keeping count for eleven days and the count was long. The storehouse boxes, which were labelled. The persimmon grafts, which Kazuki-san had labelled with little wooden tags. Ayame-san's comb, which had a maker's mark on the spine. The bell at the west gate, which had a date on it. The big kettle, which was called *Old Woman* by the whole kitchen and had been called that since before sensei was born.

Ayame-san listened all the way to the end without once telling Nagi to hurry up.

Then she said, "Ah."

And then she said a word Nagi had not heard an adult use in this house before, and stood up, and went to find the priestess.

  

Nagi was not present for that conversation. She was, however, present for the one that came after it, because it happened in the small room off the *shoin* [8] where lessons were held, and Nagi was in that room, and nobody remembered to send her out.

This turned out to be lucky. Nagi filed it under *use: being small*.

"It wants a name," sensei said.

Masaya-sama was sitting with a scroll open in front of him that he had stopped reading approximately eleven minutes ago. The fox was around his wrist. It was not squeaking. It had gone the particular kind of still that Nagi had learned meant *listening very hard and pretending not to*.

"It has a name," Masaya-sama said.

"Does it."

"Yes."

Sensei waited.

Masaya-sama looked at the scroll. Nagi, from behind him and slightly to the left, in the corner, being small, watched the back of his neck.

"Natsu," he said.

The fox came off his wrist so fast that Nagi heard the air move.

It hit the *tatami* [9] and went across it in a straight line, poured up the wall, got onto the lintel above the door, and stopped there. It made a sound Nagi had never heard it make. It was not the pleased squeak and it was not the annoyed squeak. It was thin and it went on too long and it made the hair on Nagi's arms stand up.

Nobody moved.

"…Right," said Masaya-sama.

  

Here is what Nagi understood, and what she did not.

She understood that the fox had been called something before. You did not run from a name unless the name had been used on you.

She understood that Masaya-sama had known what to call it all along, and had not, and had let everyone in the house go three months calling it *the fox* and *that thing* and, in Maro-sama's case, *the noodle*.

She did not understand why the fox had run.

She thought about it for two days.

On the second day she was in the north garden, avoiding a *kanji* exercise, and the fox came and put itself along the top of the stone lantern in a way that suggested it was also avoiding something.

"Hello," said Nagi.

The fox squeaked. The pleased one.

"You aren't Natsu," Nagi said.

The fox stopped squeaking.

Nagi had come out here with nothing prepared, which was unlike her, and she found herself talking anyway, which was very unlike her.

"I think you were," she said. "I think that's why he said it. He wouldn't say a wrong thing. He isn't careless." She thought about it. "He's the opposite of careless. He's so much the opposite of careless that it makes sensei tired."

The fox's tail, if it was a tail, moved once.

"But you ran," said Nagi. "So it's wrong now."

The garden did the things gardens do. Somewhere behind the wall Kazuki-san was talking to a tree in a low voice, which he did, and which everyone had agreed not to mention.

"I know a bit about that," Nagi said, to the lantern rather than the fox, because it was easier. "Not the same. But a bit."

She did not explain what she meant, because she was not sure she could have, and because the fox did not seem to need her to.

After a while it came down off the lantern and wound around her wrist instead, which it had never done, and which was warm, and which Nagi did not deserve and could not think of a use for.

She sat very still so as not to spoil it.

  

That night she went and stood outside the door of the room where Masaya-sama worked, and did not knock, for four whole minutes.

"Nagi-chan," he said, from inside. "You can come in, you know. You've been out there long enough to grow a tree."

She came in. Her ears were hot.

Masaya-sama was on the floor with papers around him in a pattern that only made sense to him, and a cold cup of tea at his elbow that Ayame-san would find in the morning and be silent about. He had taken the mask off, which he did in private, and which Nagi was still not used to, because his face was so much younger than his voice.

He looked six. He looked exactly six.

Nagi had learned not to trust that.

"You didn't call it Natsu again," she said. Which was not how she had planned to start.

"No," he agreed.

"Why?"

He was quiet. He picked up the cold tea, discovered it was cold, and put it down again like a man who had done this many times and expected to keep doing it.

"Because it isn't," he said. "I got it wrong. That happens."

"But it *was*."

"It was." He looked at the papers instead of at her. "That's not the same thing as is."

Nagi thought about this hard enough that she forgot to be shy.

"Then what is it?"

"I don't know," Masaya-sama said.

And Nagi, six years old, standing in a doorway in a house where every single thing had a use, felt the floor tilt very slightly, because she had never once heard an adult say that without also saying something afterwards to cover it up.

He did not cover it up. He let it sit there.

"I've been thinking about it for three months," he said. "I've been thinking about it instead of doing several things I should be doing." He gestured vaguely at the papers, which Nagi could now see had not been touched in some time. "And I've got as far as: I don't know what it is, and it doesn't want to be what it was, and it has been extremely patient with me about both."

The fox, which had come in behind Nagi without either of them noticing, made the pleased squeak from somewhere near the floor.

"Yes, thank you," said Masaya-sama. "Very helpful."

  

"It could pick," Nagi offered.

She had been sent to bed and had come back, which was a thing she had not known she was capable of.

Masaya-sama, who had not moved, did not seem surprised. "Pick what?"

"Its name. It could pick." Nagi shifted from foot to foot. "You could say some and it could…" She ran out of sentence.

"Squeak."

"Yes."

Masaya-sama looked at the fox. The fox looked back at him with its two black pinheads and did not squeak at all, which Nagi felt was unhelpful of it, given the circumstances.

"That is," he said slowly, "an extremely sensible idea."

Nagi went red to the ears and stared at the floor.

"It is also," he added, "going to be extremely humiliating for me."

"Sorry."

"Don't be sorry, you're right." He was already reaching for a brush and something to write failed names on, which told Nagi he had decided about four seconds ago and had only been waiting for a reason. "That's the annoying part."

  

It took an hour and a half.

Nagi kept count of the ones that failed because keeping count was what she did, and because at some point in the middle of it the whole thing stopped being solemn and became the funniest thing that had ever happened to her.

*Kon*: silence.

*Kogitsune*: silence, and then a slow deliberate turn of the head away, which somehow was worse.

*Shiro*: an annoyed squeak so immediate that Masaya-sama said "fair" out loud.

*Ukanome*, which Nagi did not understand and which made sensei, arriving in the doorway at exactly the wrong moment, say "**absolutely not**" in a voice like a gate closing.

*Hachi*: a very long pause. Then Masaya-sama said, "No. No, that one's mine," in a strange flat way, and moved on quickly, and Nagi added it to the list of things she would think about later.

*Tama*: the annoyed squeak.

*Mame*: the annoyed squeak, twice.

*Noodle*, offered by Maro-sama, who had by now also arrived and had sat down without being invited and had helped herself to the cold tea: the annoyed squeak, and the fox left the room entirely for six minutes.

"You've offended it," said sensei.

"Good," said Maro-sama.

  

By the end of the hour there were nine people in a room that comfortably held four.

Ayame-san had come to see why the lamps were still lit and had stayed. Kazuki-san had come to find Ayame-san and had stayed, and had brought, for reasons nobody interrogated, a persimmon. Two of the shrine maidens had appeared in the doorway and had been waved in by Maro-sama, who was not the mistress of the house and behaved like it constantly. Indra-sama arrived last, took in the scene in one pass, said nothing at all, and sat down in the best position in the room with the ease of a woman who had never in her life had to ask.

The fox sat in the middle of the *tatami* and rejected names.

"*Yuki*," tried one of the maidens.

Annoyed squeak.

"*Akashi*."

Annoyed squeak.

"*Ō-Inari-sama's Left Sandal*," said Maro-sama.

"Maro," said sensei.

"It's a *long* night, Musashi."

Nagi had her hands over her mouth. She was not managing to be quiet about it. Nobody seemed to mind. That was another thing she had not got used to.

And then Kazuki-san, who had not said one word all evening and who was still holding his persimmon, said, "It came in the winter."

The room turned.

Kazuki-san went the colour of his own fruit. "Sorry. I only meant… it came in the seventh day after. Which was winter. And it doesn't like the pond because the pond's cold, so it's not a winter creature, but it *came* in the winter, and the trees were doing the winter ones then. The *fuyuzakura* [10]. And the *fuyugaki* [11], which is what I've got here, which is not relevant, I'm sorry."

He stopped. He looked like he wanted the floor to take him.

The fox uncoiled and went across the *tatami* and up Kazuki-san's arm and sat on his shoulder and made the pleased squeak directly into his ear.

  

Nagi watched Masaya-sama's face.

She was, she would realise much later, the only person in the room watching Masaya-sama's face, because everyone else was watching the fox, and because Nagi had spent three months learning to watch him the way she had once learned to watch a doorway.

He had gone still. Not the polite stillness he used in front of the town. The other one.

"Fuyu," he said.

The fox went off Kazuki-san's shoulder and across the floor and up Masaya-sama's sleeve and around his wrist, twice, and then a third time, which it had never done, and it squeaked and squeaked and squeaked.

Somebody laughed. Nagi thought it might have been her.

"Well," said Maro-sama, into the noise. "Thank the gods. I was going to suggest *Persimmon* next."

  

Afterwards there was tea that was actually hot, because Ayame-san had gone and made it without being asked, and the two maidens were sent to bed and did not go, and Indra-sama had a cup and said, of the entire evening, exactly one sentence, which was: "The name is fine."

"Thank you, Seiran-san."

"It is a very small fox," said Indra-sama, "and you spent ninety minutes on it."

"Yes."

"I will be telling Kyoya."

"Please don't."

"I have already decided."

  

The room emptied slowly. Nagi, being small, was overlooked, and stayed.

Sensei was the last to go, and she stopped in the doorway with her hand on the frame and said, without turning, "Tsunayoshi."

"Mm."

"Winter is the season your grandfather died in."

Nagi felt the air change.

"I know," said Masaya-sama.

"Do you know that you knew?"

There was a pause that went on long enough for Nagi to hear the wind in the eaves and, somewhere further off, one of Kimiko-sama's out-of-season trees dropping something.

"…No," said Masaya-sama. "Not until you said it."

Sensei nodded once, at the door frame rather than at him, and went out, and slid it shut behind her with great care.

  

So then it was Nagi, and Masaya-sama, and a fox called Fuyu.

Nagi should have gone. She had already stayed past two separate points at which staying became a decision. She did not go.

"Masaya-sama."

"Nagi-chan."

"What's it for?"

He looked up.

Nagi's ears were burning again but she had started so she went on, because stopping halfway was worse. "Everything's for something. Ayame-san's for the house. Kazuki-san's for the garden. Sensei's for the shrine. I'm for…" She stopped. Changed track. "Fuyu isn't for anything. It doesn't do anything. It eats and it steals and it sits on people."

"That's true."

"So what's it for?"

Masaya-sama put the scroll down. He had never once, in three months, told her a question was silly, and he did not do it now, and Nagi had not known until that moment how hard she had been braced for it.

"Nothing," he said.

"…Nothing?"

"Nothing at all." He said it easily, like it was a small fact about the weather. "It doesn't work. It doesn't guard. It doesn't fetch. It won't do a single thing anyone asks it to and it never has."

"Then why is it here?"

"Because it wanted to be," said Masaya-sama, "and I let it."

  

Nagi took that home with her and could not make it lie flat.

She lay in the dark with the wisteria moving against the window and turned it over and over. *Because it wanted to be, and I let it.* No use. No reason. No line you could follow from one thing to the next.

It should have been frightening. In the before, things that happened for no reason were the worst kind. Things that happened for no reason could stop for no reason.

It was not frightening.

She could not work out why it was not frightening, and eventually she stopped trying, and just held on to it, which was itself a thing she did not have a use for.

  

She did not sleep much. Near dawn she got up and went along the cold walkway to the west *engawa*, because that was where you went, and found she was not the first.

Masaya-sama was on the boards with his knees drawn up and a blanket that was not doing its job and Fuyu asleep in the crook of his elbow like a badly coiled rope.

He did not look surprised to see her. He moved over.

Nagi sat.

The sky over the east wall was doing the thing it did before it decided to be morning. Above it, faint and enormous and always there, the vault of gold that Nagi had stopped being able to see about a month after she arrived, in the manner of a ceiling.

"Masaya-sama."

"Mm."

"You have a lot of plans."

He breathed out through his nose, which might have been agreement and might have been pain.

"Is Fuyu in any of them?"

"No," he said.

"Not one?"

"Not one."

Nagi thought about the list. About the boxes with labels and the grafts with tags and the kettle that had a name older than sensei. About Ayame-san putting down the *inari-zushi* and daring anyone to say a word.

About a fox that had run from the name it used to have, and had been given a new one, and had been allowed to be nothing.

"That's good," she said.

She felt him look at her.

She kept her eyes on the wall, because it was easier, and because she was six and not stupid.

"Is it?"

"Yes," said Nagi. "Because if it had to be for something, it'd have to be good at it. And then if it wasn't good at it any more…" She shrugged with one shoulder. "It'd have to go."

The boards were cold under her. Somewhere in the kitchen a door went, and there were the first sounds of Ayame-san being awake and being furious about the state of the tea things.

Masaya-sama did not say anything for a while.

Then he said, "Nagi-chan."

"Yes?"

"You are also not in any of my plans."

Nagi's chest did something complicated.

"…Oh," she said.

"None of you are," he said, in that same easy way, looking out at the wall. "Not one. I checked."

Fuyu shifted in his elbow and made a small sleeping squeak.

Nagi thought: *that is a strange thing to check.*

Nagi thought: *that is a strange thing to be able to say out loud.*

And Nagi, who was six, or close to it, and who had a list in her head of what everything in the house was for, and who was very patient, and who watched people the way she had once watched doorways, looked sideways at the person sitting next to her on the cold boards with a useless fox asleep on his arm.

She did not know yet what she was looking at.

But she moved him, in the list, out of *holds up the sky*.

And put him somewhere she did not have a heading for.

  

The sun came up. It was not a big sunrise. It was the kind that happens most days and does not ask you to look at it.

Ayame-san found them both twenty minutes later and was extremely rude to a person who held up the sky, on the subject of blankets, at length, and Nagi laughed so hard she got the hiccups.

Fuyu slept through all of it.

## Chapter End Notes

1. **Engawa** - the veranda-like strip of flooring running along the outside of a traditional Japanese house, between the interior and the garden. Neither inside nor out.
2. **Fusuma** - sliding opaque panel doors used to divide rooms.
3. **Kudagitsune** (管狐) - "pipe fox". A small spirit fox kept in a bamboo tube. Traditionally invisible to everyone but its keeper, traditionally used for divination and cursing, and traditionally a catastrophe for the household's finances. Fuyu does not do any of that. Fuyu is aware that Fuyu does not do any of that.
4. **Inari-zushi** - sweet fried tofu pouches stuffed with rice. Named for Inari, whose foxes are said to like them. The Sawada household serves them constantly and has done for four hundred years, and nobody has ever pointed out that this is essentially bribery.
5. **Ketsueki** - Blood. Storm.
6. **Chiyu** - Healing. Sun.
7. **Amazake** - a sweet, low or non-alcoholic drink made from fermented rice. Given to children at festivals.
8. **Shoin** - a formal reception room. Also, in this house, wherever Musashi happens to be sitting.
9. **Tatami** - woven rush floor matting.
10. **Fuyuzakura** - winter-flowering cherry.
