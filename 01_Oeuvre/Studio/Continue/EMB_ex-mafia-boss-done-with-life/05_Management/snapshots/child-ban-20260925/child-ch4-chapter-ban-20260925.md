# Local Woman Reads A List Nobody Left For Her. Local Boy Is Billed For The Worst News Of His Year.

## 4.1

The letter was four lines long and it came on the twenty-eighth of March, and he read it standing in the shrine yard because he did not trust himself to carry it as far as the house.

> *I have found where he was.*
>
> *Not where he is.*
>
> *There are six places. I will write when there are seven.*
>
> *Do not do anything.*

*Do not do anything,* thought Tsuna, and laughed once, badly, at a piece of paper in a cold yard. *She has put that in a letter to me as an instruction. She does not know that it is also a court order.*

*She thinks she is asking me to trust her. What she is actually doing is describing the only thing I am permitted to do, and she has got it exactly right by accident, and I am never going to be able to tell her that.*

He wrote back the same day. It took an hour, and the finished letter was two lines, and the second line was *I will not*. He spent forty of the sixty minutes deciding whether those three words were a promise or a complaint, decided they could be both, and sent it.

*Six places,* he thought, on the way back down from the post box. *She has six and she is telling me there will be a seventh, which means she thinks the list continues. Why does she think that? What is in the six that says there is a seven?*

*And why is she writing to me at all? She has no obligation. She has not been commissioned. The **Sanctuary** does not require it and I did not ask.*

*…Because she is a Namimori woman doing a thing outside Namimori, and there is nobody else in the world she can tell. That is not affection. That is the shape of a town with a boundary round it.*

Then there were seven months, and the seven months were mostly other things, because a town does not stop.

**April.** A letter came from the house of Kōtokui, three lines, on paper that cost more than the postage. It said that at the equinox the name **Raiga** had been given, in the usual manner, before the house.

It did not say to whom.

Musashi read it twice and observed that a house which will not write a boy's name on a letter is a house that has not finished being ashamed. Then she put it in the file, because that is what the file is for.

*So they did it,* thought Tsuna, *and they did it in March as arranged, and they still cannot make themselves put the name and the boy in the same sentence. Is that shame, or is that a house keeping its options open?*

*And which of the two did they hand it to? I have no way of knowing, and I am not owed one.*

**May.** The Disciplinary Committee passed fifty and instituted a system of shifts, which Kyouya did not announce and which was simply in operation one Tuesday. Shouichi Irie built a thing that made a noise, and his father, who was managing, said it was fine. Kawahira came every other Sunday and complained about the price of squid, about the neighbour's dog, and about the coming summer, and about nothing else at all.

*Nothing else at all,* wrote Tsuna on the sheet, in May, in the third column. He has written that four times now and he has never once written what he thinks it means.

*A **Sky** four hundred years old sits on my step every fortnight and talks about squid. Either he wants nothing, which is not a thing he has ever been, or he is waiting, and a thing that old waiting is not a pleasant object to keep in the house.*

**June.** Nagi held a **Gen'ei** across the whole of the shrine roof for eleven minutes, in front of her grandmother, on purpose. Then she was sick. Then she did it again the following week for nine minutes and was not sick, and Musashi wrote the date on the doorframe with the ones for height.

⚠️ That is a **Mist** of six doing at seven minutes what most houses do not ask for at seventeen, and nobody in that family said so out loud, because in Namimori you do not tell a child what she has just done.

Ayame started a second file for the visitor logs, because the first one would not shut. There had been eleven visitors in January and there were forty-one by June, and about a third of those were people who had heard something about a piece of paper.

**August.** Ferri, who had been in Namimori for ten months, was asked to settle an argument about a boundary hedge. He did it so well that two households who had not spoken since February began speaking again. Then he went home and sat down and told nobody that it was the first time in thirty-two years anybody had asked him to decide anything.

**October.** Tsuna turned nine. There was a thing at the shrine, because there is a thing at the shrine, and the **Kotei** of a **Sanctuary** stands through it. He stood through it.

*Nine,* he thought, somewhere in the middle of it, with sixty people in the yard and a cup in his hand he had not drunk from. *Nine is the first number that has been in my way. At eight I was too small for everything, which is restful, because too small for everything means you are not measuring. Nine is close enough to measure against.*

*How much of what I cannot do this year will I still not be able to do next year?*

And every few weeks, at no regular interval, a letter came from a woman in a grey coat, and he read each one twice and answered none of them with anything useful, because there was nothing useful he was allowed to say.

## 4.2

The second letter was long, and it was the one where she stopped being careful with him.

> *Kohaku-sama,*
>
> *You will want to know how I am doing this, and I am going to tell you, because you are nine years old and because if I do not tell you then you will work it out from four facts and get it seventy per cent right, which is worse.*
>
> *I am not tracking him. Nobody has ever tracked him. Six separate professional outfits have looked for that boy in eight years and not one of them has got closer than a month behind, and two of them were better than me.*
>
> *I am reading a list.*

She had six places by then. She set them out in the order she had found them, which was not the order they had happened in. Then she set them out again in the order they had happened in, and the second list is the one that stopped her.

Every one of the six was a place he had left.

That is not remarkable. A boy who has been moving for eight years has left a great many places.

What stopped her was **when** he had left them. In every single case it was between nine and nineteen days before somebody arrived who was looking for him. Not the day before. Not in a panic. Nine to nineteen days, every time, six times, across four countries.

> *A frightened child runs the night before, if he runs at all. A lucky child runs sometimes and is caught the other times. This is neither. This is a person who knows, and who knows early, and who leaves with enough margin to leave tidily.*

And the second thing, which she wrote in a flatter hand, as though she had put the pen down between the paragraphs:

> *The six places are not random either. I have been sitting with them for four days.*
>
> *He went to the safest one first. Then the second safest. Then the third. He has been working down a list, Kohaku-sama, in order, and burning each one as he goes, and he has never once gone back up it.*
>
> *Which means he made the list before he started. Which means he made it at three years old, or he has been remaking it every year since, and I do not know which of those is worse to think about.*

*He ranks,* thought Tsuna, sitting on the floor of the room with the door that does not close, with the letter on his knees and his chest doing something inconvenient. *Of course he ranks. That is what he is. That is the thing they took him for.*

*And what is that like? Not the power. Everybody asks about the power. What is it like to be six years old in a strange kitchen and have the room sort itself before you have taken your coat off?*

*Does he get to be wrong? Ever? Is there a single thing in his life he has been allowed to find out slowly?*

*I had a **Flame** they wanted and I had a name they wanted and I have spent two lives being the thing in the room that everybody is calculating about. And I have never once had to carry what he carries, which is that the calculating happens **inside** him and does not stop.*

*And nobody has ever asked what it is like to be a child who cannot look at a room without it sorting itself.*

He read the rest of it and then went back and read the fourth paragraph again, and then he sat for a while and did the piece of arithmetic he had been avoiding since March.

*Six places. In order. Each one abandoned with two weeks in hand.*

*So he is not hiding from the people looking for him. He is ahead of them, and he has been ahead of them since he was three, and staying ahead is not the hard part for him. Staying ahead is the easy part.*

*Then what has he been doing for eight years?*

*…Waiting for the list to get to somebody worth stopping at.*

*He has been descending a ranking. And a ranking has a bottom. What happens when he reaches it?*

## 4.3

The fourth letter came in July and it was the shortest of all of them and he has it still.

> *Seven.*
>
> *I was wrong about the list. It is not in order of safety. Ask me in a month.*

The fifth letter took five weeks and arrived in the last week of August, and he read it at the kitchen table at seven in the morning with his grandmother sitting opposite him not asking, and he had to put it down twice.

She had worked it out in a bus station in Lyon at two in the morning, with the six places written on the back of a timetable, and what she had worked out was this:

The list was not ranked by safety. It was ranked by **who would understand the list**.

> *Every one of those six towns has a broker in it. Not a good one, in most cases. But a person whose trade is arranging other people's names.*
>
> *He did not go to those places because they were safe. He went to them because if anybody ever came looking for him properly, the person who noticed the pattern would have to be somebody who reads placements for a living.*
>
> *It is an examination, Kohaku-sama. He has been sitting an examination for eight years and marking it himself. Everybody who came after him failed it. They were looking for a boy, and the boy had left a question.*
>
> *I passed it in a bus station at two in the morning and I was sick in the lavatory afterwards.*

*He set a test,* thought Tsuna. *He set a test that can only be passed by the exact kind of person he needed, and then he sat still inside it for eight years and let everybody who was not that person walk past.*

*And nobody in those six towns ever felt him. That is the other half of it and I have only just seen it.*

*A **Lightning** can be felt across a street. A **Storm** in a temper can be felt across a valley. A **Sun** you notice before you see him, and a **Cloud** you notice by the gap he leaves.*

*I stood in my own yard in February and read a **Lightning** off an eleven year old at eight metres without trying. And I could not read the boy standing next to him at all.*

*Whatever this child has, it does not push. There is nothing to feel.*

*A **Sky** walking past him in a market gets nothing. A **Mist** hunting him gets nothing. A **Storm** would walk into him in a doorway and apologise to the air. And that is not training, because nobody trained him.*

*That is simply what a ranking is. It looks at the room, and the room does not look back.*

*He is the only kind of person in this world who could have done what he did, and the reason he had to do it is the same reason he could.*

*That is not hiding. That is not even running. That is the most patient thing I have ever heard of and it was done by somebody who was three.*

*What does a child have to have understood, at three years old, to start doing that?*

*And here is the question I do not want to write down. If the list is a test, then everybody who walked past it failed, and he watched them fail, one at a time, for eight years.*

*Six outfits. Two of them better than Tomoko. He stood in a village and watched a professional come within nineteen days of him and not see the thing he had spent his whole life building, and then he packed, and went down to the next one.*

*What does that do to a person? What is left of your opinion of adults after the eleventh one?*

The seventh place was a fishing village on the Atlantic coast, and she did not go to it.

She went to the sixth, which he had left nineteen days earlier. She sat down in the one café it had and wrote out the whole of her working, in full, on seven sheets of paper. Then she left it with the woman behind the counter and said: if a boy comes in, give him this.

Then she took a room above the café and waited.

> *He came in on the fourth day. He read all seven sheets standing up, at the counter, in about nine minutes. That is faster than I can read my own handwriting.*
>
> *Then he asked the woman whether the person who wrote it was still in the village, and she said yes, upstairs, and he said thank you and went and sat down at a table by the window and ordered nothing and waited for somebody to come and get him.*
>
> *He was not hiding from me. He was giving me the last piece, to see whether I would come down the stairs alone.*
>
> *I came down alone.*

Tsuna found out three days after it happened.

He has thought about those three days more than is reasonable.

On the Tuesday it happened. On the Friday a letter reached a shrine in Mie. In between there were seventy-two hours in which the most important thing in his year had already occurred, and he had gone on doing the accounts.

*Good,* he made himself write, on the sheet, that Sunday. *That is the correct number of days. That is what it looks like when a thing is genuinely not mine.*

*It does not feel like anything I would recommend.*

⁂

Shouichi asked him in September, on the step, with the radio in pieces again.

"Do you know where my mum is?"

Tsuna had been waiting five months for that question and had prepared nine answers and every single one of them was a lie of a different weight, and he had been quite pleased with the third one.

"Yes," he said.

Shouichi's hands stopped.

"You know."

"I know roughly which country. I do not know the town and I do not know what she is doing this week, and I have not asked, and I am not going to."

"Why not!" said Shouichi, and it came out much louder than he meant, and a gull went up off the wall. "Why would you *not* ask! You could just ask!"

"Because she did not tell me either."

That landed. He watched it land, which is a thing he has never enjoyed and has always made himself do.

"She wrote to *you*," said Shouichi.

"She writes to me about her work. She has not once told me where she is, and I think that is on purpose, and I think it is on purpose because of you." Tsuna picked up the small screwdriver and turned it over. "If she does not tell anybody, then nobody can be asked. Including a nine year old on a step who is being asked right now."

Shouichi sat with that for rather a long time for an eight year old.

"She said two weeks," said Shouichi, to the radio rather than to him. "In January. She said two weeks and it's September."

"I know."

"Nobody has *said* that to me. Not Dad, not anybody. Everyone just goes on about the cooking." He put a screw down very precisely on the cloth. "It's been eight months and I'm the only one who's counting and I'm eight."

*He is not,* thought Tsuna. *He is not the only one who is counting and he never has been, and I cannot tell him that, because the only way to tell him is to tell him how I know.*

"You are not the only one," he said, which was as far as he could go, and which he had not planned and did not regret.

Shouichi looked at him sideways for about four seconds.

"That's stupid," he said at last.

"Yes."

"It's stupid and it *works*," said Shouichi bitterly, "which is the worst kind," and took the screwdriver back, and did not ask again for four months.

*And I did not lie to him,* thought Tsuna, walking up the hill afterwards. *I have just been more careful with a child than I was with a **Sanctuary** full of adults, and he is going to remember this conversation when he is thirty.*

*Was that the right thing?*

*…I do not know. I know it was the only true thing available, and those are not the same, and I have had two lives to learn the difference and I still check.*

## 4.4

She did not describe him. That was deliberate, and she said so.

> *I am not going to tell you what he looks like or what he sounds like, because you have never met him and because the first thing everybody does with him is make him into a thing they have an opinion about.*
>
> *One thing only, because you will ask it and because you are entitled to it as the **Kohaku**. I sat across a table from him for four days and I could not read him. Nothing. I am a **Ryokugyoku** and a poor one, but I have sat opposite a **Ketsueki** in a bad temper and known it through a wall, and this boy at a metre and a half is an empty chair.*
>
> *It is not concealment. Concealment has an edge to it. There is simply nothing there to meet, and I have never in twenty years of this trade met a person who reads as nothing.*
>
> *I will tell you what he said.*

He was eleven years old.

He had been out of the hands that took him for rather longer than anybody would have guessed. Where he had been in between is his own business. She did not ask, and she has not asked since.

He gave her a name to use. It was not his, and he said so in the same breath, in the manner of a person handing over a coat check.

And then, on the second evening, in a café that had closed for the night, with a woman he had met the day before, he set his position out in order, in the manner of a party to a negotiation.

> *He said: if I go with you, I stop being hard to find.*
>
> *He said: at the moment there are perhaps forty people in the world who want me. None of them can have me. That is the whole of what I own.*
>
> *He said: the day I stand in a house with a name on the door, I am worth what I can do again. I have been worth nothing for eight years and it is the only thing that has kept me alive.*
>
> *And then he said the part I have not stopped thinking about. He said: I have already ranked it. I did it in the café, while I was reading your handwriting. I know what the odds are and I am not going to tell you, because if I tell you then you will argue with the number instead of with me.*

She wrote that she had not argued at all. She wrote why, and the why is the part Tsuna read four times.

> *Everybody who has ever wanted that boy has argued with him. That is what wanting him looks like from the inside: you find the number he has given you and you tell him it is wrong.*
>
> *I am fifty-one years old and I have been on the other side of that conversation. When a person tells you what they have worked out, and you argue with the working instead of answering the person, what you are saying is that their working is a thing to be got round.*
>
> *He has had eight years of being got round. I was not going to be the eleventh.*

She told him one thing instead.

> *It took me about eleven seconds. I told him that I was asked to find him once, eight years ago, by people who meant to sell him. I told him I did not know that at the time. And I told him that when I found out I burned the whole contract and my whole life with it, and ran, with a husband and two children and one of them born on the road.*
>
> *I told him I had been trying to find him since, on my own money, and had failed for eight years.*
>
> *And I said: I am not here because somebody sent me. I am here because I could not stand it. And you can check that, because checking is what you do.*
>
> *He looked at me for a while. Then he said: I know. That is the third thing I ranked.*

**He chose on the fourth morning.**

He came downstairs with nothing, because he had nothing. He said that he had decided. And he said he wanted it written down somewhere that he had decided it himself, and not been persuaded, and not been rescued.

> *So I wrote it down, on the back of the same timetable, and he signed it, and I have it here.*
>
> *He knows exactly what it costs. He told me the cost before he told me the decision, which is the wrong order for a child and the right order for an accountant. He said: I will be findable by Tuesday. He said it in the voice of a man reading out a train time.*
>
> *He is not coming to Namimori. He has not agreed to it. I have not asked. You are not to ask either.*
>
> *He is coming to the place I have arranged. He will stay there for as long as he chooses to stay. The arrangement has a door that opens both ways, and he has read the whole of it twice.*
>
> *He asked me who the town's Sky was. I told him. He said thank you and did not ask anything else, and I want you to sit with that, Kohaku-sama, because I think it is the politest refusal I have ever been given.*

Tsuna sat with it.

*He asked, and then he stopped asking. He has ranked me too, obviously. And whatever number I came out at, it was not high enough to be worth a question, or it was high enough that he did not need one, and I am not going to know which.*

*And I do not get to know. That is the arrangement I signed in January, in four words, on the back of a piece of not quite paper.*

*I wanted it to be the second one.*

*That is worth writing down, because it is the least useful thing I have thought all year. I wanted a child I have never met to have ranked me kindly…*

*…and he is eleven, and he has been alone since he was three, and what I want has never been one of the inputs.*

⁂

The last letter of the year came in November, and it is the only one in which she asked him for anything.

> *Kohaku-sama. One thing, and then I will stop writing until I am home.*
>
> *Do not put him in the file.*
>
> *Not under his name and not under the name he gave me and not under a mark. Not in the visitor log, not in the shrine archive, not in the second file Ayame started in June. Nowhere.*
>
> *I know what I am asking. I have lived in that town for nine years and I know that its answer to every single thing is to write it down, and I know that you have spent this year finding out why that works.*
>
> *It does not work here. He is alive because there is no line anywhere with him on it. The day a **Sanctuary** in Mie has a record of him, he is a **Sanctuary** in Mie's business, and then he is worth something to somebody again, and that is the exact thing he traded eight years of his life to stop being.*
>
> *You are the **Kotei**. Nobody can make you leave a thing out. That is why I am asking rather than arranging it myself.*

He read it four times. Then he went and stood at the window, which is what he does.

*She is right. She is right and I hate it.*

*I want to go through her reasoning looking for the hole. There is no hole. Looking for one is simply the shape my objection is arriving in.*

*Every instrument this town has works by writing. The contract. The seven bonds. The instrument Genichi signed. The note that says nothing was decided. The visitor log with a mountain in the house column.*

*And the whole of this year has been me learning that an open column is a decision and that a blank space does work. So what is a person who must not be in any column at all?*

*…He is the limit of the thing. He is the case the machine cannot hold without breaking him, and the correct answer is to not put him in the machine.*

He wrote back in two lines, the second of which was *he will not be in any file of ours*, and then he did a thing he has done perhaps five times in his life: he went and told Ayame a rule without telling her the reason.

She wrote it down. Of course she wrote it down. She wrote **no entry is to be made** and the date, and signed it, and filed that, which Tsuna thought about for some while afterwards and decided was either the funniest thing in the town or the most frightening, and that on balance it was both.

## 4.5

The other thing arrived in the third week of October, eleven days after his birthday, and it came in an envelope with no stamp on it.

Inside were two pieces of paper.

The first was a cutting from an Italian financial daily, four column centimetres, about an internal reorganisation at a shipping concern in Palermo. It named nobody. It said that certain personnel matters had been resolved and that the Ninth's office regretted the disruption. It was the kind of paragraph that is printed so that somebody can point at it later and say it was published at the time.

The second was an invoice.

It was made out in a small, fussy, entirely legible hand.

It itemised: **one cutting, one envelope, one delivery, no commentary.** The total was four hundred and eighty lire.

Tsuna looked at it for a long while.

*Four hundred and eighty lire. That is not a price. You cannot buy a coffee for that.*

*He has charged me an amount specifically calculated to be too small to be a fee and too deliberate to be a favour. Because a favour would mean he had done something, and he has not done anything, he has sold me a newspaper cutting at cost.*

*And in the corner it says no commentary, which is itself a commentary, and he knows it is, and he wrote it anyway.*

*Mammon. You absolute…*

*No. Be fair. An Arcobaleno who sells information does not have a category for this, and a **Mist** who is also an accountant has exactly one way of saying a thing he is not allowed to say, and this is it. He has put it through the books. He has made it a transaction so that it is not a gift.*

*And four hundred and eighty lire is going to sit in a ledger in Italy for the rest of his life, and he will know what it is, and I will know what it is, and nobody else on earth will ever be able to read it.*

He put the invoice down and picked up the cutting again and read the four column centimetres for the sixth time, and the sixth time was the one where his hands stopped working properly.

*I know what this is.*

*I have known what this is since I was one year old. I have known the month and I have known roughly the year and I have known what it does to the Ninth and what it does to the Varia and what it does to a man who is nineteen and has just found out what he is not.*

*And I have been sitting in a fishing town in Mie with a certificate in a drawer saying I am nobody, and it has happened exactly on time, and the world has not diverged one single centimetre on this one.*

⁂

He went and found his grandmother first, which is new, and which took him two hours to decide to do and about nine seconds to do.

He gave her the cutting. She read the four column centimetres twice, at the kitchen table.

Then she did not say any of the things a person says. She asked the question that was actually in front of them.

"Who signed our ceasefire?"

"He did."

"Not the organisation."

"No," said Tsuna. "Him. On our steps, in January of last year, in his own hand, and his second signed under it as a witness. It is a personal instrument. That is why it worked."

Musashi put the cutting down and looked at it for some while.

"Then we have a piece of paper," she said, "signed by a man who is currently unavailable, binding an outfit of professional killers who did not sign it, and the person they answer to is the same man."

"Yes."

"How long have you known that was a problem?"

"Since about four minutes after I signed it," said Tsuna. "It was still the best instrument available. A **Sanctuary** is held up by bonds and by documents, and the document I could get that day was that one."

*And I am not going to say the rest of it,* he thought, with his hands flat on the table. *The rest of it is that I signed a thing with a man and not with an organisation because a man can be argued with and an organisation cannot, and that judgement was correct, and today it has a hole in it exactly the shape of one man.*

Musashi did the thing she does, which is to take a problem apart into the parts that can be worked and the parts that cannot.

"Does the Ninth know we have it?"

"There is a line in a register in Italy. A clerk read it once and closed the file." He turned his cup round. "And one other person knows, and that person is an **Arcobaleno** who sells information for a living and who has had eighteen months to sell it and has not."

"What about the seven?"

"The seven houses answer an attack," said Musashi. "They do not answer a vacancy. If I pull those bonds because a man in Italy has gone into a box, I have spent the whole instrument on a rumour, and the next time something real happens the **Ketsueki** will come at a walk."

She counted it off on the table with one finger, which is how she thinks.

"[[ayame|Ayame]] is a **Ketsueki** and she will want to. [[kazuki|Kazuki]] is a **Chiyu** and he will not, and he will be right." She moved her finger a third time. "[[indra|Indra]] is a **Yasuragi**. She will already have worked it out and will say nothing until asked, and when she is asked she will be four days ahead of both of us."

"Then we do nothing," said Musashi, "and we do it very carefully, and if anybody from that outfit comes up those steps this winter we treat them exactly as we treated them in January and we do not once mention that the man who signed is not available. Ayame is to be told. Nobody else."

"Agreed."

"And you will put it in the file."

"Yes," said Tsuna, and meant it, and it went in the file the following day with the date it was signed on it.

⁂

He did the arithmetic that night, on the floor, properly, with a pen, because that is what he does instead of the other thing.

*Right. Assume I want to act. What does acting look like?*

*One. Get to Italy. Eight days by the routes I know, and I did it at seven, and I could do it at nine, and I would be a nine year old alone in Palermo with no standing and no name and nothing to show anybody.*

*Two. Get to the place. I do not know where the place is.*

*I could find out in perhaps three weeks through the shrine network. Which would mean using the shrine network. Which would mean arranging, and I have signed a document saying I will not arrange…*

*…no. Stop that. That is not the reason and I am not going to let it be the reason. The clause is about a child in Europe. It has nothing to say about this. If the only thing stopping me were a piece of paper from Vendicare I would go tonight and argue about it afterwards.*

*So. Three. Assume I am standing in front of it. What do I do?*

He sat with the pen for some while.

*Work it properly. Not the version where I am brave. The version with numbers in it.*

*The technique wants a **Flame** held at a purity I have hit exactly twice, both times for under four seconds, and both times I was on the floor afterwards. It wants it held for minutes. It wants a body that can take the cold coming back the other way, and mine is nine years old and I measure my own **crux** once a month because it drifts.*

*And it wants a **Sky** who is not in the middle of something. I am in the middle of eleven things.*

*I cannot do it.*

*Not the ice itself. The technique for the ice is a technique and I have seen it done and I know what it costs the person doing it, and it costs an adult in his prime very nearly everything he has. I have the knowledge of it in my head and I have nine year old lungs and a core I measure once a month to make sure it has not drifted.*

*If I went, I would get there, and I would fail, and failing would tell forty people in that organisation that there is a child in Mie who knows things he cannot possibly know.*

*And he would still be in the ice. And he would be in it for longer, because after that they would move him.*

He wrote the numbers down. He is very good at numbers and he did not shade them and the answer did not change on the second pass or the third.

*I am four years short. Possibly five.*

*That is the whole of it. There is no clever version and no door I have not tried and no favour I can call in, and the reason I am not going is not that I am forbidden and it is not that I am being wise.*

*It is that I am nine, and it is not enough, and it will not be enough for a long time…*

*…and there is no arrangement of those words that makes them into an excuse, and I have tried about six.*

He put the pen down. Then he got up and went out to the sessha, at half past one in the morning, in October, and measured.

He does it on the first Sunday of the month, and this was a Thursday, and he did it anyway. A number written in a notebook is not the same thing as a number you have just taken.

**Crux:** where it has been since the winter, which is where it is supposed to be. No drift in ten months, which is the whole reason he agreed to the measuring.

**Soulscape:** nine steps. Nine, as it has been for ten months, as it was in December when Kawahira first measured it and told him what nine meant.

**Flame:** goes where he puts it and stays.

Then he did the part he does not do monthly, because there is no reason to and because it is unpleasant.

He took the **Sky** up, on his own, in the dark. He held it at the purity he can reach, and then pushed past that, as he has done twice before, and counted.

Three seconds. Then three and a half. Then his hands would not close properly for eleven minutes and he sat on the boards of the sessha with the fox against his ribs and waited for them to come back.

*Three and a half,* he thought, when he could hold a pen again. *Last February it was three. So that is half a second in eight months, at nine years old, in a body that is still growing and will keep growing and will not stop being the bottleneck for a while yet.*

*And the thing in Palermo wants minutes.*

*So the arithmetic upstairs was not pessimism. It was arithmetic. My own body has just signed it.*

⚠️ He wrote the three and a half in the notebook under the October entry, in the ordinary column, in the ordinary hand, with no comment beside it. It is still there. It is the only measurement in nine years of that notebook that he has never once gone back and looked at.

*And the part I am going to have to carry is that I knew it was coming. I have known for eight years. I could have spent those eight years becoming somebody who could do something about this. Instead I spent them building a town that does not need me, and a paper trail that says I do not exist. I would make both of those choices again. They have cost exactly this.*

He did not sleep much. In the morning he put the cutting in the drawer under the loose board, with the form from Ise and the copy of the note that says nothing was decided, and he went down and did the storeroom count.

⁂

Musashi found him on the steps that evening, which was not an accident, because she had been finding him on those steps at intervals since he was one year old.

She did not ask what was in the envelope. She sat down, which at seventy-five on those steps is a decision, and she looked out at the town for a bit.

"There is a thing my mother said," she said eventually, "which I disliked for about forty years and have come round to."

"You never quote your mother!"

"I am seventy-five and it is cold and you have had a bad envelope," said Musashi. "I shall quote whoever I like."

"She said that the whole of being a **Kohaku** is knowing the difference between a thing you are not doing and a thing you cannot do. She said most of them never learn it. And she said the ones who do learn it usually learn it too late to enjoy the difference." She put her hands on her knees. "I am not asking you which one tonight. I am telling you that there is a difference, and that you are allowed to know which one it is, and that you do not have to tell me."

"It is the second one."

"Mm," said Musashi.

They sat there a while longer. Down the hill the lights were coming on in the order they come on, which is not the order of the streets.

"Is it going to be the second one for long?"

"Four years," said Tsuna. "Possibly five."

"Then you have got four years," said his grandmother.

She got as far as the door and stopped there, with her hand on the frame, and did not turn round.

"When I was thirty-one there was a **Ketsueki** in this town who I could not help," she said. "I was the **Gen'ei** of the house and I was thirty-one and I could not do it, and I spent eleven years being sure that was a thing about me."

"Was it?"

"No. It was a thing about thirty-one." Musashi went in to see about the rice. "Eat something."

## 4.6

On the last Sunday of the year he got the sheet out from under the loose board and looked at it properly for the first time since the spring.

**SINCE JANUARY.** It had been headed that for twenty-three months by then, and he had never changed the heading, because changing it would have meant admitting how long it had been. Four columns. Sixty-one entries. And the fourth column with a line ruled through the top of it and the words **WRONG QUESTION** written above in his own small hand, and sixty-one blank rows underneath, going down the page like a held breath.

He had known since January what the right question was. He had written it out in the middle of a much longer thought on a cold night and then not done anything with it for eleven months.

*It was never did it need me. Every one of those things was somebody doing something without being asked.*

So he took the pen, and he wrote the new heading at the top of the fourth column, and it is four words long.

> **WITHOUT BEING ASKED**

Then he went back to January and worked forwards.

He did it honestly, which is the only way he knows how to do this particular kind of thing, and which is not always a kindness to himself.

**Genichi**, in February, in a room with nine people in it, saying that a conclusion is not a measurement. Nobody asked him. He had decided it in January on a set of steps and had been waiting four months for somewhere to put it.

**Ayame**, copying a visitor log twice and giving one of the copies to a nine year old who did not understand why.

**Musashi**, asking rather than telling. Twice in two months, at seventy-five, after sixty years of never once having to.

**Noriyuki**, walking eleven kilometres from a bus stop to a town nobody had invited him to, and not mentioning it.

**Tomoko**, who was asked to find that boy once, eleven years ago, by people who meant to sell him. Who has been trying to find him ever since, on her own money, for eight years. And who did not tell one person in this town that she was doing it, including him, including on the doorstep, with the page in her hand.

**Kazuki**, who has carried the winter cuttings across that yard every February for nineteen years and has never once been told why the room stands up.

**Ferri**, settling a hedge argument in August because two households asked him, and going home and telling nobody that it was the first time in thirty-two years.

**The boy himself**, aged three, building an examination out of the only thing he had and then sitting inside it for eight years, alone, and marking it.

Sixty-one rows. He filled in forty-four of them and left the rest, because he does not write down a thing he cannot check.

And doing it took him most of the evening, which surprised him, because he had expected the column to be thin.

It was not thin. Namimori turns out to be a place where a very great deal is done by people nobody asked, and he had been living inside that for nine years and counting it as weather. A **Chiyu** who carries cuttings. A **Ketsueki** who runs a boundary. A **Getsufuji** of ten with fifty boys and a system of shifts that nobody authorised. A **Gen'ei** of seven who holds a roof for eleven minutes and is sick afterwards and goes back the following week.

He counted the **Flames** in the column when he had finished, because he counts things. Three **Ketsueki**. Two **Chiyu**. One **Getsufuji**, one **Gen'ei**, one **Yasuragi**, one **Ryokugyoku**. And nineteen people with no **Flame** at all, which is the largest group by a distance and which he had not expected and sat looking at for a while.

*That is a town,* he thought, looking at forty-four lines of other people's names.

*That is what the word means. It is not the contract. It is not the seven houses and their bonds, and it is not the **Kotei**, and it is certainly not the **Sanctuary** instrument that four hundred years of people have been so careful with.*

*It is the number of people in a place who will do a thing without being asked. Everything else is scaffolding round that number.*

*And the scaffolding matters! I have spent a year proving it matters. A town with no instrument gets eaten. But the instrument is not the thing. The instrument is what you build so that the thing has somewhere to happen.*

Then he came to his own.

*And there it is,* he thought, looking at a column of other people's names on a piece of paper in a cold room. *Not one line. Not one, in thirteen months.*

*Because every single thing I did this year, I did for one of three reasons. Because it was my office. Because somebody put a document in front of me. Or because a man who has been dead for four centuries wrote out four clauses and I signed them.*

*I have not done one thing without being asked since the eighth of January, and the reason is not that I am lazy and it is not that I am good.*

*It is that I have spent the whole year being told what I may not do, by a paper I signed, by a paper I forged, and by my own arithmetic. And all three of them were right.*

He looked at the empty part of his own column for some while, and then he did the thing he has done since he was three, which is to work it through twice before letting himself feel any part of it.

*Right. Second pass. Is that actually the year?*

*…No. It is not.*

*I gave her back a page I was not obliged to give her, in a week when nobody knew I had it, and I could have kept it for another month and nobody on earth would have known.*

*That is one. That is one line, and it is mine, and it is the only one.*

He wrote it in. It took four words and it is the only entry in that column in his own name for the whole of that year, and he has never shown that sheet to anybody.

⁂

It is eleven weeks until a boy of eleven, in a house with a door that opens both ways, asks the woman who found him a question about a shrine in Mie, and does not get an answer, because she does not know it either.

It is four years until Italy. It may be six.

And it is nine days until the first Sunday of January. On that morning Sawada Masaya, aged nine, will get out a clean sheet of paper, and rule four columns on it, and write **SINCE JANUARY** at the top of it again.

He has not yet worked out that he is allowed to stop counting.
