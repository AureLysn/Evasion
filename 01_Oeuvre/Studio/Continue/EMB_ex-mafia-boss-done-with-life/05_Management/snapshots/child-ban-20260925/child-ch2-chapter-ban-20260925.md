# Local Boy Invites The Vindice To Dinner. Local Town Has Several Small Heart Attacks.

## 2.1

The letter went out on a Sunday at four in the morning, and then nothing happened for ten weeks.

He did not spend the ten weeks waiting, exactly. A **Sanctuary** does not stop because its **Kotei** has posted something to a prison. The storeroom count came due and was done. Two households fell out over a wall and were brought up the steps and fell back in again. Kyouya's Committee extended its bicycle policy to cover handcarts, which produced a week of correspondence that Tsuna read in full, with enormous pleasure, lying on his stomach on the boards of the room with the door that does not close.

The sheet headed **SINCE JANUARY** gained three entries. None of them was about Vendicare.

*Good,* he thought, on the third Sunday, with the lamp down low and the fox draped across the back of his neck. *If I write it down it becomes a thing I am waiting for, and I refuse to wait for a man who keeps his office in a place where the clocks are a suggestion.*

The fox had taken to lying there that autumn. It had started in October, when the evenings got cold, and it had never once been invited. It settled along his shoulders with its tail across his collarbone and its chin on the far side, and it held so still that anybody who could see it at all would have taken it for a very small, very expensive scarf. Nobody in the house could see it at all. It did the scarf anyway. Tsuna suspected it was practising.

*For what, though?* he asked it, not aloud. *Who are you planning to fool?*

One ear flicked against his jaw. That was the whole answer, and it was a smug one.

Somewhere in the ninth week he noticed that he had already decided what he would do if they came, and that he had decided it a long time ago, and that it was not a sensible decision.

He turned it over on the roof of the storeroom one night, in the cold, where he goes when he wants to argue with himself without being interrupted.

*Nobody invites the Vindice anywhere.* That was the first thing, and it was true. *Nobody in their right mind would think of it. No, that is too generous. Nobody in their right mind would get as far as beginning to think of it. The thought does not have a front door.*

And yet.

He thought of Grandmother at the long table last year, with the Varia across from her, reading the clause out loud twice, slowly, badly, stumbling on the old verb in the third line so that nobody afterwards could say they had not been told. He thought of Ayame pushing her left sleeve up past the scar before she walks the north border, every single time, the way other people check that the stove is off. It is a scar she should never have had to get, on the arm that carries her Storm Flame. She is prouder of it than of anything else she owns. He thought of Nagi, who had started, this winter, to play small tricks on him with her Mist Flame: a teacup that was not where it was, a door that opened on the wrong side, a single wrong stair that turned out to be the right one when he stepped on it anyway. She did it with a completely flat face and then watched him to see if he would laugh. He always did. He was never sure she knew why.

And he thought of Kawahira and Mukuro, who had gone off together three days after Christmas on some errand neither of them would describe, who got on like a house on fire, and who wrote to him on Mondays. There had been two Monday letters so far. They were cheerful and detailed and illustrated. His blood pressure went up when each one arrived, and he sulked about it for most of the morning, and he read every single one twice.

*All of them have frightened me this year,* he thought. *Every one. Some of them on purpose.*

*So if a Vindice walks into my town, I am going to invite him to dinner. And everybody is going to have a small heart attack. And I am going to count that as rent.*

The fox, across his shoulders, flicked both ears at once, which it does when it is pleased with him.

*You would think that was reasonable,* he told it. *You are a fox, and you have no standards.*

It yawned into his collar.

*And if Grandmother is angry?*

He thought about that for a while, honestly, because it was the one part of the plan that could actually go wrong.

*Then I look at her the way I looked at her about the second kotatsu. And if that does not work, I get Nagi.*

Nobody in that house had ever held out against both of them at once. He did not expect his grandmother to be the first.

## 2.2

They came on the eighth of January, at nine minutes past two in the afternoon, in snow.

Ayame saw it first, because Ayame was on the north border, because Ayame is always on the north border when something is about to go wrong, and nobody has ever proved that she arranges this.

She told him about it afterwards in exact words, which is how she tells everything. The snow by the northern boundary stone had been lying flat and untrodden since the morning. Then a ring opened in the air above it, about the height of a door, and the ring was black. The snow under it did not melt. It went dark, as if something had been spilled on it that was the opposite of light, and then the ring was simply a way through, and three figures stepped out of it and stood on the Namimori side of the stone as if they had been standing there all day.

*Night Flame,* Tsuna thought, when she got to that part, and kept his face perfectly still. *Short warp. No wasted fire at the edges. He is showing off a little, or he is being polite in the only language he has that needs no interpreter.*

Two of them were tall and wrapped in black to the eyes, with the high collars and the long coats and the bandages under the hat brims that every mafia child has been told about in the dark at least once. One wore a hat that was, Ayame said, remarkable. The other wore no hat at all, and on his left shoulder, perfectly balanced, sat a third figure the size of an infant.

The small one's pacifier was clear. It caught the grey light like a glass bead.

Ayame said she stood there for a count of four with her hand on her sleeve. Then she did the thing the north border is for, which is to stay exactly where she was, between them and the town, with her Storm Flame banked hot under her skin, and not move.

Everybody in Namimori with enough of a Flame to feel weather felt it come down the valley, each in the register of their own: the Storm households as pressure behind the eyes, the Sun households as cold in the hands, the Mist as a room with one wall suddenly missing. And further off, in seven houses across East Asia, seven house heads who hold the Flame bonds of this **Sanctuary** felt the same weather in the same second, and put their hands on their bonds, and sat very still to see whether this was the day they pulled…

Namimori has a procedure for most things. It has a procedure for Vindice, which is that the Vindice come when the contract is broken and the town gets out of their way. It has never needed a procedure for Vindice who arrive when nothing has been broken, stand politely at the boundary stone in the snow, and wait.

So the town did what it does when it has no procedure. It stood very still and looked up the hill.

Tsuna was in the accounts room. He had felt the ring open as a cold spot at the back of his head, a Flame that was not any of the seven and did not want to be, like a draught from a door somebody had left ajar in another building, and he had put his pen down and waited to see whether it would move. It did not. It stayed at the stone like a caller who has knocked once and intends to be patient about it, and after a minute of that he stood up, and the fox rose with him and settled around his neck, and he went and found his coat.

Nagi, under the table with a book, asked where he was going, and he told her he was going to invite some people to dinner. She considered this and asked which people. He said the ones at the north stone.

Nagi came out from under the table. She is six and she does not run in the house, but she walked to the window very fast, and looked out, and looked for some time.

"They're Vindice," she said, and when he agreed that they were: "You can't invite Vindice to dinner!"

He asked her why not.

She opened her mouth, and closed it, and he watched her go looking for the rule and fail to find it. It was one of the most satisfying things he saw all winter.

"Sensei will be angry," she said at last, and he said that she probably would.

"Can I come?"

"No. Go and tell her we're having guests. Tell her three. Tell her one of them is small."

He went down the hill in the snow with his hands in his pockets and his Sky Flame folded small and quiet under his ribs, where it could not be mistaken for a challenge.

*What is the worst that happens?* he asked himself on the second bend. *He says no. What is the second worst?* He considered that one for a while, and then decided that he would rather not know until he had to.

The Committee got there before he did, which he had expected, because the Committee gets everywhere before everybody. Kyouya was not with them. Kyouya was on the roof of the school building a hundred metres back, sitting on the parapet with his hands in his sleeves, watching, and Tsuna understood from the angle of him that he would come down the moment he was needed and not one second before. The Cloud on that roof has opinions about being needed, and most of them are loud!

At the bottom of the north road, where the boundary stone sits, the Committee had set out its folding table. There was a small boy behind it with a clipboard. He was perhaps nine. He was the youngest member the Committee had, and it showed in the way he held the pen, which was as if he meant to stab somebody with it if the visitor log required.

The visitor log requires three things of anyone who crosses into Namimori by the north road on foot, and the boy asked all three, in order, in a voice that cracked only on the first. Name, first.

The tall one with the hat said nothing. The tall one without a hat said nothing. The small one on his shoulder inclined his head, very slightly, in the manner of a man acknowledging a clerk who is doing his duty correctly.

"Bermuda von Veckenschtein," he said. His voice was dry and thin and perfectly courteous, with an accent that had been out of fashion for a hundred years. "Of Vendicare. This is Jäger. The gentleman in the hat is not giving his name today, and I would ask you to excuse him."

The boy wrote it down, and his hand did not shake; Tsuna, who has since seen the page, was very proud of him and has never told him so. House: *Vendicare will do.* The boy wrote that down too. Then he looked at the last line of his form, and at the three figures, and at the snow, and decided, as he explained to Tsuna much later with great seriousness, that the form was the form.

"Did you bring a bicycle?"

There was a pause. It was not a long pause. It was, however, a pause in which the Warden of Vendicare looked down at a nine year old with a clipboard, and the whole of the north road held its breath.

"We did not," said Bermuda. "Is it customary?"

"It's policy," the boy said. "If you bring one you have to leave it here."

"Then I am glad to have spared you the trouble."

The boy wrote *no*. Tsuna has since seen the page. The *no* is very neat.

That was when Tsuna came round the last bend.

He had seen them before, in a different life, and the second time was no easier. The long black coats and the bandages, and the shape under the coat of the hatted one, at the sternum, a hard round weight about the size of a child's fist, hidden but not hidden well enough for someone who knew to look for it. A pacifier, stone grey, the Flame gone out of it so long ago that even the colour had died. Jäger would have one under his collar as well. Only the small one wore his in the open, clear as water on a cord, because only the small one had a Flame of his own to fill it.

*Hello,* Tsuna thought. *You look well, which is a lie, you look like a corpse in a good coat, but it is the kind of lie that one says.*

He stopped at a polite distance from the table, which put him about four metres from Jäger's boots. The fox lay perfectly still around his neck. He felt it breathe once against his ear and then stop, the way a cat stops when it has decided to become furniture.

Bermuda turned his head.

It was not a large movement. It did not need to be. For a second or two the Warden of Vendicare looked at Tsuna, and then at Tsuna's collar, and then at Tsuna again, and Tsuna could not have said, then or afterwards, whether the look had stayed on his shoulder one breath longer than a look at a collar needs to stay.

*Did you see that?* he thought at the fox. *Did he see you?*

The fox did not answer, because it was being a scarf with its whole heart.

"You will be the Sky of Namimori," said Bermuda, who had never in his life, Tsuna suspected, called anybody by a Flame they did not have.

"I'm the **Kotei**," Tsuna said. "Here. It comes to about the same thing." He bowed, properly, the correct depth for a host receiving someone who outranks him. "Thank you for coming so far. It's a long way, and it's cold, and it's going to be dark soon. Would you like to come up to the house and have dinner with us?"

He heard the small boy at the table drop his pen.

He heard, further back, something that might have been Ayame inhaling through her teeth. Somewhere up the hill a door shut very hard. On the school roof, Kyouya sat up.

Jäger turned his head, slowly, to look at the Warden on his shoulder.

Bermuda looked at Tsuna for what felt like the whole of a winter.

"Dinner…" he said.

"It's only nabe. It's January. My grandmother does a good one, and she'll be offended if there's a guest at the north stone and nobody feeds him." Tsuna considered. "She'll be offended anyway. But less."

*Say no,* said a small sensible voice at the back of his head, the one that has survived three wars and a funeral. *Please say no. Say no and hand me an envelope and go home.*

"How very kind," said Bermuda von Veckenschtein. "We should be delighted."

The small boy picked up his pen. He wrote something else on the visitor log, in the margin, very small. Tsuna has seen that page as well. It says *DINNER???* and it is underlined twice, and then, underneath, in smaller letters, *they said yes!*

Later, in the town, people would argue about who had the worst afternoon. The heads of the seven houses, with their palms on their bonds for forty minutes while a nine year old asked a Vindice about bicycles? Ayame, who had to walk behind the Warden of Vendicare all the way up the hill because the north border walks behind guests, and who said afterwards that it was the longest eleven minutes of her life and that Jäger's coat smelled of rain in a stone room? The Committee boy, who went home and would not eat? Kyouya, who came down off the school roof and fell in beside Tsuna for the last two hundred metres, and said nothing, and at the gate of the house asked his one question?

"Does he have a bicycle."

"No."

Kyouya nodded, satisfied, and went to stand in the yard with his arms folded, where he could see the dining room window. He stood there for four hours.

Most people agreed afterwards that the worst afternoon was Grandmother's. They were wrong. Grandmother had the second worst. The worst was the man who delivers the fish, who came up the hill at a quarter past three with the order for the house, rounded the corner of the kitchen, and met Jäger coming the other way.

## 2.3

Grandmother was standing in the front hall when they came in. She was wearing the grey kimono, the good one, which meant she had changed in under nine minutes and put her hair up with a thread of Mist Flame, which she does only for funerals, which Tsuna had not known was possible and which he filed away as a fact about his grandmother that he had never wanted to need.

She did not look at the Vindice. She looked at him.

It was a very short look. It contained, as far as he could tell, the entire history of the Sawada family, three separate opinions about his upbringing, and a date on which he would be spoken to. Then it was gone, and she turned to the guests and bowed exactly as deep as the head of a house bows to a visitor whose rank she has not been told and does not intend to guess.

"Sawada Ruriko," she said. "Ruri of the Sawada. You are welcome in this house."

Tsuna had not heard her use her formal name to a stranger in three years. He understood that it was a wall, built out of courtesy, very quickly, in the front hall.

Bermuda understood it too. He inclined his head from Jäger's shoulder, and then, with great care, as if it were the most natural thing in the world, he stepped down.

He did not jump, and there was no Flame in it that Tsuna could see; he simply stepped off a tall man's shoulder into the air of the hall and was, a moment later, standing on the step in front of her, a figure about as high as her knee, in a black coat that fitted him beautifully.

"Madam," he said. "Bermuda von Veckenschtein, of Vendicare, at your service. I must apologise for the hour and for the weather and for my young host, who I believe did not consult you."

"He did not."

"I thought not. He has that look." Bermuda turned, and looked up at Tsuna, and something in the bandaged face moved in a way that on anybody else would have been a smile. "One knows it. One was young oneself, once, in a different century."

*Oh no…* Tsuna thought. *He's charming, and nobody warned me he could be charming, and Reborn never said.*

Then he thought, *Reborn would not have said. Reborn would have found it funny,* and he had to look at the floor for a second.

The table was the problem, and it was a real one.

The dining room of the Sawada house seats ten on the floor at the low table, and eight comfortably, and on most nights it seats three. On alternate Sundays it seats five, because on alternate Sundays an antique dealer comes up the hill with his student, eats whatever is put in front of him, and complains for an hour about the price of ink.

The house had been keeping their things since before Christmas, the way a house does for people who are expected back at no fixed time. There was a chipped blue cup on the shelf by the screen that only Mukuro would drink from, because it was ugly and he said it looked like a person. There was a flat grey cushion with a scorch on one corner that nobody else was allowed to sit on. And on the low table under the window there was a shogi board with a game on it, eleven moves from the end, which had been left exactly as it was on the twenty-seventh of December with instructions that nobody touch it on pain of a curse Mukuro had described in some detail.

Tsuna walked into the dining room ahead of the guests and dealt with the cup and the cushion in about four seconds. The cushion went behind the screen. The blue cup went into his coat pocket, where it clinked against the fox, which did not move.

The shogi board he could do nothing about. You cannot move a game eleven moves from the end without it looking exactly like a game somebody has hidden.

*Who looks at a shogi board?* he asked himself. *Nobody. Nobody at all, except a man who has spent several hundred years counting what was taken and not returned, looking for one very particular absence, who would notice at once that a game has two players and neither of them is in the room.*

Grandmother, in the doorway, saw him do it. She did not ask. She put it on the list of things she would ask later, he could see her do it, and then she came in and began to arrange her guests.

There was the question of where to put the Warden. The head of the table was hers. The guest of honour sits on the right hand of the head. The guest of honour, in this case, was about as tall as the table.

Nagi solved it. Nagi went out of the room without a word and came back a minute later with a stack of three flat cushions and, on top of them, the small square one with the embroidered rabbit that she had used until she was four, and which she kept in a cupboard because she said it was not finished with her yet.

She put the stack down on the right hand of the head of the table. She put the rabbit cushion on top. Then she stepped back and looked at the Warden of Vendicare with her completely flat face and waited.

Tsuna held his breath while Bermuda regarded the rabbit for some moments. Then he climbed the stack, with great dignity, and sat on it, and arranged the skirts of his coat, and folded his small bandaged hands in front of him on the lacquer.

"Thank you, young lady," he said. "It is a very good rabbit."

"It's for special guests," said Nagi.

This was not true. It was, as far as Tsuna knew, the first time she had ever lent it to anybody. She sat down beside Tsuna and did not look at him, and under the table her foot found his ankle and pressed it once, hard, which is what she does when she has done something and wants to be sure he saw.

*I saw,* he thought. *I will never recover.*

Jäger did not sit. He stood behind the Warden's cushion with his back to the screen, where he could see the door and the window and the whole of the room, and he did not take off his coat, or show the stone pacifier under it. The hatted one had not come up the hill at all. He had stayed at the bottom by the boundary stone, standing beside the Committee's folding table in the snow, and Tsuna learned later that the Committee had brought him a chair and a flask of tea and that he had not touched the tea but had sat in the chair, which the Committee took as a great victory.

The fish man had recovered enough to deliver the fish. Grandmother went into the kitchen to see to it herself, which she never does, and which meant she wanted four minutes alone.

That left Tsuna, Nagi, the Warden of Vendicare on a rabbit, and Jäger, in a dining room that suddenly felt very small.

Bermuda said that it was a charming house, and Tsuna thanked him and said it was old.

"All the best ones are." The Warden looked round the room with evident interest. His gaze passed over the shelf by the screen and did not stop. It came to rest on the shogi board under the window, and stayed there, and Tsuna felt his heart go round once, like a coin on a table. "Someone is in the middle of a very interesting game."

*There it is.*

"It's mine…" said Nagi.

Tsuna did not look at her, because he did not dare.

"I play both sides," she said, in the flat helpful voice she uses when she reads aloud. "I'm losing."

She did not play shogi. She had never played shogi in her life. Nobody had told her to say anything at all. She had simply looked at the board, and at Tsuna putting a cushion behind a screen in four seconds with his face held very carefully still, and decided on her own account whose game it was. She said it with complete conviction. It was, he realised, the first lie she had ever told in front of him that was not a trick with a teacup.

It was a good lie because it was boring, and nobody interrogates a six year old about shogi.

"Both sides," said Bermuda. "How very sensible. It is the only way to be quite sure of the quality of one's opponent."

"The other side cheats," said Nagi.

Tsuna looked at the ceiling and composed himself. On his shoulder, the fox let out one long slow breath against his neck, and he felt its tail twitch, once, across his collarbone.

## 2.4

Grandmother came back with the pot.

Nabe in January in the Sawada house is closer to a weather system than a meal. There is the pot on the burner in the middle of the table, and the steam off it, and the plates of things to go in: fish, tofu, cabbage, the good mushrooms, the leeks cut on the slant the way Grandmother insists and nobody else has ever managed. Everybody reaches. Nobody waits to be served. It is the least formal thing that house does, and she had chosen it, Tsuna realised, on purpose. You cannot hold a Vindice at arm's length across a pot you are both putting your chopsticks in.

*She is fighting,* he thought, with enormous affection. *She has lost the front hall and she is fighting from the kitchen.*

Bermuda handled the chopsticks like a man who had learned them a very long time ago and practised since. He took one piece of tofu and one of fish and a leaf of cabbage and ate them with evident attention, and then he put the chopsticks down across the rest and did not reach again for some while.

"Is it not to your taste?" said Grandmother.

"It is excellent, madam. I regret that I do not eat very much. It is not a matter of preference." He paused. "It has been some years since anyone has cooked for me at all. You will forgive me if I take my time over it."

Grandmother looked at him for a moment. Then she picked up the ladle and put a second piece of fish in his bowl without asking, exactly as she does for Tsuna, and exactly as she did, once, for a man from the Varia who had not eaten in two days.

"Take it slowly, then," she said.

*Did she just give the Warden of Vendicare seconds?* Tsuna ate his rice and looked at the pot and felt something in his chest go warm and complicated, and decided to deal with it at some other time.

They talked, for a while, about nothing: the snow, the trains, whether the Italian word for a certain kind of mushroom was the same as the French. Bermuda had a way of talking about nothing that was clearly a skill, formed over years of dinners in houses that were afraid of him, and he used it on Grandmother like a key cut long ago for a lock he visited often. He asked after her garden. He admired the screen. He asked Nagi what she was reading and listened to the answer, which was long and was about crabs, with complete seriousness.

*He is good at this,* Tsuna thought. *He is very good at this. That is not reassuring.*

Then, with the pot down to broth and the rice nearly gone, the Warden of Vendicare folded his hands on the table and said, quite pleasantly, "Do you know, Sawada-dono, I keep a gallery."

"Oh?" said Grandmother.

"Not a fashionable one. It is in a cave." Bermuda's head tilted. "There are paintings on the walls. They are very old, and they are not very good, and I have spent a great deal of time looking at them. Each one marks a particular kind of day. The day when a certain gentleman comes to choose the seven strongest Flames in the world, one Sky and one each of Storm, Rain, Sun, Lightning, Cloud and Mist, and give them something to carry. And the day, some years later, when he comes back to choose again."

The steam went up off the broth between them, and Tsuna did not move.

He knew the cave, because he had stood in it at fourteen, with Reborn on his shoulder and the Warden of Vendicare in front of him in the dark, and looked up at the paintings with a torch, one after another, the same seven figures in the same seven colours and the same tall shape standing over them. Selection. Battle. Selection. Battle. All the way back until the stone was too worn to read.

*You are going to tell her about the paintings,* he thought. *You told me about the paintings. You told me in the dark and I didn't sleep for a week.*

*Don't react. You are eight, and you have never heard of a cave.*

He picked up his cup and drank from it, and it was empty, and he drank from it anyway.

"The seven are called Arcobaleno, now, and what they carry is a pacifier," Bermuda went on. "They have had other names. And if one counts the paintings, and one is patient, one learns something. A set of seven does not last forever. It lasts, on the whole, twenty years. Sometimes thirty. Then the burden wears them through, and the gentleman comes back, and there is a new painting."

"And you count them," said Grandmother.

"I have nothing but time, madam. I count everything." He paused. "The present seven have held for somewhat over thirty years."

Nagi looked up from her bowl. "Is that bad?"

"It is interesting," said Bermuda. "Which is not the same as bad, young lady, but is often more expensive." He turned his small head towards her, very courteously, as if she were a colleague. "There are reasons. One of them carries a Cloud Flame of truly ridiculous size. More Flame than sense, I am afraid, but it helps. The seven are, all of them, very strong, perhaps the strongest set there has ever been. And there was an accident at their choosing. A woman was chosen, and a man followed her in who was not meant to be there, and at the last moment the burden decided it liked him better. So now there are seven who carry it properly, and one who carries a part of it without the name, and the weight is shared among eight instead of seven." He opened one hand, slightly. "A little lighter for everyone. A little longer."

*Lal,* Tsuna thought. *Colonnello, and the Rain pacifier that picked the wrong soldier and was right to. Of course you know about them. You've been watching them since before they were born.*

"But not forever," said Grandmother.

"No. And there is one of the seven Arcobaleno who always goes first, madam. It is always the Sky." Bermuda's voice did not change. "The Sky carries the most. The Sky breaks earliest. The present set has already had two. The first died. The second holds the post now, and she is not well, and she has no child yet to take it from her. One can count that, too."

*Luce. Aria.* And a girl not born yet, who would be born, who would look up at him from under a white hat in a future that was not coming, and smile, and say that she knew. *Uni.*

He put the cup down very carefully.

"So you watch," said Grandmother.

"So I watch. I am sure you understand. When a painting is about to be made, it is useful to know in advance who is likely to be in it." Bermuda turned, then, not quickly, and looked down the length of the table at Tsuna. "And I will be frank with you both, because I have eaten your fish and it would be ungracious not to be. The gentleman who does the choosing is someone I have been trying to meet again for a very long time. He does not answer letters. He comes, however, for the strongest. He always comes for the strongest. And so for some centuries now I have made it my business to know where the strongest Flames in the world are sitting, and to be sitting nearby when he arrives."

*And there it is,* Tsuna thought. *There's the whole of it.*

*You are not here about my letter. You are here about the thing that might be in the painting after next.*

The shogi board sat under the window like a held breath. Somewhere in Japan or out of it, a man who had lived in these woods before there was a village was doing something he would not describe with a boy who drank from a cup that looked like a person. And the only thing that had ever stood between that man and the creature on the rabbit cushion was that nobody had yet had any reason to look for him in Namimori.

*I am the reason,* Tsuna thought. *I am the strongest Sky Flame in a hundred kilometres and I am sitting here eating cabbage and I have his student's teacup in my pocket.*

The fox on his shoulder had gone very still, and it was no longer the stillness of a scarf.

"I'm eight," he said, and when Bermuda agreed that he was: "I don't think I'd be much use in a painting."

"No? Perhaps not." The Warden's bandaged head tilted, very slightly. "I would not worry, if I were you. I am only a very old man with a very long memory, visiting a house where someone has been kind to him. Old men look at children. It is one of the few pleasures left to us." He turned back to Grandmother. "With your permission, madam, I should like to call again. Once or twice in the year. Nothing formal. I should like to see how the garden comes on."

He was not talking about the garden. Everybody at the table knew he was not talking about the garden, including Nagi, who had stopped eating.

*That is a surveillance request,* Tsuna thought. *That is a surveillance request with its hat off, asking to be let in out of the snow.*

*And if she says no, he comes anyway, and stands at the north stone, and does it from outside… and if she says yes…*

He looked at the Warden of Vendicare, sitting very upright on a cushion with a rabbit embroidered on it, with a second piece of fish going cold in his bowl because a grandmother had put it there, and he thought, quite suddenly and against every single thing he knew about the man: *He would be good at this.*

*Not the watching, but the rest of it, being the adult in the room. He would be a good adult to have. Patient, honest in the ways that matter and polite in the ways that don't. He would come to the house twice a year and bring something terrible from a European shop and ask Nagi about crabs and remember the answer. He would tell me the truth about anything I asked him.*

*Anything except one thing.*

*And about that one thing… he would pull this town apart with his fingers.*

"You're very welcome to visit," he said, before Grandmother could answer. "Any time. Tell us first, though. The fish man's heart isn't very good."

There was a pause while Grandmother looked at him, and he did not look back. He had just given away the only thing she had left to decide, at her own table, and he would pay for that later, and he knew it.

"Twice a year," Grandmother said at last. Her voice was perfectly even. "Write first. Come by the road."

"Madam," said Bermuda, and inclined his head. "You are very kind."

*She will never forgive me for this,* Tsuna thought. *She will forgive me by Sunday.*

## 2.5

There was yōkan after, cut thin, and tea. Bermuda ate one slice of the yōkan with a small fork that Grandmother fetched for him from somewhere without being asked, and pronounced it the best he had eaten in Japan, which was probably true. Then he folded his napkin and put it beside his plate and said, "And now, I think, I owe you an answer."

Jäger moved, behind him, for the first time in two hours. He reached inside his coat and brought out a long envelope, dark grey, sealed in black, and laid it on the table in front of the Warden.

*Here it comes…* Tsuna thought. *The bill.*

He had spent two months preparing to pay it. He had four kinds of currency ready in his head, stacked like trays. There was money, of which the **Sanctuary** had more than people thought, because a town full of Flame users does not pay for its own firewood. There was service, an open favour of the kind Vendicare had been known to take from very old families. There was information, and he had a great deal of that, some of it from a future nobody else had seen. And there was his own name, the real one, the one that his letter had been careful not to use, the one he had spent the whole of the autumn deciding he would not give up and knew he would give up if it came to it.

He had been ready for any of those. He had not been ready for a guest to read him the bill after dessert, as a courtesy, in a voice like a man proposing a toast.

"It is customary, where I was young," said Bermuda, "for a guest who has been fed to return the favour in writing. So I shall read you my reply, and then I shall leave it with you, and you may do with it as you like." He broke the seal with one small finger. "There are four clauses. I will ask you to forgive the form. My office has been writing in this form for a very long time and has forgotten how to stop."

He read.

"*One.* The question put to this office is answered yes. There is a line."

Tsuna breathed out, very slowly, through his nose.

"*Two.* The line is supplied whole and without omission. No payment is taken for it. Not money, not goods, not service, not information." A pause. "*The Warden does not take payment for the reading of a book.*"

Four trays, put down, one after another, in Tsuna's head. He had not even had time to pick one up.

*Then what?* he thought. *Then what, then what, then…*

"*Three.* This office refers to the enquirer's letter of July, a year and a half ago, concerning the erasure of a famiglia and a Sky's right of retaliation notwithstanding a declared neutrality. The Warden put a question at that time. The enquirer answered it." Bermuda turned the page. "*The second clause of that letter remains under consideration.*"

Grandmother's eyes went to Tsuna. He kept his on the Warden.

"*Four.* For so long as the second clause remains under consideration, the enquirer shall not himself go to recover the child. Nor send. Nor arrange, purchase, commission or set out the means. Nor be the hand." Bermuda lowered the page an inch. "*Should the child come to him, that is the child's doing and not the enquirer's, and this office will not look at it.*"

He folded the letter and laid it on the table.

The steam had stopped rising off the pot. Somewhere outside, very faintly, Tsuna could hear Kyouya shift his weight in the snow.

*He has not taken one thing I own,* Tsuna thought. *He has taken something I am allowed to do.*

*And I do not keep a ledger for those. It has never once occurred to me, in forty-eight years, that being allowed was a thing a person could run out of.*

It was, he realised, extremely funny. It was also the most elegant piece of extortion he had seen in two lifetimes, and he had been married, briefly, to the Vongola accounts.

"You can't tell him that!" said Nagi.

Everybody looked at her. *Where did that come from?* Tsuna thought, and then, *oh. From me. It came from me.*

She had gone pink. She is six and she does not raise her voice in the house, and she had not meant to, and now that it was out she did not know what to do with it. She looked at the Warden of Vendicare, on her rabbit, with her fists on her knees, and her chin up.

"You can't tell him what he's not allowed," she said. "This is his house. He's the **Kotei**."

It was, Tsuna thought, the single most dangerous sentence anybody had spoken in that room all night, and it had been spoken in his defence, by a six year old, to a man who had once stood in front of Giotto and watched him sign.

Bermuda looked at her for some moments. Then he did something that Tsuna had not seen him do in either life. He bowed to her. Seated, from the waist, the whole small body inclining over the folded letter.

"You are quite right, young lady," he said. "In his house, he is. And I should never presume to tell a **Kotei** what he may do in his house. But the child is not in his house, you see. The child is in the world. And in the world, I am afraid, I am something rather worse than a guest."

Nagi looked at Tsuna.

"It's all right," Tsuna said. And then, because she deserved the truth and it would cost him nothing: "It's a fair price. It's a very good price, actually! I wish I'd thought of it."

"Thank you," said Bermuda, and sounded as though he meant it.

Grandmother had not spoken. She was reading the letter now, upside down, from where it lay, which Tsuna had not known she could do either. When she had finished she looked up.

"This touches the contract," she said.

"It does, madam. Which is why I am glad you have heard it at your own table and not at second hand." Bermuda inclined his head towards her. "Your **Sanctuary** is a very old document. My office sees to its keeping. I would not wish anyone to say, later, that its **Kotei** signed something in the dark."

"He has not signed it."

"No," Bermuda agreed. "He has not."

Everybody looked at Tsuna.

He took the pen that Jäger was holding out to him, which had appeared from somewhere, and he signed it, in the square hand he uses for the town, at the foot of the fourth clause. He did not read it again because he did not need to. On his shoulder, the fox pressed its nose against the hinge of his jaw, once, and held it there while he wrote.

Then Jäger put a second, thinner envelope on the table, and the Warden slid it across the lacquer with the tips of his small fingers until it rested beside Tsuna's bowl.

"The line," he said. "As promised. Whole and without omission. You may read it after we have gone. I find people prefer to read that sort of thing without being watched."

## 2.6

They left at twenty to nine, by the road, on foot, because Grandmother had said *come by the road* and the Warden of Vendicare apparently intended to start as he meant to go on.

Tsuna walked them to the gate. Kyouya was still in the yard, with snow on his shoulders, and he watched Jäger go past him with the expression of a boy memorising the dimensions of something he intends to fight later. Bermuda, from his high seat, looked down at him and said, "Good evening, young man. You keep a very orderly boundary, for a Cloud."

Kyouya considered this for a while.

"Yes," he said.

At the bottom of the hill the hatted one rose from his Committee chair and fell in behind them without a word, and the three of them walked north up the road in the dark, the small clear light of the pacifier going smaller and smaller, until just short of the boundary stone a black ring opened in the air and they went through it and were gone. The Committee boy logged the time of departure. He also logged, Tsuna learned later, a note under the departure which said *thanked us for the chair.*

Tsuna stood at the gate until his feet were cold, and then he went back into the house to be killed.

Grandmother was in the dining room. She had not cleared the table, and the rabbit was still there. She was sitting at the head of it with her hands folded, looking at the rabbit cushion, which was still on top of its stack at her right hand.

"You put," she said, "the Warden of Vendicare. On Nagi's rabbit."

"Nagi put him there."

She did not acknowledge this. "You brought him into my house. Without asking me. In the middle of the afternoon. With the fish not yet up the hill." Her voice was quite level. It was the level of a very full bowl. "You are the **Kotei** of a **Sanctuary**, Masaya, and you invited the Vindice to dinner. Nobody invites the Vindice to dinner. There is no one living who would think of it."

"Yes, Grandmother."

"Do you know what it did to Ayame? To walk behind that man all the way up the hill?"

"She said it was the longest eleven minutes of her life."

"It was the longest eleven minutes of *my* life, and I was in the kitchen." She shut her eyes. "And then you gave away my answer. At my own table."

That one was fair, and he did not argue it. He came and knelt on the far side of the table from her, beside Nagi's place, and he looked at her.

He did not do anything in particular with his face. That was the whole secret of it. He had worked out a long time ago, in a different house, with a different family, that the look worked best if you did not try; if you simply let yourself be exactly as sorry as you actually were, and exactly as eight as you actually were, and waited. He was quite sorry and he was very eight, and he waited.

Grandmother opened her eyes, and looked at him, and closed them again at once.

"Don't…" she said.

He waited.

"Masaya. I am seventy-five years old."

He waited a little more.

She opened her eyes. She was not quite beaten. He could see the place where she was holding the line, very firmly, with both hands, a long way back behind her face.

*All right,* he thought. *Reinforcements.*

He did not call for Nagi. He did not need to. Nagi had been in the doorway the entire time, in her nightclothes, with her hair down, and she had been waiting for exactly this. She came in without a sound and knelt beside him and looked at her teacher too.

Nagi does not do anything with her face either. Nagi's face does not do things. That is what makes it so devastating when she puts it next to his, at the same height, with the same patience, in silence.

Grandmother lasted nine seconds, and Tsuna counted every one.

"Oh, for… both of you," she said, and put her hand over her eyes. "Go to bed. Go to bed, both of you, before I say something I'll have to take back in front of the fish man. Nagi, take your rabbit."

Nagi took her rabbit. At the door she stopped and looked back at him, and then at the rabbit, and then at him, and said, "He said it was a very good rabbit."

"He did."

"It *is*!" said Nagi, and went, and Tsuna heard her going up the stairs one at a time, slowly, the way she does when she is very pleased and does not want anybody to know, with a thin thread of Mist Flame trailing up the stairwell after her like a cat that has not been told it can come.

Grandmother lowered her hand.

She said that he would come twice a year, and Tsuna said yes. She said that he was looking for someone, and Tsuna said yes to that too.

"Someone he thinks might come here." She did not ask whom. "Then I will want to know, before the summer, what he eats. If he eats anything. I will not have him sit at my table twice and have the same fish."

*Is she going to feed him?* Tsuna thought. *She is going to feed him.* *She is going to fight him with food until one of them dies, and it isn't going to be her.*

"I'll find out," he said.

"You will." She got up, slowly, with a hand on the table. At the door she stopped. "And Nagi does not play shogi."

"Nagi said that. I didn't tell her to."

"I know you didn't. She will have to learn now." Grandmother looked at him for a moment longer, and he could not read her face at all, which almost never happens. "Go to bed, Masaya."

He did not go to bed. He went up to the room with the door that does not close, and took Mukuro's blue cup out of his pocket and put it on the shelf, and sat down on the boards with the lamp low and the thin envelope in his lap.

The fox came down off his shoulders at last, stretched from its nose to the end of its tail, and lay down against his knee.

*You were very good,* he told it. *You were the best scarf in Japan!*

It looked at him with great seriousness. Then it looked at the window, north, where the road went, and then back at him.

*I know,* he thought. *I don't know if he saw you. I don't know if he saw anything. He looked at my collar once and I will be thinking about it until I'm ninety.*

He opened the envelope.

It was a single sheet, a copy in a clerk's hand, very old fashioned, ruled into six columns, with eleven lines on it. Ten of the eleven were not about the Saint-Exavier child. They were the lines above and below the one that was, other people's children, copied out whole and without omission exactly as clause two had promised, because it had plainly not occurred to the Warden of Vendicare that anybody might want only the middle of a page.

The columns were headed **TAKEN. FROM. BY. DATE. RETURNED. NOTE.** On the eleventh line the first four were filled in: one child, aged about three; the house of Saint-Exavier; a family name he knew and had expected and did not bother to feel anything about; a date eight years gone.

The fifth column, the one headed **RETURNED**, was empty: no line drawn through it, no mark of any kind, only the clean blank of a box that somebody, every time they opened the page, had decided to leave empty for eight years. Down the margin beside it ran eight small dates, one for each year, where the line had been checked and left exactly as it was.

*It is not a gap,* he thought. *It is a decision. They have not stopped counting.*

The sixth column, headed **NOTE**, was a list of enquiries: who had asked about this line, and what they had offered, and what had been done about it. There were four, set out in order of what had been offered, the smallest at the top and the largest at the foot, because a creditor does not care when you offered, only what. Three were families, with sums written out in full, and the third of them was the house of Saint-Exavier itself, offering enough to buy a street. The fourth, at the foot, below even that, had no name.

*UNNAMED. THROUGH THE EASTERN CHANNEL. JAPAN, PREFECTURE OF MIE.*

*OFFERED: THE WHOLE OF HER BOOK.*

*NOT ACCEPTED. NO PERFORMANCE POSSIBLE.*

February, eight years ago. Six weeks, he worked out, after a woman in a grey coat had come over the boundary into Namimori with a baby and a bag and nothing else, and been let in.

He sat for a long time with the sheet on his knee.

*Six weeks…* he thought. *She got a roof over her head, and the first thing she did with it was offer every name she ever sold, and every name she ever hid, for news of a child she had never met.*

*And they said no. And she never told anyone. And she has been living three streets down from me for eight years, doing the shopping.*

The fox put its chin on his knee.

*I'm not allowed to go and get him,* Tsuna thought. *I signed it. It's a very good clause.*

*But it doesn't say anything about her…*

He folded the sheet along its old creases and put it inside the book on the shelf, next to the blue cup. Then he lay down on the boards with his hands behind his head and looked at the ceiling, and began, with enormous care and a great deal of pleasure, to work out how to tell Irie Tomoko that he knew.
