# Local House Brings Its Children To Be Weighed. Local Boy Asks What The Paper Does With A No.

## 3.1

He went on the Friday, because Shouichi had an examination on the Thursday and because a thing like that does not get delivered on a Tuesday merely because the person holding it has finished thinking.

The Irie house is the fourth along the lower road, with a hedge that Masatsugu keeps at exactly the height the town by-law permits and not one centimetre lower. Tsuna had been through that gate perhaps forty times in his life. He had never once come to it carrying something.

Tomoko opened the door with flour on one wrist and a pencil behind her ear, and she looked at his face first and at his hands second, and the whole of her went still in the doorway.

"Come in," she said, and stood back from the door, and he did not come in.

He had worked that part out on the walk down and he had worked it out twice, because it mattered. A thing given inside a house is a visit. A thing given at a door is a delivery, and a delivery ends when the object changes hands, and this one was not his to turn into anything larger than that.

He took the sheet out of the oilcloth and held it out to her.

"This is yours," he said. "It was always yours. I have not done anything to it and I am not going to say anything about it."

*And that is the whole of what I am allowed,* he thought, with his arm out and his chest doing something he had no intention of examining on a doorstep. *Not one word past that. Anything I add is arranging, and I have signed a thing that says I will not arrange…*

*…and I would like to add about nine words, and I am going to add none of them.*

Tomoko Irie took the sheet and unfolded it and read it standing up, in the cold, with the door open behind her and the flour going hard on her wrist.

It took her a long time. Not because the page is long, because it is not; it is six columns and eleven lines and most of it is other people's children. She read the ten she had not come for, twice, in the manner of a woman who has been a professional reader of other people's paperwork for twenty years and does not know how to stop being one. Then she got to the eleventh.

Her **Flame** did something while she read. Not much, and not on purpose. A **Ryokugyoku** under pressure banks rather than flares, which is the whole reason Lightning is the one you put in front of a thing, and hers went down and in and stayed there like a hand closing.

*She is holding it,* he thought. *Eight years of being a Lightning in a town that has never once needed her to stand in front of anything, and she is holding it on a doorstep in front of a child.*

He watched her face when she reached the fifth column and found it empty.

He watched her face again, a good deal later, when she reached the sixth.

That one took four seconds, which he counted without deciding to. In the first second she read the entry. In the second she understood that it was hers. In the third she understood that it had been sitting in a book in Europe for eight years with **NOT ACCEPTED** written beside it in a hand that had not cared either way.

In the fourth she understood that the eight year old on her doorstep had been holding it for nine days.

And she did not ask.

*She knows,* thought Tsuna, and he did not move at all. *She has just worked out exactly how long I have had this, and she is not going to ask, and she is not going to make me say it. Why?*

*…Because asking would put it back on me. If she asks, then I explain, and then this becomes a thing that happened between the two of us, and then she owes me a conversation about it. And she will not have that. She has decided in about one second that whatever this is, it is not going to be a transaction.*

*I did not think of that. I thought about eleven things on the walk down here and I did not think of that one.*

*Why not? What was I actually planning for? A refusal, an argument, a question about the nine days, a demand to know how I got it… every one of those has me talking. Every single version I rehearsed has me talking.*

*Is that what I have become? A person who prepares eleven scripts and none of them is the one where somebody simply takes the thing and closes the door?*

Tomoko folded the sheet along its creases, exactly along them, and put it inside her cardigan.

Then she thanked him for the walk down and observed that it was cold, and told him that Shouichi was at the shop and would be sorry to have missed him, and every one of those sentences was a door being closed gently and on purpose. He said to tell Shouichi good luck for Thursday. She told him Thursday had been yesterday.

"Then tell him I said it late," said Tsuna, "and that I meant it on the right day."

She almost smiled and then did not, and the door shut, and that was the whole of the transaction. He stood on the lower road for some seconds before he went back up the hill, and he could not afterwards account for those seconds at all.

*Right,* he thought, climbing. *That is done. That is done and it is out of my hands and it is genuinely out of my hands, which is the part I have not had in a very long time.*

*She did not thank me. Good. If she had thanked me I would have had to work out what I was being thanked for, and the honest answer is: for giving back a thing that was already hers, nine days late.*

## 3.2

She was gone within forty-eight hours, and he did not find out from her.

He found out on the Sunday, at the shrine, from Shouichi. The boy was sitting on the step with a radio in pieces on a cloth and a screwdriver in his teeth. He said it the way an eight year old says a thing nobody has explained to him, which is to say as though it could not possibly be important.

"Mum's away," he said. "Dad's doing the cooking. It's bad."

Asked where, he said work, and did not know, and took the screwdriver out of his mouth to look at a resistor with enormous suspicion. She had said maybe two weeks. She had taken the small case and the grey coat, and the grey coat means abroad, because the grey coat is the abroad coat.

*Two weeks. The grey coat. And he knows what the grey coat means, at eight, and he does not know that he knows it.*

Asked whether that was unusual, Shouichi shrugged with one shoulder and said not really, not now; it used to be, when he was little. His father said it was just work again. He fitted the resistor into the board and frowned at it and added, in the tone of a boy reporting a genuinely puzzling phenomenon, that his father had been in a good mood about it and had hummed.

Tsuna sat down on the step beside him and did not say anything for a while, because there was nothing he could say that would not be nine words too many.

*Masatsugu is in a good mood.*

*She told her husband. She did not tell me, she did not tell my grandmother, and she did not tell the shrine. She told the one person in this town who has been waiting eight years to watch her do something about it, and he hummed.*

*That is the correct order of operations and I have no place in it anywhere. Good. That is what returning a thing looks like when it works.*

"It's *bad*, Tsuna," said Shouichi, with feeling. "He made rice with the lid off! Who does that!"

He handed Shouichi the small screwdriver before Shouichi asked for it, which Shouichi accepted without comment as the natural order of the world.

And then he stayed.

That is the whole of scene two and it is the hardest work he did that winter. He stayed. He did the storeroom count on the Monday and the Taki liaison meeting on the second Tuesday. He signed nine things and he went to bed at the proper hour. And somewhere between four hundred and twelve hundred kilometres away, a woman in a grey coat was doing the thing he was forbidden to do, using a page he was forbidden to have given her for that purpose. He had not asked. He would not ask. He did not know.

The **Sanctuary** ran. That is the thing about a town with a contract: it does not care what its **Kotei** is carrying, and the storeroom count is due whether or not a woman in a grey coat is four hundred kilometres away doing something nobody has been told about. He found the routine steadying and then found that he resented finding it steadying, and then noted both and went to the next item on the list.

*I could find out,* he thought, on one of those evenings, lying on the boards with the fox against his ribs and the lamp low. *Three people in this town could tell me where she has gone inside a day, and one of them would not even ask why.*

*Finding out is not arranging.*

He turned that over.

*…No. Finding out is not arranging, and finding out would be the first step of arranging, and I know the difference between those two sentences because I spent eleven years in a job where everybody said the first one while doing the second.*

*So: no.*

*And what is the honest reason I want to know? Is it that I am worried about her? Or is it that not knowing is the part I cannot stand, and I have spent forty-eight years arranging my life so that I never have to do it?*

*…The second one. Obviously the second one. A **Sky** who cannot bear an open question is not a careful man, he is a man with a habit, and I have had this habit longer than I have had this body.*

**And it stayed no.** He has never, in all the years since, told anybody how much work that was, and the only person who ever guessed was a man who had spent his own professional life learning exactly how long a person can stand not to know.

## 3.3

The letter came on the ninth of February and Ayame brought it up the hill herself instead of putting it in the box, which is how he knew before he opened it that something about it was wrong.

It was wrong in the way expensive things are wrong when they arrive somewhere cheap. The paper was hand made and the hand was not merely good. It was **trained**, in a way that takes two generations to put into a family. And it had been slowed down on purpose in the third and fourth lines, so that a reader would notice the slowing.

"That is a show off," said Ayame, reading over his shoulder without apology, and Tsuna agreed that it was. She asked what they wanted, and he read the fourth paragraph twice before he answered, because the fourth paragraph is where the letter stops performing and starts asking.

"They want," he said slowly, "to look at a piece of paper."

The letter came from the house of **Kōtokui**, of the Kamo line, whose seat is north of Kyoto and whose business, for something over four hundred years, has been the calendar. They do not tell fortunes, the letter said, in a sentence that was doing a great deal of quiet work. They determine what a day is. What may be begun on it. What must not.

And they had heard, through a route the letter did not describe, that a town in Mie had produced a **written instrument for the determination of Flame status**.

They wished to examine it.

*Three months,* thought Tsuna, and put the letter down on the boards very carefully, as though it were going to go off.

*Genichi said eleven years. He said eleven years and Taipei, standing on the steps at one in the morning with a bag turning over in his hand, and he was frightened of it and he said so. Three months.*

*And it has not gone to Taipei. It has gone four hours up the road to a house that has been doing this since before the Sawada came to this valley, and they are not writing to ask whether it works.*

*They are writing to ask to see it. Which means they have already decided that it does.*

He read it twice more and then sat back on his heels and did the piece of arithmetic he had been putting off since the second paragraph.

*They used the words. All seven of them, in the third paragraph, in the right order, spelled the way we spell them.*

*Which I knew. I have known since I was one year old and it has never once mattered before, because the only people I have ever used them in front of were people who learned them here.*

**Ketsueki. Yasuragi. Chiyu. Hogo. Getsufuji. Gen'ei. Kotei.** Seven words for seven **Flames**, and they are not Namimori's. They are four hundred years old and they are the same seven in Kyoto and in Sendai and in every house in this country large enough to have a formal register. What Namimori owns is not the vocabulary. What Namimori owns is the **customs**: which door a **Ketsueki** carries a body out of, what you put in a **Chiyu**'s hands at a funeral, why nobody has ever asked a **Getsufuji** to sit in the middle of a room.

*And the tier above the Flame name is national too. Kohaku for a Sky, Ryokugyoku for a Lightning, Ruri for a Mist…*

*…and one ritual name for each, per sex, for the whole country. One. Tenmei and Amatsumi. Akizuki and Kaguya. **Raiga** and Mirai.*

*I have never thought about that for one second in my life. Why would I? There has never been more than one of anything in this house.*

Musashi read the letter twice and put her glasses on the table.

"Kōtokui," she said. "That house is finished." And when he said *grandmother* in the voice he uses for that, she did not take it back.

"I am seventy-five and I am allowed to say it in my own back room." She turned the letter over and looked at the back, which was blank, and seemed to find that informative. "The Kamo had the calendar and the Abe had the stars, and the Abe won, and the Abe are Tsuchimikado now and they are perfectly comfortable. The Kamo have had four hundred years of being the other one. When did a comfortable house ever write to a fishing town?"

He said that they were not asking for anything, and she gave him the look she gives a **Kotei** who has said something beneath him.

"They are asking to look at a thing, which is what people ask for when what they want is too large to ask for." She folded her hands on the ledger. "Masaya. Do you want to say no?"

He thought about it properly, because she had asked properly.

*If I say no, they go away and they copy it anyway, because a thing that is being talked about four hours up the road is already loose. If I say yes, they come here, and I have to sit in a room with people who read documents for a living while somebody reads out a document about me.*

*And the second one is worse. And the second one is the one where I can see what they do with it.*

"No," he said. "Let them come."

*What does a dying house want from a fishing town?* he thought, that night, with the letter on the boards and the lamp down. *Not protection; we have not got any to spare and they know it. Not the contract; a **Sanctuary** is not a thing you join by asking nicely. Not money.*

*What is left? What does an institution want, when it has four hundred years of authority and has just noticed that the authority does not work any more?*

*…It wants somebody else's authority. That is all that is ever left.*

They came on the twenty-second, and they did not come the way the Vindice had come and they did not come the way Ferri had come, and the difference was the whole of it: **they brought luggage**.

Three of them. An old woman, two attendants, and a cart from the station with four cases on it, and the cases were full of paper.

They had come to stay four days. They had booked in advance, in writing, at the inn by the harbour, and they had paid in advance. The innkeeper was so unnerved by this that she went up to the shrine about it on the Thursday.

The old woman was **Kōtokui Tomoe**. She was seventy-nine, she was very small, and she came up the shrine steps without stopping, which at seventy-nine on those steps is a statement and was meant as one.

And at the bottom of the steps, the Namimori Disciplinary Committee took her name, her house, her purpose, and her answer about the bicycle.

She gave all four without the slightest sign of finding it strange. Afterwards Tsuna worked out why, and the answer was simple and slightly humbling: a house that has kept a daily register since the sixteenth century **recognises a register when it sees one**, and does not care how old the clerk is.

"Your boy asked me what I had come for," she said, at the top, "and I told him, and he wrote it down, and then he read it back to me to check it."

Tsuna said that he was supposed to, which is true and which is the least interesting thing about it, and she turned round and looked back down the forty steps she had just come up without stopping.

"I know he is supposed to. Nobody is supposed to. That is what supposed to means. It means the thing that does not happen."

## 3.4

The two children came up on the second day, and they did not come up together, and that is the part Tsuna has never been able to explain to anybody who was not there.

The house brought one.

His name was **Kamo no Yasuyuki**, which is the formal way, and **Kōtokui Yasuyuki**, which is how a person says it when they are not trying to make a point. He was eight years old. He was very clean, very upright, and he had been taught to stand with his hands at his sides in a way that costs a child about two years to learn.

He was the first person Tsuna's own age to look at him **level** since he had been born into this life.

*Oh,* thought Tsuna, and the feeling arrived before he had any name to put on it. *Oh, that is what that is.*

*Everybody in this town looks at me from above or from below. Grandmother from above. Nagi from below. Kyouya from sideways but he is ten and he is Kyouya and that is its own direction entirely. This one is looking straight across, and he is doing it on purpose, and he has decided in about four seconds that we are the same kind of thing.*

*We are not. But I cannot say why, and he is going to keep thinking it, and I am going to let him.*

"You are the **Kohaku** here," said Yasuyuki, and Tsuna agreed that he was, and the boy nodded once as though a column had been filled in.

"I am to be the **Raiga** of my house in the spring." He said it the way a child says a line he has been given, correctly and without inflection, having said it perhaps a hundred times in eight years. "At the equinox. It has been arranged since the seventh day."

Asked *the seventh day of what*, he looked up with polite surprise, because in his house that is not a strange sentence, and said: of my life.

And the other one walked up the hill on the third day, by himself, and nobody had invited him.

The Committee stopped him at the bottom of the steps, as the Committee stops everybody, and the boy on duty that day was the small one with the clipboard who had asked a Vindice about bicycles in January and who had been living off it ever since.

He gave his name when it was asked for, and the pen moved, and then it stopped, because the next field is house and he had not got one.

The small boy with the clipboard looked at the column, and then at the visitor, and then at the column again. He was nine years old. He had four months of experience and a system, and his system had two fields, and in four months no field had ever come back empty for a reason that was not rudeness. He explained, not unkindly, that a visitor has to have a house, because it is for the file.

"I know," said the boy at the bottom of the steps. "I have thought about it on the way. You could put the mountain."

So the visitor log of the Namimori Disciplinary Committee, for the twenty-fourth of February, records three things. Under **NAME**, the word *Noriyuki*. Under **HOUSE**, the words *the mountain, above Ōtsu, he says there is no name for it*. Under **BICYCLE**, the word *no*.

Ayame had it copied twice. She has never said why, about either of the two times she has done that, and Tsuna has never asked.

Over those four days the visiting house was unsettled twice, and neither time by anything anybody said.

The first was on the second evening, when Kazuki came through the yard with the winter cuttings and the whole of the party stood up. He is a **Chiyu**. In Namimori a **Chiyu** who is carrying something living gets stood up for, and nobody in this town could tell you when that started or why, and everybody does it at about eleven years old without being taught. The Kōtokui attendants stood because their principal stood, and their principal stood because sixty years of reading rooms had told her that something was happening, and afterwards she asked Ayame what it had been.

"He was carrying cuttings," said Ayame, as though that answered it, because to her it does.

The second was the door. Namimori takes a body out of a house by the **Ketsueki** door, which is the north one. The north one is kept shut the rest of the year for that reason. Tomoe asked why a perfectly good door was nailed, and got the answer, and went quiet for some while.

*That is the whole of it,* thought Tsuna, watching her not say anything. *She has the same seven words I have. **Ketsueki**, **Yasuragi**, **Chiyu**, **Hogo**, **Getsufuji**, **Gen'ei**, **Kotei**. She learned them in a house that has written them down since the sixteenth century, and she uses them more correctly than most people here do.*

*And she has never in her life stood up for a **Chiyu** with a plant in his hands.*

*The words are the country's. The customs are the town's. I have known that since I was one year old and I have never once watched somebody find it out.*

He was the same height as his brother and he did not stand like him at all.

He had walked from a bus stop eleven kilometres away and he did not mention it. It came out two days later, and only because somebody asked him a different question. He was not dirty and he was not ragged. Everything he had on was old, mended and immaculate, which is what a child looks like when the person raising him has fierce opinions about clean and none at all about new.

And he was, without any question at all, a **Hogo**.

*There it is,* thought Tsuna, from eight metres away, with his hands in his sleeves and his face doing nothing. *That is a Lightning. That is a large one. I can feel it from here and I was not trying, and a Sky does not have to try, which is the single most inconvenient fact about being one.*

*And it is banked. Nobody taught him that. A Lightning banks by itself, the way a Storm spreads by itself, and he has had eight years on a mountain with nobody to bank it in front of.*

*And the other one is not. Or the other one is something I cannot get a reading on at all, at eight metres, in my own yard, which has not happened to me since I was three. And he has been told since the seventh day of his life that he is going to be the **Raiga** of his house at the equinox, and somebody is going to have to tell him…*

*…no. Stop. Nobody is going to have to tell him anything, because they already know, and they have known it for years. That is why they are here.*

*They are not here to find out which one. They are here to be told which one by a piece of paper, so that it is not them who said it.*

He did not enjoy arriving at that. It arrived anyway, in the middle of the shrine yard, in the cold, in about the time it takes to put your hands in your sleeves.

The two boys saw each other across the yard.

They did not run to each other and they did not look away. Yasuyuki's hands came off his sides about four centimetres and went back. Noriyuki lifted his chin a little, which is what a person does at somebody across a room he has been told not to cross.

Nobody had told either of them the other would be there. Tsuna is sure of that. He has been sure of it for years, and he cannot prove it, and it is the single most frightening thing he saw that whole winter.

*They knew anyway,* he thought. *Neither of them looked around the yard first. They looked at each other.*

*How? Is it a Lightning thing? It is not; the Lightning I have lived beside for two years does not do that. Is it a twin thing? Is there a twin thing?*

*Do they know they are doing it? Would either of them say yes if I asked?*

⚠️ He did not ask. He has gone back over that decision a number of times since and he still cannot tell whether it was restraint or cowardice, and he suspects that at eight years old there was not yet a difference.

## 3.5

It happened in the small hall on the fourth day, and there were nine people in it, and one of them was Sugita Gen'ichi.

That was not an accident and it was not a kindness. It was procedure.

The instrument they had come four hours to look at is signed by a licensed practitioner. The practitioner is alive and in the town, and Ayame had said so in the first letter she sent back. She does not leave a fact out of a file in order to make a meeting easier.

So he came. He sat four places down with a cup he did not drink from, which is what he does.

Kōtokui Tomoe read the instrument aloud. All of it. Both pages, including the parts that are only measurements, in a flat professional voice, without once looking up, and it took eleven minutes.

Tsuna sat through it with his hands on his knees.

*Twice now,* he thought. *This is the second time I have sat in a room and listened to somebody read that paper out, and the first time I told myself the reason it was bearable was that it was not about me…*

*…and it is not about me. It has my measurements on it and it is not about me. It is about what a document can be made to do after the person who signed it has gone home.*

*Eleven minutes,* thought Tsuna, listening to his own pulse rate read out in a stranger's voice in a room with nine people in it. *Four occasions across five months. Temperature, pulse, reflex, response to a **Flame** brought near and withdrawn. Every number on those two pages is true. He measured them and he wrote them down and he did not shade one of them.*

*And then there is a line at the bottom that is not a number, and that line is why everybody in this room is sitting here.*

She finished. She put the pages down and squared them, and said the thing she had come four hours and four days to say.

"It is admirable," she said. "Genuinely admirable! I did not expect that and I do not say it to be pleasant." "It is far better than anything we have. Ours is a judgement, and a judgement is a man's opinion with four hundred years standing behind it, and a man's opinion can be argued with by another man. **This is a measurement.**"

Sugita Gen'ichi said, without raising his voice, that it was a medical note.

"It is a medical note that was read out at a hearing and became the standard by which this town weighs a stranger. I have the minute of that hearing. Your clerk sent it with the instrument and I did not ask her for it." Tomoe folded her hands. "So it is not a medical note any more, Sugita-sensei. That is not an insult and I do not mean it as one. It is simply what has happened to it, and things do happen to documents, and a house that has kept a register for four hundred years knows rather more about that than most."

And then she said what she wanted, and she said it in one sentence, and she did not dress it.

"I would like it applied to my grandsons."

The room went the particular kind of quiet that a room goes when everybody in it has understood something at slightly different speeds.

*There we are,* thought Tsuna.

*Both of them. She said both. She did not say "to my grandson" and she did not name one, and there is a version of this where that is fairness…*

*…and this is not that version, because the paper does not rank. It answers yes or no. She knows that. She has read it twice and she read it aloud and she is seventy-nine and she has been reading documents professionally since before my grandmother was born.*

*So she is not asking for a comparison. She is asking for a no.*

*She wants a piece of paper with a no on it, about one of two children, signed by somebody who is not her.*

He looked down the table at Yasuyuki, who was sitting upright with his hands at his sides, and who had understood this at some point in the last four seconds, and who was not moving at all.

He looked at the other end of the room. Noriyuki was sitting on the step by the door, because nobody had told him he was allowed a place at the table. He had understood it considerably earlier, and he appeared to find it about as interesting as the weather.

⁂

Now here is the thing about being forty years old inside an eight year old, which nobody in that room knew and which is not the advantage it sounds like.

It gives you approximately eleven ways to stop this in the next four seconds and it gives you all of them at once.

He could refuse. He could refuse hard, and be a Sky refusing, and the room would fold. Kōtokui Tomoe would go back up the road with four cases of paper and her grandsons and nothing at all changed. And in three years she would do this somewhere else, with somebody who said yes.

He could ask Musashi to refuse, and she would, and it would cost her.

He could point out the flaw. **He could say, in one sentence, that the instrument returns false negatives, and that he is standing in the room and he knows.**

That sentence was available to him the entire time. It has been available since October. It is available now, and it will be available every year for the rest of his life. The cost of it is three years of being nobody, and everything those three years have been holding up.

He did none of those.

*Which of those do I actually want?* he thought, in the four seconds available. *Which one leaves this room in a state I can live with in eleven years, when somebody opens the file?*

*Not the refusal. A **Kotei** refusing is a wall, and walls get gone around. Not grandmother; that costs her and it costs her in front of a house that will talk. And not the true sentence, because the true sentence costs three years and everything the three years are holding up, and I am not spending those on a Tuesday in February to win an argument with an old woman who is right about most of it.*

*So what is left? What is left when you may not refuse, may not delegate, and may not tell the truth?*

He asked a question, because he was eight years old, and eight year olds ask questions, and because the only move left was one that made the paper do the work instead of him.

"What does it do," said Tsuna, "when the answer is no?"

Tomoe turned her head and asked him to repeat that, which she had not had to do once in four days. He kept his hands flat on his knees and said it again, in the same order: the instrument gives a yes or it gives a no, and what he wanted to know was what happens to the one who gets the no.

She said that it was a determination of fact, in the tone of a woman handing over a receipt.

"Yes," said Tsuna. "But it is going into a file, and somebody reads the file afterwards. So what happens to him?"

There was a pause of about two seconds, and in those two seconds Tsuna watched a woman who had spent sixty years being the cleverest person in every room she entered discover that she had prepared for four days and had not prepared for this.

"He is not the **Raiga**," she said.

Tsuna agreed that he was not, and asked the same question a third time, in the same words, which is a thing he learned to do at about twenty-six and has never found a better use for. She said, after a pause, that the boy was provided for. He asked whether the boy was in the house.

"He is provided for!" said Kōtokui Tomoe, and that was the first time in four days that she repeated herself, and the first time in four days that her voice had done anything at all.

Tsuna had run that exact conversation from the other side, in three countries. He had watched grown men say *provided for* in four languages while meaning nine different things by it. He let the two seconds sit there, and then he asked the last part of it in the flattest voice he owned.

"Then you already know what it is going to say," he said. "Don't you?"

Nobody answered that.

"You have known for years," said Tsuna. "You have a **Hogo** in your house and a boy who is going to be called **Raiga** at the equinox, and they are not the same boy, and you have known that since the pair of them were about four. You did not come here to find out. You came here to be told, by a document, in writing, so that when it is read out in eleven years it will not say that the head of the house decided."

He stopped. He was eight, and his voice had gone somewhere he had not authorised, and he took it back.

"I am not calling it cowardice," he said. "It is not. It is the opposite. It is a person who has decided to take the blame off a child and put it on a piece of paper, and I understand exactly why, and I have done it myself, and I would probably do it again."

*And I did it at six,* he thought, and did not say. *And the paper I did it with is on the table in front of you.*

⁂

Sugita Gen'ichi put his cup down.

He had not spoken in nineteen minutes. He is sixty-one years old and he has a practice of forty years. In January he stopped writing conclusions. He had not told anybody why, except one person, on a set of steps, at one in the morning.

"I should say something about my own instrument," he said, and Ayame said *sensei* in the voice she uses when a thing is about to become minutes, and he went on anyway.

"The first two pages are what I measured. Those are correct, and I will stand behind every line of them in front of anybody you like." He put his hands on the table. "The last line is what I concluded. And a conclusion is not a measurement. It is a man's opinion with a licence standing behind it, which is the thing you said your own method was, and you said it as though mine were different."

Tomoe began his name and did not finish it.

"It is not different. It is the same thing in better handwriting." He looked at the pages, and then at her, and he did not look at Tsuna at all, which Tsuna noticed and was grateful for and has never mentioned. "I stopped writing conclusions in January. I write what I measured and then I stop. If you want the last line of that document applied to a child, you will have to find a man willing to write it. It will not be me. I am the man who wrote it the first time, and I have spent four months finding out what that costs."

She said that he would be asked only to examine them, which was true, and which both of them understood to be true in the way that the first half of a sentence is true.

"Then examine them," said Genichi. "I will do that this afternoon and I will do it properly and I will give you two pages of measurements for each boy, free, because they have come a long way and one of them walked eleven kilometres."

He picked his cup up again.

"And on the third page I will write nothing. And you will take those six pages home, and you will have exactly what you had when you arrived, which is two grandsons and a decision, and the decision will still be yours."

## 3.6

She did not shout and she did not leave. She sat with it for a while, and then she asked one more question, and it was a better question than any she had asked in four days.

"Then what am I to take back?"

And that was the moment Tsuna did the only thing he did in that entire room that he is still satisfied with, and it was not a ruling, and it was not clever.

He looked at Ayame.

"Write it down," he said, and Ayame asked him what, exactly, she was writing down.

"That they came. The date, the house, what they asked for, what was given, and what was not." He was working it out as he said it, which he does not usually allow himself in front of people, and which four separate people in that room noticed. "And then write that **no determination was made**. In those words, not a summary of them. Sign it, give them a copy, and keep a copy here."

Ayame already had the pen out and had already begun. She asked, without looking up, what the file should say if somebody came in eleven years wanting to know what Namimori had decided about the Kōtokui boys.

"Then the file says nothing was decided," said Tsuna, "and it says that on purpose, and it says who was in the room when nothing was decided, and it gives the date."

He turned back to Tomoe.

"That is not me helping you," he said. "I want that clear. You came for a no in writing and you are going home with a sheet that says the question was asked and left open. That is worse for you. It is worse for you in every practical way, and in the spring you will still have to stand up at the equinox and give that name to one of them yourselves, with your own mouth, in front of your own house."

Kōtokui Tomoe said yes. He said that he knew. She observed, without any particular heat, that he was eight years old.

"Yes," said Tsuna, "and you brought two children four hours on a train to be weighed by a piece of paper. I do not think either of us is going to come out of this week looking especially good."

⁂

They went on the Friday, with six pages of measurements and a third page that was blank on both boys, and a signed note from the shrine that said in nine lines that nothing had been determined.

Kōtokui Tomoe went down the forty steps rather more slowly than she had come up them.

Yasuyuki went down them behind her with his hands at his sides. He stopped on the ninth from the bottom, turned round, and came back up nine steps to say the only unscripted thing anybody heard from him all week.

"I will still be given it," he said. "In the spring. Nothing you did stops that."

Tsuna said that he knew, and the boy stood on the step with his hands at his sides and asked, in the flat voice of somebody who has been holding a question for four days, what the point had been.

*Good question,* thought Tsuna, and found that he had an answer and that the answer was not a comfortable one to hand to an eight year old.

"The point is that it will be your grandmother who gives it to you," he said, "and not a piece of paper from Mie. You can argue with her."

Yasuyuki thought about that for four seconds, which is a long time on a step in February.

"I have never argued with her in my life," he said.

"No," said Tsuna. "You have got until the equinox."


Noriyuki left the same morning, separately, on foot, in the direction of the bus. He had spoken to Tsuna four times in three days and none of the four conversations had been about anything. He stopped at the bottom of the steps to be signed out by the Committee, because that is procedure, and he answered the bicycle question. Then he said the only interesting thing he said all week. He said it to a nine year old with a clipboard, rather than to anybody who mattered.

"Your file," he said. "The one with the houses in it. Does it get kept?"

"Forever!" said the boy with the clipboard, who had been waiting four months for somebody to ask, and who said it with such force that a gull went up off the wall.

"Then that is the first time I have been written down," said Noriyuki, and went to catch his bus.

⁂

That evening Tsuna sat on the floor of the room with the door that does not close and laid out three things, which is a habit now and which he is aware is a habit.

The Kasumi shrine form, with the empty box.

The copy of the note Ayame had signed, with **no determination was made** in the fourth line.

And the sheet headed **SINCE JANUARY**, which by then ran to forty-six entries and still had a line ruled through the fourth column.

*Right,* he thought. *Line them up. What did I actually do this week?*

*One. I gave a woman back a piece of paper that was hers, and she went, and I do not know where she is, and finding out is the first step of arranging, so I do not know where she is.*

*Two. I did not fix the thing that is wrong with that instrument, because fixing it costs three years and it is not the week to spend them. It is still wrong. It is going to be wrong in Taipei in eleven years exactly as Genichi said, and the only thing I changed is that it now travels with a note attached saying a town once declined to use it.*

*Three…*

He looked at the middle sheet for some time.

*Three is that I have spent a year learning what an open column is worth.*

*In October a blank box in a form from Ise stopped me dead, and I read it as a wall. In January a blank column in a ledger in Europe stopped me again and I read it right the second time, and the difference between those two readings is the only thing I have actually learned this whole winter.*

*Vendicare has a column headed RETURNED and it is empty and they have kept it empty for eight years, and what that means is that they have not stopped counting. It is not a gap. It is a decision, taken every time somebody opens the page and writes a date in the margin and closes it again.*

*And today I made one.*

*Somebody is going to open that note in eleven years and find nothing in it, and the nothing is going to be doing exactly the same work. It is going to say that on the twenty-fifth of February a room full of people who could have closed this did not close it. It left the thing open, where the two of them can still get at it.*

He put the three sheets away under the loose board.

*And that is all I can do. I cannot go and get the child in Europe. I cannot tell a woman in a grey coat where to look. I cannot say the one sentence that would take that instrument apart, and I will not be able to say it next year either.*

*I can leave things open. That is the entire list.*

*Is that enough? Is it anything at all, or is it the thing a person says when he has been stopped and wants to feel that the stopping was a choice?*

*…Both. It is both, and I am not going to be able to tell which on any given day, and I am going to have to do it anyway.*

*It is a very short list and I am going to be extremely good at it.*

⁂

He did not think about the foxes at all.

The two stone ones at the sessha gate were where they have been since before the Sawada came to this valley, one on each side of the path, identical to anybody who has not spent a lifetime looking at them. They had turned around once, eighteen months ago, in the dark, while he was busy being clever at somebody, and he had never worked out how and had never asked.

He had given a name to a different fox, a small one out of a length of bamboo, in a December that felt further away than it was.

One name. He had never once counted the other two.

Thirty-one days later a letter came from a woman in a grey coat. It was four lines long. The first line said **I have found where he was**. The second line said **not where he is**, and the rest of it is the next chapter.

Và Tsuna **không gọi đó là hèn** , cậu nói cậu hiểu, cậu đã làm thế, và cậu sẽ làm lại.

**2. Genichi là người đóng cảnh, không phải Tsuna.** Ông tự nói ra rằng **kết luận không phải phép đo**, và dùng đúng câu bà vừa dùng để chê phương pháp nhà mình.
