# Local Town Holds A Hearing. Local Boy Hears His Own Certificate Read Out As Precedent.

## 1.1

He kept a sheet for it, because of course he did, and the sheet is the shortest honest account of those eight months that anybody wrote.

It lived in the second drawer under the seal case. It was headed, in his own small hand, **SINCE JANUARY**, and it had four columns. The fourth column was the one he added in March, when he understood what the sheet was actually for.

The columns were *what happened* · *who did it* · *did I know beforehand* · and **the fourth one**.

The fourth one said: *did it need me.*

There were thirty-one entries by the end of September. He wrote *no* in the fourth column twenty-six times. He had stopped feeling much about that around entry nine, which is itself the sort of thing a person ought to notice about himself and file somewhere.

Here is what the sheet said, in the order he wrote it.

**February.** A man did not come back to a town in Liguria, and was not reported missing by anybody, because a man who has gone over a wall in Mie in January is difficult to file. Nobody in the Sawada house said a word about it. Hibari Seiran ate an enormous breakfast on the Thursday she came home and slept for fourteen hours, and three weeks later the entry in the fourth column was still *no*.

**February, later.** Eleven boys at the water tower crossing became thirty-one boys with a rota. By April they had a room. By June the room had a door that locked and a box of armbands that no adult had approved and no adult was going to raise.

Tsuna had watched that happen from about four metres away for five months, and had contributed precisely nothing, and had been asked for precisely nothing.

*Did it need me.* **No.**

**March.** In Palermo, a man who was one of the Ninth's sons stopped sleeping. By August he had withdrawn from a conversation nobody outside four rooms knew was happening. An unremarkable man in a knitted hat came to the shrine every second Sunday that spring and complained about the price of ink, and about a neighbour's dog, and about the way the summer was going to be, and never once about anything else at all.

**April.** Ayame's stitches came out. The scar went pink, then white, then settled into a long pale line on the outside of her left forearm. She has never once worn a sleeve that covers it since. She will be showing it to people when she is ninety.

**May.** Nagi put a **Gen'ei** down on purpose for the first time, in the kitchen, by accident, while reaching for something. She did it again eleven minutes later on request and then could not do it again for two months. Grandmother wrote the date on the doorframe with the ones for height.

**June.** Sugita Gen'ichi stopped being a man with a notebook and became a man with a notebook that the house filled in. He came up twice a month now. He asked better questions than he used to, and the better questions were worse to answer, and Tsuna had begun to look forward to Thursdays in a way he had decided not to examine.

**June, later.** A letter came from a house in the mountains north of Kyoto. It was four lines, it was addressed to *the Kohaku*, and it contained no request of any kind. The head of that house was eighty-nine in January. She had asked one question over her breakfast and then gone back to it.

She had apparently spent five months deciding whether to write. What she wrote in the end was a comment about the weather on this coast, and a remark that she had known a **Kotei** once and did not expect to again.

Musashi read it twice, put it in the box under the north hall with the instrument, and said nothing about it for a week.

**July.** Shouichi Irie was eight years old and had built something out of a radio that his mother had confiscated twice and then unconfiscated, which in that house counts as a review.

**August.** The **Getsufuji** on the hill acquired a second room, a filing arrangement, and a policy about bicycles. Tsuna heard about the policy about bicycles from four separate adults in one week, and every one of them reported it in the tone of a person describing weather they had decided to be philosophical about.

"He is **ten**," said Tomoko Irie, at the gate, with a box of something under her arm. "Ten! And he has a **policy**. About bicycles."

"Is it a good policy?"

"It is an *excellent* policy," she said bitterly, "and that is the part I cannot forgive."

**September.** Nagi drew a **Gen'ei** across the whole of the accounts room ceiling at four in the afternoon, deliberately, for an audience of one. Then she looked at Tsuna with the flat expectation of a child who has done a trick and requires it to be rated.

"Eight," he said.

"*Eight?*"

"Eight and a half. The corner by the light came apart." And then, because she had gone very still in the way she goes still: "Nagi. Nobody in this house could have done that at five. Nobody in this house could have done that at **nine**."

"Grandmother could."

"Grandmother is seventy-four," said Tsuna, "and has been at it since before the war, and you are going to be better than she is, and I am extremely glad I am going to be somewhere else when you find that out."


**October.** Tsuna turned eight in a house that made a fuss about it, and he let them, and he was surprised by how little it cost him to let them.

He looked at the sheet in the first week of October with the lamp on, and did the thing he had been doing since a parapet above Kawaba in January, which was the honest version of the arithmetic instead of the comfortable one.

*Twenty-six out of thirty-one,* he thought. *And the five where the answer was yes are all of them small. A signature. A word to a man at the ferry office. Somebody had to know which form.*

*So what is the sheet measuring?*

He sat with that.

*It is not measuring whether they need me. I knew the answer to that in January, on a cold step, with a cup of tea I had not asked for… and it did not kill me.*

*It is measuring what I am allowed to do. And the number is going down.*

## 1.2

The letter went out on the ninth of October and was four sentences long, and he rewrote it eleven times to get it to four.

It went to the Kasumi shrine at Ise. It is not a large place and it is not an important one. It keeps, for reasons nobody outside the trade could explain, the most complete register of **Honō** births in the four southern prefectures. They have kept it since 1801. They keep it in a room with a cedar floor, and they do not lend it, and they do not photograph it, and twice a year somebody drives down from Tokyo and is told no.

Tsuna did not ask to see the register. He knew better than that at six, never mind eight.

He asked whether a child with a **ranking** ability had been recorded anywhere in their pages between seven and twelve years ago. He did not give a name. He did not say why.

And he signed it *Sawada, of Namimori*, which is the smallest legitimate thing he could put… and which had taken two of the eleven drafts to settle.

The reply came back in nine days, which is fast.

It was a form.

It was not rude. That was the part he turned over afterwards, in the room with the door that did not close, for rather longer than the thing deserved. Nobody at the Kasumi shrine had been unkind or obstructive or even slow. They had done exactly the correct thing, which was to send the enquirer the standard instrument, because an archive that has survived two centuries has survived it by having one.

**ĐƠN XIN TRA CỨU SỔ.** Four sections. Purpose of enquiry. Relationship of enquirer to subject. Undertaking as to use.

And section two, which had eight lines, and which was headed:

**Tư cách của người xin tra cứu.**

Line one wanted the house. He could fill that in.

Line two wanted the standing of the house. He could fill that in too, and it would be a good answer, and the good answer was four hundred years old.

Line three wanted the **Honō** of the enquirer, and the rank at which it had been recognised, and the name of the person who had recognised it, and the date.

Tsuna looked at line three for some time.

*I am the Kotei of a Sanctuary,* he thought. *I hold a contract that predates this shrine by two hundred years. Every signatory house between here and the Chinese coast came off their chairs for me in January and nine of them are still furious about it.*

*And on paper I am a healthy eight year old with nothing at all.*

He put the pen down and picked it up and put it down again.

Because line three could not be argued with. There was no clerk to be difficult to. No rule to find the exception in. No person anywhere in the chain who had decided one single thing against him. The Kasumi shrine did not know he existed and had no opinion about him whatsoever.

*Nobody has refused me,* he thought. *That is the part I keep trying to get round and cannot. There is no refusal. There is a box, and the box is empty, and an empty box is not an argument. You cannot appeal against an empty box.*

*And I put it there.*

He could have filled it in. That was the other thing, and he made himself look at it properly, because refusing to look was how a man ended up making the comfortable decision while believing he had made the hard one.

He could have written **Kotei**, and the rank, and *Sawada Musashi, Ruriko of Namimori*, and a date. Every word of that would have been true. And within a month it would be in a register at Ise. Within a year it would be copied, because registers are copied. Within four years a clerk somewhere would set it beside a line in an Italian ledger, about a **Sanctuary** in Mie that invoked its bond in January…

…and then a great many people who currently believed that Sawada Masaya was nobody would stop believing it.

*Three years,* he thought. *I need three more years of being nobody. Four would be better.*

*And that child has been missing for eleven.*

He folded the form back along its original creases, which took three tries because the paper was stiff and his left hand was still not quite what it had been, and he put it in the second drawer on top of the sheet headed **SINCE JANUARY**.

Then he went and did the accounts, because it was Tuesday.

## 1.3

The application arrived on the twenty-second of October, in Italian, by registered post, addressed to *The Custodian of the Instrument, Namimori*, which is not an address and which got there anyway.

Namimori gets four or five of these a year. Most are settled at the letter stage by Ayame, who has a drawer for it. She has a form of words refined over thirty years into something that manages to be a refusal and a kindness at once. Some of them are people who have read the wrong book. Two a year are serious, and of those two, one is usually a person who has already decided and is looking for somewhere to be told no by.

A full hearing happens perhaps once in six years.

This one went to a hearing before anybody had finished reading it, because of the second paragraph.

The second paragraph said that the applicant had learned of Namimori from a line in a ledger. An incident involving an independent squad, recorded under a Monday in January, in the column the Italian side keeps for such things… and he had gone and looked up what a Sanctuary was afterwards, and had then spent four months deciding whether to write.

Ayame read that twice, put the letter down, and went to find Ruriko-sama without taking her coat off.

*That is how a name travels,* Tsuna thought, when they told him. *I knew it would. I wrote it down in March in the third column and I put a tick in it.*

*And this is the first one. Out of how many? How many people read that line?*

The applicant was called Aldo Ferri. He was fifty-four years old. He had been a bookkeeper.

He had kept books for thirty-one years for a famiglia that is not worth naming, in a town that is, and in the spring of the previous year he had made an arrangement with people who wanted what was in those books. He had done it for money. He said so in his own letter, in the first paragraph, before anybody had asked him anything at all. The shrine office noted that and did not discuss it.

Nine people had died! Two of them were people he had eaten dinner with for two decades. He had not expected nine. He had expected an arrest, and a mess, and a change of management. There is a kind of man who genuinely does not think past the point where his own part ends. Aldo Ferri had been that kind of man for fifty-three of his fifty-four years, and had stopped being it on a Tuesday in April.

He was not asking for forgiveness. His letter was explicit about that, and Musashi said afterwards that it was the only line in it she believed on the first reading.

He was asking for a wall that other people had built, and he said so, and he said he knew what that made him.

He was asking to be somewhere the people looking for him could not lawfully come.

"He is not a victim," said Ayame, in the shrine office, on the first night, with the letter flat on the table between six people and a pot going cold. "I want that said at the start, because it is going to get said later by somebody who thinks it is clever."

"He is not."

"And the clause does not say victim," said Ishii-san, from the end of the table, eighty-two now and still doing the second Tuesday of the month in Taki. "I have read it more times than any of you. It says **refuge without condition**. It has said that for four hundred years. If it had meant the deserving it would have been a much shorter document and we would have had a much easier four centuries."

Somebody laughed, briefly, and then stopped.

The argument went on until one in the morning, and it was not the argument anybody had expected on the way up the hill.

Nobody in that room was trying to keep him out. That is the part that would surprise an outsider most, and it surprised Tsuna a little too, and he spent a while afterwards working out why it had.

*Because I have spent two lifetimes in rooms where the first question is whether a man is worth the trouble,* he thought. *And the first question here was not that. The first question here was who pays.*

Ishii-san made the only speech of the night, and it was three sentences long, and she made it at half past eleven with her coat still on.

"I was nine when we took the Nakamura family," she said. "Nineteen thirty-eight. My mother put four of them in our back room. One of them stole from us for a year, and my mother knew, and she let him. She said a person who steals in a house where nobody locks anything is telling you something, and you had better listen." She pulled her coat straight. "He stopped in the end. He is buried on the hill. **Ours and not ours** is the only question this town has ever been able to answer, and we have never once got it wrong by being too generous!"

Nobody argued with that either.

What they were arguing about was something narrower, and much worse, and it took until one in the morning to say it plainly.

## 1.4

Here is the problem, and it is worth setting out plainly, because the entire chapter of the town's history that follows turns on it.

**Refuge without condition** is not complicated. A person arrives, the town takes them, and the contract covers them from that moment. Tomoko came through that door eight years ago, heavily pregnant, with a husband carrying a toddler and a bag between them and a famiglia behind them, and the whole of the procedure took an afternoon.

What is complicated is the **other** clause, the one Musashi read twice on the steps in January: an attack upon a Sanctuary is an attack upon every party to the agreement, and the answer is not optional and not proportional.

Which means admitting a hunted man is not a kindness the town performs by itself. It is a cheque drawn on seven houses across East Asia. And a **Sanctuary** that draws that cheque carelessly twice will discover, on the third occasion, that the bond has opinions.

So the hearing had to establish one thing before it could establish anything else.

And to establish it, Namimori had to say out loud, in a room, in front of an interpreter, a thing it had never once needed to put into words.

There are **seven**. A **Ketsueki** goes and does. A **Yasuragi** takes the heat out of a room, except here, where it does the opposite. A **Chiyu** mends and a **Hogo** holds. A **Getsufuji** stays where it is put. A **Gen'ei** is the one no instrument finds… and a **Kotei** is the one the other six come home to.

Every household on that hill could have recited it. Not one of them had ever had to write it down, and Ayame wrote it down that night, and it is in the file, and it is the first time in four hundred years that the list appears in the town's own hand.

*That is what a stranger costs,* Tsuna thought, watching her do it. *Not the risk. The **explaining**. Four hundred years of knowing, and one Italian bookkeeper, and now it is a document.*

**Are the people hunting him Flame people?**

Not *dangerous*. Not *organised*. The instrument does not care about either. It cares whether they carry **Honō**, because that is the only thing it was ever built to hold off, and a **Sanctuary** that forgets the difference has forgotten what it is.

Because if they are, the machinery engages one way, and the seven houses are in it whether they like it or not. And if they are not, then this is a matter for a fishing town, a locked door, and a great deal less paperwork.

Ferri said no.

He said it in the letter. He said it again at the hearing, through an interpreter from Kōbe, in the flat exhausted manner of a man who has answered the same question eleven times and has stopped expecting the answer to help.

He was a small grey person in a coat that had been good four years ago. He sat with both hands on the table where everybody could see them, which nobody had asked him to do, and which three separate people noticed and did not remark on. He answered every question put to him and volunteered nothing. Ayame asked him twice whether he understood that admission was **without condition** and could not be taken back. He said yes the first time. The second time he asked her whether that meant it could not be taken back **by him**.

"…By you?"

"If I wished to leave," said Aldo Ferri. "Later. If I found that I could not do this. Is the door only one way?"

Ayame wrote the question down before she answered it, which is her habit when somebody surprises her.

"It is not a prison," she said. "You may go whenever you like."

"Then I will stay," said Ferri, "and I would like that written also, because a man should be able to point at the page where he chose."

The famiglia he had robbed was ordinary. Nasty, competent, well connected in a small way, and without a single **Flame** worth the name in three generations.

And Namimori had no way to check.

That is the thing outsiders never grasp, and the thing the town itself had never had to say out loud before. Namimori does not **test** for **Honō**. Namimori **knows**. You look at a person and you know. It is not a skill and nobody is taught it, and every child in that town could sort a room by **Honō** before they could tie a knot.

But knowing does not produce a document. And a hearing that draws on seven houses produces a document or it produces nothing.

"We need a standard," said Musashi, on the third night. "Not for him. For the file. Something a house in Taipei can read in eleven years and see that we were not careless."

"We have never needed one."

"We have never had a bond pulled in living memory either, and then in January we pulled seven." She put her hands flat. "Find me a determination of Flame status made inside this boundary, in writing, by somebody licensed to make it. If there is one, it is our standard. If there is not, we invent one tonight and every Sanctuary in East Asia inherits it, and I would rather not."

It took Ayame forty minutes.

She came back with three, and put them on the table in date order, and the third one was the most recent by eleven months.

Tsuna, who was sitting against the wall at the back because he was eight and because nobody had told him to leave, watched her set it down and knew what it was from the shape of the paper.

*No,* he thought.

*No, no, no.*

**SUGITA GEN'ICHI**, licensed practitioner, Namimori. November of the previous year. Subject examined on four occasions across five months. Findings recorded in full. Conclusion in the second-to-last line, in a hand that slopes very slightly to the left:

> *Entirely ordinary. No indication of Flame.*

"That is the most recent," said Ayame. "And it is the only one of the three made by somebody still alive."

Sugita Gen'ichi was sitting four places down the table with a cup he had not drunk from.

He did not move at all. He looked at the paper, and then at Musashi, and then back at the paper, and whatever went across his face went across it slowly enough that three people saw it and none of them said a word.

Because he knew. He had known since one o'clock in the morning on a Friday in January, in his own surgery, with eleven stitches finished and the book open.

And what lay on the table in front of six people was the document he had signed **before** he knew. It was accurate about every single thing it measured. It was the most rigorous piece of work of its kind anybody in that town had produced in forty years… and which was, on the one question that mattered, wrong.

"It is a good instrument," said Musashi.

"It is."

"It is thorough, and it is dated, and it is signed by a man with a licence, and the man is in the room and can speak to it." She did not look at Tsuna once. "Are we agreed that it is the standard?"

Ishii-san said yes. Kaneko-san said yes. The man from the lower shrine said yes. Ayame said yes and wrote it down.

Gen'ichi said, "Yes," and put his cup down, and the cup made a small sound against the table that everybody heard.

And at the back of the room, against the wall, with his hands in his lap and his face doing nothing whatsoever, Sawada Masaya listened to his own lie become the law of a four hundred year old contract.

*It is my fault,* he thought, and then made himself correct it, because the comfortable version was standing right there being much easier to hold. *No. It is not my fault, and calling it my fault is a way of making it about me, and it is not about me, it is about a man called Ferri who is going to be admitted or not admitted on a standard that does not work.*

*It is my doing. That is a different word and it is the right one.*

*And how many other things is it going to be the standard for? How many years? Who reads it next, and what do they use it to decide?*

He counted the people in the room. Six, plus the interpreter, plus Ferri in the outer room, plus himself.

*Eight,* he thought. *Eight people know what that paper says. Two of us know what it does not say.*

*And I cannot tell the other six without telling them everything, and I have three years of being nobody left to spend, and I have just watched three of them go across a table in eleven minutes.*

Gen'ichi found him afterwards, on the step outside, at twenty past one in the morning.

He did not sit down. He stood with his bag in one hand and his coat over the other arm, in the manner of a man who has decided to say one thing and go, and who is going to say it standing up.

"I said yes."

"You did."

"I have been turning over whether I should have." He looked out at the dark instead of at the boy. "And I have got as far as this. If I had said no, they would have asked me why. And I would have had to answer, in a room with six people in it, and one of them writing everything down."

*And then it would not have been your secret to keep,* Tsuna thought. *It would have been a finding, in a file, with a date on it.*

"Yes," he said.

"So I said yes. And it was the correct thing, and I am going to be unhappy about it for a while, and I would like you to know that I am aware of the difference between those two facts." Gen'ichi shifted the bag. "Do you understand what I am telling you?"

"That you are not asking me to make you feel better about it."

"…Yes." A pause. "That is exactly it. How old are you?"

"Eight."

"Mm," said Sugita Gen'ichi, in the flat register he uses for a reading that does not agree with the previous reading, and did not pursue it.

He came up twice a month now. He had been coming up twice a month since June. The black book now had a section with seven definitions in **Musashi's** ordering and his own handwriting, and a page of measurements from the previous December that had an explanation beside them at last, and a date.

And on the page after that, started in August, unheaded, there was a list that nobody had asked him to make.

It was short. It was in the same hand and the same ink. It said things like: *does not flinch* · *checks his own pulse before the wound* · *corrects himself aloud, then corrects the correction* · *is polite to the woman who cleans and not polite to me, and the second one is the compliment.*

He had not shown that page to anybody and did not intend to.

"May I ask you something," he said. "Not as your doctor."

"You may ask a great many."

"That is her line."

"It is a good line," said Tsuna.

Gen'ichi almost smiled and then did not, because he had got to the question and the question was harder than it had looked in the surgery.

"That paper," he said. "The one I signed. It has just become the rule that this town measures strangers by, and it is going to be copied, and in eleven years somebody in Taipei is going to apply it to somebody I will never meet." He turned the bag over in his hand. "Does that frighten you?"

Tsuna thought about lying.

He thought about it properly, the way he thinks about everything, in the four seconds available: the comfortable answer was **no**, and the comfortable answer would close the conversation cleanly, and the man in front of him had earned rather better than a clean close.

"Yes," he said.

"Good." Gen'ichi put his coat on. "It frightens me a great deal, and I have been standing on that step for six minutes trying to decide whether I was the only one."

"You are not."

"No." He got the second sleeve after two tries. "Then we are two people who are frightened of the same piece of paper, and neither of us is going to do anything about it tonight, and I find that I would rather be two than one."

He got as far as the bottom of the steps.

"Sawada-kun."

"Yes?"

"The next time somebody asks me to certify something," said Gen'ichi, without turning round, "I am going to write down what I **measured** and I am going to stop. I am not going to write a conclusion." He started walking. "It is a smaller document. It is also a true one. Good night."

*He has just changed how he practises,* Tsuna thought, listening to him go down the hill. *Over one page. At sixty-one years old, in a country he moved to eleven months ago, because of a thing he signed before he knew.*

*And in eleven years the file in Taipei is going to be better for it, and he will never find that out.*

*Who else does that? Who does that quietly, with nobody watching, for nothing?*

He sat on the step for a while after the footsteps stopped.

*Twenty-six out of thirty-one,* he thought. *And I have been writing the wrong number in the fourth column since March.*

*It was never did it need me. Every one of those thirty-one things was somebody doing something without being asked. That is what the column was measuring and I did not see it, because I was reading the sheet for my own name.*

## 1.5

The standard did what a standard does, which is to produce an answer, and the answer was the wrong shape.

By the Thursday there was a determination in the file.

*Aldo Ferri, fifty-four, bookkeeper. Assessed against the Sugita instrument. No **Ketsueki**, no **Yasuragi**, no **Chiyu**, no **Hogo**, no **Getsufuji**, no **Gen'ei**, no **Kotei**. No indication of **Honō** of any kind.*

Ayame recorded it in a hand that does not slope at all, and she wrote the seven out in full, because the list had gone into the file on the third night and a list that exists gets used.

And the moment that line went into the file, the contract stepped back from him. Quietly, completely, and without anybody in the room intending it.

It took the room most of an hour to work out why, and it was Kaneko-san of all people who said it first, with the cat asleep inside her coat and her reading glasses pushed up into her hair.

"Then nobody is coming," she said. "Are they? If they come for him."

Nobody argued with her, because she was right, and the argument in the room reorganised itself around that in about four seconds.

Here is the machinery, and it is worth being exact, because the town had to be.

The seven houses answer an **attack upon a Sanctuary**. And what makes an attack an attack, in the language of an instrument written four hundred years ago by people thinking about other people like themselves, is that it comes from the world the instrument was built to hold off. A Flame world. Houses, bonds, obligations, the whole vast old apparatus that Namimori sits inside like a stone in a shoe.

Ordinary men with ordinary reasons have never been the contract's business.

So if a dozen unremarkable and extremely motivated Italians arrive in Mie in March, the bond does not stir. Not one house head anywhere comes off a chair. There is no clause, no answer, no chains, and the seven copies in seven storerooms across East Asia stay exactly where they are.

There is only Namimori. Four thousand people, one contract that does not apply, a hill, and whoever happens to be standing on it.

"Say the rest of it," said Musashi.

"You know the rest of it."

"I do. Say it anyway, because it goes in the file, and in eleven years somebody is going to read this page and I want them to see that we said it out loud."

So Ayame said it out loud. Admitting Aldo Ferri meant accepting a risk the contract expressly did not insure, and paying for it, if it came, entirely out of a town of four thousand people… and that was not a decision six people in a shrine office were entitled to take.

The vote went out on the Sunday.

Every household with a name in the register gets one voice, which is the oldest procedure Namimori has and is used about as often as the box comes out of the storeroom. It is not a ballot. People come up the hill. Ayame reads the question, and they say the word, and she writes down the name and the word beside it.

It took four hours and eleven minutes. The queue went down past the second torii and round the corner by the paper shop, and it was the coldest Sunday of the autumn, and nobody went home. Kaneko-san brought out a kettle at some point in the second hour and then a second kettle, and by the third hour there were four of them going and a small unauthorised economy in rice crackers.

Ayame sat at the table at the top of the steps with her left sleeve pushed up, as she has sat for every formal thing in that town since January, and read the question to each household in turn.

The question is eleven words long and has not changed since the eighteenth century.

The answers, mostly, were one word. A few were not.

The fish man said yes and then said it again because he did not think he had said it loudly enough. A woman from the north road said yes, then asked whether she might add something, and was told that she might not, and said it anyway. Her grandfather had come here in 1946 with nothing, and had not been asked a single question… and Ayame wrote **yes** and did not write the rest, and everybody understood that she would remember it.

One household said no. They gave a reason, and the reason was a good one, and it was about the four thousand people and the arithmetic. Ayame wrote it down in full and read it back and thanked them, and they went home, and nobody in that town has ever held it against them, and three people said so out loud that week without being asked.

Tomoko Irie stood in the queue for two of those hours with her son beside her. When she got to the table she said **yes** in a voice that carried further than she had meant it to, and then stood there for a second longer than the procedure required.

*She came through that door,* Tsuna thought, from the top of the steps. *Eight years ago, about to give birth, with Akiko on her husband's hip and a famiglia behind them. And there was no vote for her, because nobody was hunting her with a **Flame**, so the contract simply covered it and the town simply did it.*

*Shouichi was born on the road. He has never lived anywhere that was not this.*

*She has been waiting eight years for somebody to ask her to pay that back. And nobody ever did, because this town does not keep that kind of ledger…*

*…which is the entire reason she would die for it.*

The count was not close. Namimori has spent four centuries being a place that takes people. A town which has done a thing for four hundred years does not require much persuading to do it again, even when the arithmetic is bad. Especially when the arithmetic is bad; several of the older ones said something to that effect on the way up, in different words, all of them slightly annoyed at being asked.

Aldo Ferri watched the queue from the window of the room he had been put in, which had a good view of the steps and a door that was not locked.

He had been in Namimori eleven days by then. He had been fed four times a day by three separate households who did not coordinate with each other. He had been asked exactly two questions about his past by anybody. And at some point in the second week he had started crying at inconvenient moments, which nobody mentioned and which two separate people quietly arranged to be elsewhere for.

What he mostly could not get over were the children, and the **Getsufuji** who ran them.

"There are boys," he said to the interpreter, on the Sunday, watching the queue. "With the armbands. At the crossing by the water tower. They have been there since six this morning, and they have moved the queue twice, and the second time was correct." He pressed his fingers against his eyes. "Who are they? Are they police? Is it a school thing? The oldest is a child."

"He is ten."

"Ten," said Aldo Ferri.

"He has been running it since January," the interpreter said, and then had to stop and think about how to put the rest of it, and in the end said: "Nobody asked him to."

Ferri looked out of the window for a while at thirty-one boys in armbands managing four thousand people, competently, in the cold, under the direction of a ten year old who had not spoken to a single adult all morning.

"In my town," he said, "there was a man. He arranged things. If you wanted a permit you went to him, and it cost, and everybody knew, and the mayor knew." He rubbed at the window with his cuff, which did not help, because the condensation was on the inside. "He was forty-five and he had eleven people working for him and it took him twenty years to get that queue to move properly."

"Ah."

"That child has had nine months!" said Aldo Ferri. "Nine months, and nobody pays him, and he has a **policy about bicycles**."

The interpreter, who had been born in Namimori and had never once in his life found any of this remarkable until he had to explain it to somebody, discovered that he did not have anything to put in the gap.

"He is a **Getsufuji**," he said eventually.

"What is that?"

"It is…" The interpreter tried twice. "It is what he is. It is not a job."

Ferri looked at him, and then back out of the window, and did not ask anything else for a while.

"This is a very strange town," he said.

"Yes," agreed the interpreter, who had been born there, and who was going to think about that conversation on and off for eleven years.

## 1.6

Tsuna did not vote, because he was eight, and the register has a rule about that which is one line long and four hundred years old and which nobody has ever needed to look up.

He went up the hill anyway. He stood at the top of the steps for the whole four hours, next to his grandmother, because the **Kotei** of a **Sanctuary** stands where the town can see him on a day like that. He had worked that out by himself at some point during the second hour, and Musashi had noticed him work it out, and had said nothing at all, and had been pleased in a way that took her the rest of the winter to stop being.

That night he sat on the floor of the room with the door that did not close, and laid three things out in front of him on the boards.

The form from the Kasumi shrine, folded along its creases, with the empty box.

The sheet headed **SINCE JANUARY**, which now ran to thirty-four entries.

And a copy of the determination in the Ferri file, which Ayame had made for the archive and which he had asked for and been given without a single question, because he was the Kotei and because Ayame does not ask questions she can answer herself.

*Right,* he thought. *Line them up and look at them. All three, and no flinching, and no version where I come out of it well.*

*One. There is a register at Ise that would tell me whether that child was ever written down, and I cannot open it, because there is an empty box and I am the one who emptied it.*

*Two. The same emptiness, in a different piece of paper, has just told seven houses across East Asia that a man in my grandmother's spare room is not their business. He is going to live or not live on the strength of a thing I arranged when I was six.*

*Three.* He looked at the sheet for some time. *Three is that both of those are the same object. It is not two problems. It is one object, and it has two ends, and I have been holding the comfortable end since November.*

He did not sit there long. He had never been a person who sat with a thing once he had named it properly. That was true at forty and it is true at eight, and it is arguably the most dangerous habit he owns.

*And here is the thing I have been walking round since October.*

*Inside the boundary I am the **Kotei**, and the word does everything. It opens the storeroom, it moves sixty people onto a set of steps in January, it puts seven house heads out of their chairs at nine forty on a Monday morning.*

*Forty kilometres out, it is a noise. Ise has never heard it. The Kasumi shrine keeps a register of **Honō** births going back to 1801 and there is no column anywhere in it for what a fishing town calls its Sky.*

*So the title is not currency. It is currency **here**. That is not the same thing and I have been spending it as though it were.*

*So. Who holds a register I can actually get into?*

**Not Ise. Not the shrine association, which would send me the same form with a different crest. Not the prefectural archive, which is legally worthless and historically fascinating and holds three copies of the wrong document.**

*Who keeps records on children taken by Flame families, and keeps them properly, and has kept them for longer than anybody, and does not care in the slightest what my paperwork says about me?*

He knew the answer before he finished the question. That is the trouble with the answer, and it had been the trouble with it for about four months, which is roughly how long he had been not asking it.

He got the writing box down.

The first letter he had ever sent to that address had gone out eighteen months ago and had been a justification. Nine pages, three of them clauses, explaining why the erasure of a famiglia sat inside a Sky's right of retaliation notwithstanding a declared neutrality. It had been an argument, and it had been a good one, and it had been answered in **four words**.

This one was going to be different, and he understood the difference perfectly and disliked it intensely.

*Last time I explained myself to him. This time I am going to ask him for something.*

*And he is going to say yes, eventually, because he is curious and because I have one thing he wants. And then he is going to name a price, and it will not be money, and it will not be anything I have thought of.*

*What does a man who cannot die want from an eight year old?*

He did not have an answer to that.

He wrote the letter anyway.

It took him until four in the morning and it went through nine drafts, and the drafts were the interesting part, because eight of them were arguments and he burned all eight.

The ninth was eleven lines long and contained no argument whatsoever.

It did not explain what he was. It did not justify anything, and it did not mention the Estraneo, and it did not once use the word **Sky**. It said that a child of the Saint-Exavier family had gone missing eight years ago, and that the child had a ranking ability, and that no register in four prefectures would open to the person writing.

Then it asked one question, and the question took him two of the four hours.

> *I am told you keep records of what was taken and not returned. I am asking whether there is a line for this one.*
>
> *I have nothing to offer you that you would consider payment. I am aware of this. I am asking anyway, because the alternative is to stop looking, and I find that I am not able to do that, and I would rather ask badly than stop.*

He read it back at four in the morning and hated it, and sent it anyway, because eight better letters were ash in a dish on the desk and he had run out of ways to be clever about it.

*He is going to answer,* he thought, putting the lamp out. *Not because of the child. Because that is the first honest thing anybody has put in front of him in a very long time, and he is bored, and being bored for four hundred years is its own kind of hunger.*

*And then he is going to name a price.*

Aldo Ferri was still in Namimori nine years later. By then he kept the shrine accounts, and was extremely good at it, and had never once been asked to explain himself to anybody. He learned the seven words in his second winter, off Ayame, over a ledger. He learned them in the order the town says them: **Ketsueki · Yasuragi · Chiyu · Hogo · Getsufuji · Gen'ei · Kotei.** He got them wrong for a year, and then never again.

And nobody ever came for him at all.

That is the ending nobody in that shrine office would have believed on the third night. It is also the reason the vote that Sunday is not remembered in the town as a difficult one… and why the file, when Taipei finally asked for it in eleven years, went out with a covering note four lines long.
