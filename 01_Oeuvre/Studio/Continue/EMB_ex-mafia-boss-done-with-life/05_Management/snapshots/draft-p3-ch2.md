# Chapter 2: Local High Priestess Sets A Place For Something That Does Not Eat. It Has Seconds

Sawada Musashi drew up the seating for the winter assembly thirty-one times. On the thirty-second attempt she put her brush down, looked at the paper, and said a word that would have got her struck across the knuckles at fourteen.

The paper was not complicated. It was a rectangle with the dais at the top and forty-one boxes below. Each box took a name. Each name took a flame beside it and a house beside that. The whole arrangement ran on rules so old that three of them no longer had reasons anybody living could explain. The Kurokawa sat below the Miura because of a dispute over a well in 1608. Nobody remembered the well. Everybody remembered the seating.

She could do it in her sleep. She had done it in her sleep, twice, and had woken up and found the finished chart on the desk in her own hand, which had frightened her rather less than it should have.

The problem was not the forty-one boxes.

The problem was the two boxes at the top, on either side of the dais. One of them now had to contain a man whose age she did not know, whose house she did not know, whose province she did not know. His species she was no longer prepared to guess at.

"*Gen'ei*," she said aloud, to the paper.

That was the other problem, and she had been refusing to think about it for eleven days. She had been refusing to think about it with a thoroughness that would have impressed anyone who did not know her, and that Maro, who did, had been enjoying since the fourth.

  

The rite of assembly was not a ceremony in the sense that outsiders used the word. It was an audit. Twice a year the flame-active families of Namimori came to the Sawada shrine, along with the four districts that had petitioned into its shelter. They stood, in order, in front of whatever the shrine currently had by way of a Sky. The standing was the point. You could tell everything from the standing. Who had lost strength. Who had gained it. Whose second son had come into a flame nobody expected and whose eldest daughter had not come into one at all. Musashi could read a room of ninety people the way a doctor reads a wrist. She learned it from a woman who learned it from a woman, back and back… to a girl who walked into an empty valley four hundred years ago and decided a town would go there. That girl was a *Kohaku* too. Everybody in the district knew it and nobody in the district said it, which was the local method for everything.

For six years the shrine had conducted the audit with a hole in the middle of it. The dais had been empty and everyone had looked at the empty dais and everyone had agreed, in the enormous cooperative silence that this town did better than anything, not to say why.

This year the dais would not be empty. This year there would be a *Kohaku* on it, aged six, in twelve layers of ceremonial silk he could not carry the weight of, and on his left and on his right there would be Guardians. Two of them, which was two more than the shrine had needed to seat in four hundred years. Musashi spent a week in the record room reading precedent and found precisely none. There had been *Kohaku* in this valley before, and they had died without Guardians, and the last one had died four hundred years ago.

Musashi picked the brush back up, because the chart was due at the second shrine in the morning and because a thing you are avoiding gets heavier, which was one of the four or five pieces of advice her own teacher had given her that had turned out to be true.

*Yasuragi*, she wrote, in the left-hand box, and then under it, smaller, in the formal hand: **Hibari Seiran, Seigyoku of the house of Hibari** [1].

That one was simple. Left was the senior side [2]. It had been the senior side since the court at Heian and would go on being the senior side long after everyone in this room was ash. And Indra had knelt in the snow in front of a six-year-old, and sworn in the old form, with her mother's blade across her palms. There was no argument to have. The Hibari had held the martial pillar of this town for four centuries. Their strongest living daughter had just put herself under the Sawada roof. If Musashi tried to seat her anywhere but the left, Indra herself would correct the chart, with a face like a shut door.

She looked at the right-hand box for a while, long enough that the lamp needed trimming and she did not trim it.

Then she wrote *Gen'ei* in it, and the brush stopped. Because she was *Gen'ei*. She had been *Gen'ei* for forty years, and everybody in the district from the fishmonger to the head of the Kurokawa called her that.

  

"You could use his surname," said Maro, from the doorway.

"His surname is Kawahira."

"Then use Kawahira!"

"Maro." Musashi did not look up. "Look at the chart. Look at what is written in every other box on it."

There was a pause. Azuma Maro, high priestess of the second shrine, forty-one years a friend and forty-one years a trial, came across the mats without being invited. She sat down on the wrong side of the desk and read the chart upside down, which she was better at than most people were the right way up.

"Ah," she said.

"Yes."

"Every single one is a flame name. *Ketsueki* of the Kurokawa. *Hogo* of the Miura. Every box on the page."

"Yes."

"Because that is the *system*," said Maro, with relish. She had spent four decades enjoying other people's problems. "Casual register uses the flame. Formal register uses the ceremonial name and the house. And the man does not have a house."

"He does not have a house."

"Does he have a ceremonial name? Every *Gen'ei* in the country has a ceremonial name, it is the one thing we are *given*."

"I have no idea," said Musashi. "I have asked him twice."

"*Twice*, and nothing? Gen'ei, I get more out of the Kurokawa!"

"And?"

"The first time he told me it was a very long story and would I like some tea, in my own house, from my own kettle. The second time he pretended to be extremely interested in the ceiling."

Maro produced a noise of pure delight and had to put her hand over her mouth.

"It is not funny."

"It is *tremendously* funny, Gen'ei, and you are going to have to bear it."

"I have a *Kotei* on my dais, a Hibari on my left, and a hole where a name should be on my right, and you are *enjoying* this."

"Enormously," said Maro. "It is the best winter I have had in years."

  

Here is what Musashi knew about the man.

He had been circling her house for a year. Not approaching. Circling, at a distance of about four streets, the way weather circles. She had never once caught him, and she had been looking, and she was not a woman who failed to catch things on her own ground. Twice in that year Maro's ring went hot enough to raise a welt. Twice Maro came across the district at a dead run in her house clothes, to report that something had touched the wards and *apologised*. Wards were not built to receive an apology.

He addressed her grandson as Sawada-san. Not *Kohaku*. Not *Tenmei*, which was the ceremonial name a Sky of a prominent house was owed, and which two of the four district heads were already using in letters. Sawada-san, flatly, like a man in a bank, and the boy had never once corrected him.

He did not eat, and he ate. Musashi had watched this happen four times and had not managed to see the seam. He would sit at a table and take food. The food would go. He would put the bowl down empty, and at no point in the process did Musashi's eye report that a person had eaten. It was like watching a very good conjuror shuffle a deck. You knew there had been a shuffle. You did not have the shuffle. Was it *Gen'ei*? She could not even establish that much, and she had been a *Gen'ei* for forty years.

His flame was *Gen'ei* and it was wrong. Not stronger. Musashi had stood beside stronger, and had married one, and had buried him. Ayame's grandmother was stronger, and could hold a full *Gen'ei* veil over the shrine approach for nine hours. Musashi felt that as heat and pressure and effort, like standing near a forge. What she felt off Kawahira was none of those things. What she felt off Kawahira was the sensation of a room being slightly larger on the inside than the outside… all the time, everywhere he was, and no effort at all. A *Gen'ei* that size should have been audible from the road.

And he had harmonised with her grandson before either of them laid eyes on the other. That was not how harmonisation worked. That was not how *any* of it worked. Nobody in the house would give her a straight answer about it either, least of all the six-year-old.

Musashi was sixty-three years old. She had buried a husband, driven off a daughter, and raised a shrine out of its own ashes twice. She had reached the age where very little in the world could make her afraid.

She was afraid of this.

Not of him. She wanted that distinction clear in her own head, because it mattered. Indra once told her that fear you cannot name will make your decisions for you, and hand you the bill later.

She was afraid of the *shape* of it. She had seen exactly one man attach himself to a member of her family. She watched that man be useful, and pleasant, and generous, and unfailingly correct. And she watched her daughter thin out like tea left standing, until there was nothing in the cup but the colour.

  

"He is not Iemitsu," said Maro, who had been watching her face and who had never in forty-one years pretended not to.

"I know that."

"Do you?"

"Maro."

"Because you have the chart in front of you," Maro said. "You have written the man's name in the box. You have been sitting here since the lamps were lit, and I have watched you *not* pick up the brush eleven times."

Musashi picked up the brush and set it down in the rest with a click.

"Iemitsu was useful," she said. "That was the whole of it. He was so useful that by the time I understood what was happening, everybody in this house could give me a reason he should stay. Every reason was a good one. And Nana could not give me a single reason she was happy." She heard her own voice go and stopped, and started again lower. "He never lied to me. Not once. I want that understood. I went back through nine years of it looking for a lie and there was not one. He was simply *useful*, and my daughter went quiet, and I did not notice until she was in Tokyo."

Maro said nothing for a moment. Then: "And Kawahira is useful."

"Kawahira," said Musashi, "is the most useful person who has ever walked into this shrine."

  

That was the part that would not let her sleep.

Eleven days ago the man had removed a problem that had been eating the shrine's income for two years. A Vongola audit clerk, third rank, entirely legitimate, was working his way through the district's shrine registrations with the patience of a man who intended to find something. Musashi fed him falsified paper for seven months. She knew, as one knows weather, that she would run out of paper before he ran out of patience.

Kawahira had listened to her explain this over tea. He had asked two questions. He had gone away.

Four days later the clerk had been promoted, transferred to a desk in Palermo, and married. Not by Kawahira, Musashi was fairly sure. But in the correct order, at the correct speed, with the sort of documentation that made further inquiry a discourtesy. The man even wrote a genuinely warm letter of apology for the disruption to his survey.

He also, in the same eleven days, identified two of the four wards on the east approach as decorative, corrected them, and declined to explain how he had known.

He taught Nagi a *Gen'ei* exercise Musashi was saving for the child's ninth year. The child asked him, and he had apparently not thought to check whether she was allowed.

And he mended the shrine's second-best set of ritual bells, cracked since 1971, by holding them and looking mildly embarrassed.

"He fixed the *bells*," Musashi said.

"I heard about the bells."

"The whole district heard about the bells. Kazuki has told the story eight times and it gets longer."

Maro's face did something complicated. "Gen'ei. May I be unpleasant?"

"When have you ever asked."

"That is the third time tonight you have listed things he has done for the house, and the tone you are using is the tone people use for *debts*." Maro leaned in. "Has it occurred to you that you are angry with him for being helpful, and that this is not entirely about him?"

"Yes," said Musashi. "About four days ago. I have been enjoying it enormously."

  

The thing was, the boy trusted him.

Not liked. Musashi could have worked with liked. Her grandson liked a great many people, in the general, careful, distributed way he did everything, and it cost him nothing and told you nothing.

He trusted this man, and Musashi knew exactly how she knew, and hated it.

She had a pseudo-bond with the child. It formed in the first month of his life out of sheer panic, hers and his. That is how those things go between two flame-active people frightened at the same time in the same room: her *Gen'ei* went looking for something and found a *Kohaku* the size of a loaf of bread, and neither of them had any say in it. It was the reason she could talk to him at all in that first terrible year, when he was a doll with the wrong eyes. It was not deep. It was not a Guardian bond. It gave her about as much as a hand on a shoulder gives you in the dark, which is to say: presence, direction, and whether the person is bracing.

Her grandson braced. Constantly. He braced when the district heads arrived. He braced when the post came. He braced, God help her, when she walked into a room. It was a fractional gathering-in that she was certain he had no idea he did, and that she had felt every day for six years, and had never once found a way to mention.

He did not brace around Kawahira.

She had noticed it at the fox wedding and had told herself it was exhaustion. She had noticed it again at the negotiation, and had told herself the boy was concentrating. She noticed it a third time on an ordinary Tuesday, with no ceremony and no audience. The man came up the path. Her grandson was on the engawa with his ledgers. And the small tight thing at the back of Musashi's ribs that was her grandson went *loose*, all at once, like a fist opening.

Nobody did that to him. Not Ayame, who had raised him. Not Kazuki. Not Maro. Not her.

"He is six," Musashi said, to the seating chart. "He is six years old and he has decided, and there is no mechanism in the world by which I get to overrule it. Not one. I have looked, Maro. I spent an evening looking."

"There is one," said Maro. "You could forbid it."

"And?"

"And he would say yes, Grandmother," said Maro, "and then he would do it anyway. And he would be *sorry*. And he would arrange things so that you never had to find out, and he would be very good at it… and you would spend the rest of your life not knowing which parts of your own house were arranged for your comfort."

Musashi looked at her.

"Yes," said Maro. "I know. I have met him."

  

She wrote *Gen'ei (Kawahira)* in the right-hand box, in a hand she was not proud of, and went to bed, and the next morning discovered that the man had come to breakfast.

  

Nobody had invited him. Musashi established this later with some rigour, going round the house one member at a time. The answers were all the same shape. He was simply there when they came in, sitting on the far side of the low table by the north window, in the place nobody sat because the draught came under the shutter. Hands folded. Looking politely at the middle distance, like a man who had been waiting twenty minutes and did not mind.

"Good morning," he said.

"Good morning," said Ayame, in the voice of a woman recalculating rice. She went out. She came back four minutes later with an extra bowl and set it down in front of him with a small hard sound.

That was the first day.

He came the next day too. And the day after. And by the sixth day the household had performed the manoeuvre it performed about everything. It absorbed the impossible thing completely, and at speed, and then denied with its whole chest that anything had changed. On the ninth day Musashi came in to find that the draughty place by the north window had acquired a cushion, and that the shutter had been mended.

"Who mended the shutter?"

"Kazuki-san," said Nagi, who was carrying folded cloth and had stopped walking in order to answer properly, because she answered everything properly.

"Why?"

Nagi considered this with the terrible thoroughness of a six-year-old who has been asked a direct question by the head of the house. "Because Kawahira-san sits there," she said, "and Kazuki-san said the draught was rude."

  

Musashi went and found Kawahira on the west engawa, in the afternoon sun, doing nothing whatsoever.

That was another wrong thing. Everybody in this house did something. Ayame moved through it like a current. Kazuki was in the garden from first light and half the night besides. Maro was never at rest even sitting down; her hands worked, her jaw worked, she was a woman who had to be actively prevented from reorganising other people's cupboards. Even the child sat with paper.

Kawahira sat with the sun on him and his hands on his knees and looked at the wisteria, and had apparently been doing so for an hour and a half.

"You are a Guardian," Musashi said, without preamble.

"I am."

"Then you understand that there are obligations."

"I have been told there are seven," said Kawahira. "I have not been told what they are. I assumed somebody would get around to it… and I have been enjoying the wait."

She sat down on the boards a little way along, not near him and not far, which took some doing and which he noticed and had the sense not to mention.

"Attendance," she said. "That is the first one and it is the only one people ever get wrong. A Guardian is not a title and it is not a favour. It is a *position*, and the position is physical. It means you stand where the house says you stand. At the rites, at the assembly, at the funerals… and at the table." She let that sit. "You have discovered the table on your own."

"The food is very good."

"You do not eat."

"Ayame-san's stock is four days old and she begins it again on the fifth. She has never told anybody that. I worked it out on my own and I have been insufferable about it for a week." He paused. "To myself. There was nobody to be insufferable at."

"I do, actually," said Kawahira. "It is only that I do not need to, and people find the distinction upsetting. So I have got into the habit of not drawing attention to whichever half of it is currently happening." He turned his head. His eyes did the thing they did, which Musashi had now seen four times and could not get used to. It was a slow adjustment of the light in them that had nothing to do with the light outside. "Ruriko-san. You have been walking around your own house for eleven days with a question in your mouth. Ask it, or I shall have to keep pretending I cannot see it, and I am not as good at that as you think."

  

She asked it.

She meant to ask it carefully. She had a version prepared that came at it from the side, which is how anything of this weight gets approached here. None of that survived contact.

"What do you want with my grandson?"

"That is not the question."

"It is *my* question!"

"It is a question you already know the answer to," said Kawahira, "and you are asking it because you want me to say something you can catch me on. Which is fair. I would do the same." He looked back at the wisteria. "Ask me the other one."

"Do not tell me what I am thinking in my own house."

"Then ask me the other one, and I will stop."

Musashi discovered she was standing. She had not decided to stand, and at sixty-three a person does not simply arrive on her feet, and the fact that she had was more informative than anything she was about to say.

"Fine!" she said. "Fine. *Fine.* You want the other one? Here is the other one. My daughter married a man who never told me a single lie. He was helpful. He was helpful for nine years. He solved problems for this shrine that I could not solve. Every one of them was a real problem. Every one of them stayed solved. And by the end of it my daughter could not tell me one thing she wanted that was hers. She went to Tokyo. I did not go after her, because I was *proud*, and I have had twenty-six years to think about that." Her voice had gone somewhere she did not use it. She let it. "And you have been in my house eleven days. You have fixed the wards and the bells and the shutter and a Vongola auditor. My grandson goes soft when you walk up the path, and he does not do that for me. He has never once done that for me."

The wisteria moved. Somewhere behind the house Kazuki was talking to something, in the low patient voice he used for trees and for nobody else, and a thread of *Chiyu* came over the wall with it, warm as a hand on the back of the neck.

"So," said Musashi. "That is the other one."

Kawahira was quiet for long enough that she began to be angry about the quiet as well.

"I am not going to reassure you," he said at last. "You would not believe it, and you would be right not to, and then we would both have wasted an afternoon." He turned his hands over on his knees. He looked at them as if they belonged to somebody he was thinking about. "I will tell you two true things instead, and you may do what you like with them."

"Go on."

"The first is that you are not wrong about the shape. Something did attach itself to your family because your family was useful. That has happened. It happened a very long time ago and it is still going on. And the person it happened to is not your daughter." He said it without any weight at all, which was how she knew. "You are looking at the wrong end of your own house, Ruriko-san. And I am not going to tell you which end is the right one. It is not mine to tell. Also you will find it yourself within the year and be furious about it, and I would rather not be standing there when you do."

Musashi sat down again. Slowly.

"And the second?"

"The second," said Kawahira, "is that I am extremely dangerous to your grandson, and it is not for any of the reasons you have been lying awake about." He smiled. It did not reach anything. "I am not going to explain that one either. But you asked me a real question and it deserved something real. That is the most real thing I have said out loud in a length of time you would find unpleasant to hear."

"*Is* it a lie?"

"No."

"Then why tell me?"

"Because you are the only person in this house who is frightened of me," said Kawahira, "and I have found, over a considerable period, that it is worth keeping one of those."

  

He came to dinner on the twelfth day, and the twelfth day was the one that mattered, and Musashi did not work out why until she was lying in the dark afterwards.

Dinner at the Sawada shrine was not a formal meal. That was a thing outsiders got wrong constantly. They came expecting the twelve layers and the incense and the terrifying old woman. What they got was a low table in a room with a bad draught, nine people, and Ayame refusing to let anybody help.

That night there were eleven, because Maro had turned up, and because Indra had come to deliver a report and had been captured.

Musashi sat where she always sat and watched a table she had watched for six years. This time she watched it with a stranger in it, which is the only way anyone ever really sees a room they live in.

Ayame served. Ayame always served, and Ayame served in a fixed order that she would have denied having. The child, the old women, the guests, the household, herself. It put a six-year-old at the head of a queue containing the two most senior priestesses of the district and a woman who could kill everyone present. Nobody had ever remarked on it. It had been going on since the boy was two.

"You are not eating," Ayame said to Tsuna, four minutes in.

"I am eating."

"You have moved it."

"That is a *stage* of eating."

"Ruriko-sama, he has moved it."

"I saw," said Musashi.

"*Traitor*," said her grandson, with enormous dignity, and ate some of it, and Kazuki laughed into his sleeve and got looked at.

Kazuki had brought persimmons, because Kazuki brought persimmons the way other people brought weather, and he explained them. He explained them at length. He explained the graft, the parent tree, and the year. The parent tree was a *fuyugaki* [3] and had come from a temple in Gifu in his grandfather's time. This particular batch had gone through a frost at exactly the right moment, which he described as luck, and which everybody present understood to be nine months of work.

"Try one!" he said to Kawahira, and then went scarlet, because he had said it in the voice he used for family.

Kawahira took one.

Musashi watched him take it. She watched him not know what to do with it for about a quarter of a second. Then he ate it, properly, visibly, with the seam showing, which she had not seen him do before and did not see him do again for two years.

"That is very good," he said, and sounded, for about a second and a half, like a man reporting something that had happened to him rather than a man being polite.

"It's the *frost*!" said Kazuki, at a volume that made Nagi jump and the fox come off the beam.

  

The fox stole from Maro.

This was not new. The fox stole from Maro roughly every other day, on the settled understanding that Maro was the only person in the house who would react. Maro reacted magnificently. The fox worked this out within a week and had structured its entire social life around it.

"*Ō-Inari-sama preserve me from this animal!*"

"Maro."

"It has had my egg! Gen'ei, it has had my egg, look at it, look at its *face*, it is not even ashamed."

"It is a fox," said Indra.

"It is a *criminal*."

"It is a fox," Indra repeated, "and this is what they are for," and went back to her rice. Maro made a noise like a kettle coming to the boil, and then gave the fox her other egg, and dared the table with her eyebrows to remark on it.

Nagi had her hands over her mouth again. She did that constantly now, and she had stopped hiding it. Musashi noticed in the second week, and marked it down privately as the single best thing that had happened in this house in six years.

  

Kyouya arrived at the point in the meal when nobody could get up.

He arrived as he always arrived. Through the garden, without announcement, in a coat that had been expensive before he did whatever he did to it. Kusakabe Tetsuya followed at four paces, and at least had the grace to look at the door.

He put a dead thing on the engawa.

"For you," he said.

Everybody looked at it. It was a bird. It was quite a large bird. It was also, Musashi noted with the part of her brain that had been raised in a shrine, extremely fresh and entirely undamaged apart from the one thing that had killed it. That was a level of skill she did not enjoy contemplating in a six-year-old.

"Thank you," said Tsuna, in the voice of a boy who had been thanking people for dead animals for some months and had got good at it.

"It is the second one."

"I know it is the second one."

"There will be a third."

"Yes," said Tsuna, "I did work that out, thank you, Kyouya."

"Hn," said Kyouya, and sat down at the table uninvited, and was immediately handed a bowl by Ayame, who had been feeding him since he was three and had never once acknowledged doing it.

Musashi looked at Kawahira.

Kawahira was looking at the bird.

"Is that," he said, "a courtship gift?"

"It is the second of three," said Musashi.

"And what happens after the third? Is there a fourth? Is this a *series*?"

"After the third," said Musashi, "the Hibari child will present a formal petition. In writing. To me, as head of the house. And I will refuse it, because he is six. And he will present it again every year until I stop refusing it." She drank her tea. "He has already told me to my face that he intends to live longer than I do."

Kawahira put down his cup. "How long have you known him?"

"Since he was born. His mother put him in my arms at the naming and told me his *Getsufuji* had come in at four days, which is not possible, and which I have never repeated to anybody."

"And is that the *usual* rate of progress, or is he ahead?"

"He is ahead," said Indra, without looking up, "and I have stopped apologising for it." She turned her cup a quarter turn. "His father was worse. My husband proposed to me in writing, at nineteen, in a letter which listed my faults, and I married him, so I am in no position to criticise the method."

  

And here is what happened, and Musashi did not see it happen, because she was watching her grandson.

It went like this.

Ayame said, "You have not had a second bowl." Tsuna said he was fine. Ayame said, "Ruriko-sama, he has not had a second bowl." Musashi said, "I know." Tsuna said, "This is a *conspiracy*," and got a second bowl.

Maro said something scandalous about a district head. Kazuki said something about frost. Nagi, asked a direct question by Indra about her *Gen'ei* exercises, went the colour of a lantern and answered in eleven words. She was then told, by the most frightening woman in the district, that eleven words was the correct number.

The fox got onto the table and was removed. It got back on and was allowed to stay on the understanding that it stayed on the west end.

And at some point in the middle of it, Tsuna said something dry, and Kazuki laughed, and Ayame said, "*Don't* encourage him."

And the boy who held up the sky over this town put his face in his hands and said, "I am *surrounded*."

In the exact voice of a six-year-old at the end of a long day. With no calculation in it anywhere.

Musashi's chest hurt so suddenly and so specifically that she had to put her cup down.

It was the first time in six years, and Musashi sat with her hand on a cup she was not drinking from and did the arithmetic twice, because she did not believe it the first time.

She counted back afterwards, lying in the dark, and made herself do it properly instead of comfortably. Six years. In six years she had heard her grandson be *funny* perhaps two hundred times. He was funny. He was very funny, in the manner of someone who has learned that a joke is a door you can put between yourself and a question. She had heard him be kind more times than she could count. She had heard him be patient in ways that had made her want to break something.

She had never once heard him complain.

Not seriously. Not lightly. Not once in six years, not through a broken wrist, not through the year the *Kotei* dome went up and he was ill for eleven weeks afterwards and nobody could tell her why. Not as children complain, which is not a complaint at all but a form of leaning. *I am tired, hold this, I am surrounded, look how surrounded I am.* You had to be very sure of a room to lean on it.

He had done it in front of Kawahira.

  

She looked up, then, because she wanted to see whether the man had noticed, and she caught him.

He was not doing anything. That was what she would remember. His hands were on the table. His face was doing the pleasant nothing it did. If she had not spent forty years learning to read the exact instant a face stops being a face and becomes a held position, she would have gone straight past it.

He was looking at the table.

Not at Tsuna. At the *table*. At Ayame putting down a bowl in front of a child in a fixed order she would deny having. At Kazuki explaining a persimmon to a room that had heard it. At Maro giving away her second egg. At a Hibari eating uninvited out of a bowl he had been handed since he was three. At a six-year-old apprentice with her hands over her mouth. At a dead bird on the engawa that was the second of three.

At a house that was closed around a child in eleven places at once, none of them on purpose.

And Musashi, who had spent eleven days terrified of what this man wanted with her grandson, watched him look at it… with the expression of somebody standing at the window of a shop that shut a very long time ago.

  

She got him alone afterwards, on the engawa, in the cold, because she was not going to leave it. The house was doing the noise a house does after eleven people have eaten in it, and there was a thread of *Ketsueki* coming out of the kitchen where Ayame had lost patience with the boiler again.

"You did not know," she said. "Eleven days in this house and a year around the outside of it, and you did not know."

"Ruriko-san."

"You did not know. You have been in and out of this house for eleven days and around it for a year, and you did not know what it *was*." She pulled her shawl up. "You thought he was alone."

Kawahira did not answer that.

"You thought he was alone," said Musashi, "and you were going to be the thing that wasn't."

"That is a very unkind reading."

"Is it wrong?"

"No," he said.

The wind came over the east wall and went through the wisteria, and something up in the town let out a single note and stopped, which was the Hibari changing watch.

"He is not alone," said Musashi. "He has never been alone. He has been *managed*, by a great many people who love him, and there is a difference. I have known there was a difference for about four years and have not been able to make it useful." She heard how that sounded and did not take it back. "Do you understand what I am telling you?"

"You are telling me that a house full of people can hold a child up perfectly and still not reach him."

"Yes."

"I know," said Kawahira. "I have seen it before. Twice." He was quiet a moment. "The first time I did nothing, because it was not my business and I had work. And I have thought about it for a great while since. I want to be clear that I do not think I was wrong, and that it has not stopped mattering. Those are two separate facts. They do not resolve into each other, no matter how long you leave them."

Musashi looked at him sideways.

"How old are you?"

"…Older than the seating chart," said Kawahira.

"That is not an answer."

"No," he agreed. "But it is the one that will fit in the box."

  

She rewrote the chart the next morning.

It took her four minutes, which was the humiliating part, because she had spent eleven days on it.

She did not move him to the left. Indra had earned the left and would go on earning it. There was no version of the world in which a woman who knelt in the snow gets displaced by a man who turned up to breakfast.

What she did was smaller.

Under *Gen'ei* in the right-hand box, where the ceremonial name and the house should have gone, she had left a gap for eleven days, because there was nothing to put in it.

She wrote three characters.

**Of this house.**

Then she sat back and looked at it and thought: *that is a mistake, and I am going to pay for it, and I do not know when.*

She left it, and blotted it, and put the chart in the lacquer box with two hundred and eight others, and went to see about the lamps.

  

"You will have to be called something," she told him at the assembly, in the four minutes before the doors opened, while forty-one families arranged themselves in an order set by a well nobody could find.

"I am aware."

"Are you? Because you have had eleven days to raise it and you have raised the drainage twice."

"Everyone in that room will call me *Gen'ei*. Then they will look at you, and there will be a silence. This town is very good at silences. They will decide something in it, and I will not be able to unmake it afterwards."

"Would you like to decide it instead?"

"I would like," said Musashi, "for you to have a name."

Kawahira looked at the doors.

"Sawada-san asked me the same thing," he said, "on the second day. Not like that. He did not offer me a name. He asked what I wanted to be called, which is a much worse question, and I did not have an answer for him either." Something crossed his face and was put away. "I have had a great many names. I have had so many that the process of acquiring another one is not, for me, the thing that it is for you. And your grandson looked at me for a while and then he said: *then we shall wait.*"

"He said *what*…?"

"He said, *then we shall wait*, and he changed the subject to drainage," said Kawahira, "and he has not raised it since. Not once in eleven days. He has arranged for a man with no name to sit at his table every night and be handed a bowl. He has not asked me a single further question about it." He was still looking at the doors. "I do not know what to do with that, Ruriko-san. And I would take it as a kindness if you did not tell him so."

Musashi stood in the cold corridor with forty-one families murmuring on the other side of a paper door.

She thought about a fox that refused a name for ninety minutes while nine people shouted suggestions at it. And about a child who watched that happen from the corner of the room, and learned something from it that nobody taught her.

"Kawahira-san."

"Yes."

"When the doors open," she said, "you will stand on the right, one pace behind the dais, and you will not speak, and when the Kurokawa look at you, you will look back."

"And if they ask my name?"

"They will not ask *you*," said Musashi. "They will ask *me*. That is the entire point of a seating chart."

  

The doors opened, and the cold came through with the smell of forty-one families' incense, and the *Kotei* dome over Namimori did the thing it did at the turn of the afternoon, which was to go the colour of a persimmon.

Her grandson went up onto the dais in twelve layers of silk that weighed more than he did, in a mask that hid everything but his mouth.

He did not stagger.

The whole room went down. And Musashi watched forty-one families discover, in the same half-second, that the rumour was true and that the thing on the dais was extremely small.

She heard the sound she had heard at the fox wedding. It was not a sound of awe. It was the sound of ninety adults thinking, in unison and against their will, *he's tiny*, and being unable to do anything about their faces.

The Kurokawa looked at the right-hand side of the dais.

"*Gen'ei* of this house," said Musashi, in the voice she had spent forty years building, "stands at the right hand."

And the room did what rooms do. It accepted a fact because a woman with a good voice said it in the right order, and moved on to the business of the season.

By the fourth day of the assembly two of the district heads were using it in letters. By the spring it was simply true. Nobody could afterwards remember it having been decided.

  

The audit itself took four hours, and Musashi conducted it, and for the first hour she forgot entirely that there was anything unusual in the room.

That was the thing about the rite. It swallowed you. The families came up in their order and gave their house and their flame and their count of the year, and you stood at the foot of the dais and received it, and the receiving was a physical labour that nobody outside the shrine had ever believed in. Ninety flame-active people in one hall put out a pressure like weather coming over a ridge. Somebody had to hold the shape of it, and the somebody was always the priestess, and Musashi had been doing it since she was thirty-one and had the shoulders to prove it.

The Kurokawa came up first, as they had since the well. Two *Ketsueki* and a *Hogo*, one of the *Ketsueki* newly come in and burning far too hot for a girl of nineteen, which Musashi noted and filed and would send Ayame about within the month. The Miura brought four and declared three. The old man from the Shimon district, who had walked here in bad weather at eighty-one because he had walked here every winter for sixty years, gave his flame as *Chiyu* in a voice that had thinned since the summer, and Musashi took his hands in front of everyone, which was not in the order of service and which she had done for him twice before.

The four petitioning districts came last, which was the humiliation and the point. Yokkaichi sent nine. Mie sent eleven and an apology. Suzuka sent a woman who could not have been thirty and who gave her house in the wrong form twice and then, on the third attempt, correctly, with her jaw set, and Musashi decided on the spot that Suzuka would get its shelter and that this woman would be the reason.

Through all of it the child on the dais did not move.

He was not required to. That was the shape of the rite: the Sky sits and the shrine receives, and the whole architecture of the afternoon is arranged so that the Kohaku does not have to do anything at all except be the fact that everybody else is arranging themselves around. Musashi had explained this to him twice in the week beforehand, at length, in the flat reassuring voice she used for terrifying things, and he had listened with enormous courtesy and had already known.

What she had not explained, because she did not know how to, was what the room would do.

  

It happened in the second hour, and Musashi felt it a good two seconds before she understood it, which was the correct order for anything involving a *Kotei* and was one of the reasons the rite had a priestess in it at all.

A *Getsufuji* from one of the outer families came up to declare. He was a big man with a Cloud's bad temper and a good enough reason for it, and he had come to this shrine for nine years and had stood in front of an empty dais for all nine, and something in him had evidently been saving itself up.

He got halfway through his house and stopped, with his *Getsufuji* going up around him like a hedge, and his second cousin behind him reaching out and then thinking better of it.

Then he put his forehead on the mats, which was not in the order of service either, and stayed there for a length of time that had the two Kurokawa girls looking at the floor.

The hall went absolutely silent. Musashi did not move, because moving would have made it a mistake, and it was not a mistake. She stood at the foot of the dais with ninety people's flames pressing on the back of her neck and understood, with a completeness that would keep her awake later, that the man had not planned it and could not have stopped it.

*Harmony* did that. Not the ceremony. Not the incense. A Sky in a room full of unbonded flame-active people is a slope, and things run downhill whether or not anybody has consented, and every single person in this hall had spent six years standing in front of the place where a Sky should be.

She looked up, past the incense and the forty-one bowed backs, at the dais.

Her grandson had gone very slightly forward. That was all. Six inches, in twelve layers of silk, with a mask on. And out of the small tight thing at the back of her ribs came something she had felt perhaps four times in six years, which was her grandson's flame moving on purpose, deliberate and enormous and gentle, as a hand goes flat when a horse is frightened.

The pressure in the hall dropped. All of it, at once, the way a *Kotei* does it and nothing else in the world does it, and ninety people breathed out together without knowing they had been holding anything.

The *Getsufuji* sat up with his face wrecked, and finished declaring his house, and went back to his place, and three people down the row moved over without being asked to make room for him in a spot that was not his.

  

Musashi looked to the right-hand side of the dais, one pace back, because she wanted to know.

Kawahira had not moved either. He stood in the position she had put him in, hands loose, face doing nothing, exactly as instructed.

But the light in the hall was wrong.

It took her a full minute to find it, and when she found it she nearly lost the order of service. Every lamp in the hall was burning normally. Every lamp in the hall was *also* burning about a tenth brighter than it should, evenly, in a way that no lamp does and no *Gen'ei* she had ever met could have held across a room that size for two hours without effort or attention or, apparently, noticing.

He had veiled the entire assembly.

Not to hide anything. There was nothing in that hall to hide. He had simply, at some point in the first hour, taken the ambient state of a very large room and smoothed it, as one might straighten a picture on a wall while walking past, and then gone on standing there.

Musashi finished the audit, having a great deal of practice at finishing things while carrying something she did not want, and she got through the last nineteen families without dropping a syllable of it.

But somewhere in the third hour she caught herself doing arithmetic she did not enjoy, which went: *if he can do that without attending to it, then in a year of circling this house he was never once trying, and I never once caught him because there was never anything to catch.*

  

At the close, the four district heads came up together for the shelter petitions, and this was the part with the words in it, and the words were four hundred years old and Musashi had said them two hundred and eight times.

"The Sawada shrine keeps the gate," she said. "*Kotei* sits. Who stands?"

And Indra said, from the left, in a voice that carried to the back wall without appearing to be raised, "*Yasuragi* of the house of Hibari stands. East gate."

And there was a pause of exactly the correct length.

And then, from the right, one pace back, a man with no house and no province and no ceremonial name said, "*Gen'ei* of this house stands," and did not elaborate, and the four district heads bowed to him as though he had given them a lineage.

Musashi kept her face, having kept her face professionally for four decades, through two funerals and one wedding she had not wanted to conduct.

Inside it she was thinking, with something she declined to examine: *he has a good voice for it. Of course he does. He has had time to practise.*

  

Afterwards the families went, and the shrine filled with that particular exhausted flatness that follows a rite.

Musashi found her grandson sitting on the steps behind the hall with the whole twelve layers still on, and the mask pushed up on top of his head like a boy who had been playing at something.

He looked about four.

"You did well," she said.

"I did fine."

"I said what I said."

He made a small noise. She lowered herself down beside him, which took a while now and which she had stopped pretending it did not.

"Grandmother."

"Mm…?"

"Thank you for the chart."

Musashi had been braced for a number of things at the end of a four-hour rite. That was not among them.

Musashi kept her eyes on the empty courtyard. "It is a seating chart. It is the least interesting object in this shrine."

"You wrote *of this house*," said Tsuna. "In the box. For someone who does not have one." He was picking at the edge of a sleeve, and stopped, because Ayame would notice. "That is not a small thing to write down. People will read that chart in a hundred years."

"Then they will read it and be confused, and it will serve them right."

He laughed. It was a real one, short and surprised out of him. She put it away with the other one from the dinner, in the small collection she had been keeping for six years and had never told anyone about.

"He is not going to tell me what he is," Tsuna said, after a while, in the tone of somebody continuing a conversation he had been having on his own for some weeks.

"No."

"Does that bother you?"

Musashi thought about it properly, because he had asked properly.

"It bothers me," she said, "that he is the only person in your life who has not tried to be useful to you."

Her grandson went very still.

She had not planned to say it. It arrived whole, as things do at the end of a rite when the flames are low in everybody. She heard it land.

And she felt the small tight thing at the back of her ribs that was her grandson do something she had never felt it do in six years, which was to *flinch*.

"Ah," he said.

"Masaya."

"No, that's…" He stopped. He put the mask down on the step beside him, very carefully, as one puts down something expensive. "That's a fair observation. I'll want to think about it."

"You will not think about it," said Musashi. "You will file it, and you will be very pleasant to me for four days, and I will not see it again."

"…Probably."

"Yes."

They sat there. Somewhere in the shrine Ayame was shouting at somebody about the good bowls. Somewhere further off the fox was making the noise it made when it had got into a room it was not supposed to be in.

"He came to breakfast," Tsuna said eventually, "and I didn't invite him. I've been trying to work out why he did that for eleven days. I have four theories and I don't like any of them."

Musashi looked at the top of his head. At the mask on the step. At twelve layers of ceremonial silk on a body that was going to have to grow into it for another forty years.

She thought about a man standing at a shop window.

"Try a fifth," she said.

"Which is…?"

"That it was warm," said Musashi, "and he had been outside for a great while."

  

Her grandson did not say anything to that.

But when they got up and she put her hand out for the mask, he gave it to her instead of carrying it himself. He had never done that. It was such a small thing that she did not trust herself to comment.

She carried it in. It weighed about as much as a *Kohaku* aged six, which was to say almost nothing at all, which was to say more than she could lift.

  

## Chapter End Notes

Ruriko is having a difficult month and it is only going to get worse, sorry Ruriko!

1. Seigyoku (青玉) - sapphire, lit. "blue jewel". The word for a Yasuragi/Rain flame *user*, as opposed to Yasuragi, the flame itself. Casual settings use the flame name, formal settings use the ceremonial name plus the house, and only prominent families bother with the third column at all. Indra is Hibari Seiran formally, Yasuragi to her face, and something considerably shorter to her son.
2. Left and right - in the Heian court order the Minister of the Left outranked the Minister of the Right, and flame society, which is nothing if not committed to doing things the hard way, kept the whole apparatus. Guardian positions at a formal rite are read the same way: left is the senior hand. Nobody has ever written down what happens when a Sky acquires his second Guardian before his first, and Musashi has now discovered why, which is that you have to make it up and then look confident.
3. Fuyugaki - the winter persimmon again. Kazuki will find you.
4. Hell Rings - the shrine's outer wards. They resonate rather than alarm, which means the person who feels it is whichever priestess is wearing the paired ring, which means Maro has been woken up at four in the morning by a polite man for a year and has developed opinions.
5. The audit - twice-yearly, and the standing order really is that old. The well dispute of 1608 was between two branch families that have since merged, and the well was filled in during the Meiji period, and the Kurokawa still sit below the Miura and will tell you it is about respect.
6. "Of this house" - 当家の. In the ceremonial-name column this is grammatically a house attribution, and Musashi has used it as one, and she is aware that she has just made a man with no history a member of a four-hundred-year-old priestly line by writing three characters on a seating plan at six in the morning while annoyed. It will come up.
7. Courtship gifts - three, escalating, presented without comment. The Hibari have done it this way for a long time and consider the written petition afterwards to be the romantic part.
8. Kyouya and Tsuna are the same age here. I know the maths in a certain earlier chapter says otherwise and I have made my peace with it. Two small predators sizing each other up is a different story from a big one adopting a small one, and I wanted the first story.
9. Pseudo-bonds are not Guardian bonds. They form between flame-active relatives, usually in a crisis, and they carry presence and direction and not much else. Musashi has had one for six years and it has told her exactly one useful thing, and she has been unable to do anything with it, and that is the most realistic thing about it.
10. Nana is not in this chapter and Musashi has not mentioned her since the third scene, which is deliberate, and which those of you who have read Part 1 Chapter 2 will find annoying, sorry.

Kudos to anyone who spots which of Kawahira's two true things Musashi has already been given the answer to, and by whom, and in which chapter.
