The voices stopped.

Byakuran had been reaching for the salt. That was the thing he kept coming back to, afterwards, when he tried to explain it to people who could not possibly understand. He had been reaching for the salt, and Bluebell had been arguing with Daisy about whether a hot dog was a sandwich, and Kikyo had been pretending not to listen while listening very hard.

And then the voices stopped.

Not quieted. Not muffled. He knew what muffled sounded like, he had spent years learning the difference between a version of himself who had gone silent and a version of himself who had simply gone. This was neither. This was a room full of people all turning their heads at once to look at the same door.

Three seconds. Maybe four.

Then they came back, all of them, all together, saying the same word in a hundred registers and none of them his own.

*Wrong.*

"Byakuran?"

He put the salt down. His hand was very steady, which was how he knew it was bad.

"Izu-kun," he said. "Where is he?"

…

Izuku found him in the east corridor, the one that went nowhere. It had been built to go somewhere, once, before the renovation, and then the renovation had happened and the corridor had stayed, and now it was a hallway with a window at the end and no doors along it. Nobody used it. Nam kept a mop closet there, out of spite for the architect.

The boy was standing at the window with his back turned.

Izuku stopped six meters away and did not go closer.

He was not stupid. He had spent enough of his life around impossible things to know that the correct response to an impossible thing was distance, and then more distance, and then information. So he stayed where he was, and he tasted the air.

There was nothing there.

That was the first wrong thing. Not that the boy had no Flames, plenty of people had no Flames, Mei had no Flames and Izuku could still find her in a crowded room by the shape of the hole she made in things. This was different. It was like putting his tongue to a battery and finding the battery had been drawn on the wall in chalk.

The second wrong thing was the hair.

"Um," the boy said, without turning around. "I don't think I'm supposed to be here."

Green. Curly at the back where it always went curly. The exact same cowlick that Izuku had given up fighting when he was eleven.

"No," Izuku agreed. "You aren't."

The boy turned around.

Izuku had been braced for it. He had counted the corridor. He had already worked out that he could reach the window in four steps and the boy in three, and that he had one glove off and one glove on and it would take him about a second and a half to fix that if he wanted to, and about half a second if he did not.

He had been braced for it and it did not help at all.

Freckles. The mole under the lip. The scars on both hands, but the wrong scars, thick and ropey and burned across the knuckles instead of the thin white lines that Izuku had collected from a decade of things going badly in different ways.

Fifteen. Maybe fifteen and a half. Standing in a hallway in a house in a country that would arrest him on sight, in a school uniform, with a bag over one shoulder, looking at Izuku with an expression that Izuku had last seen in a mirror at the age of fourteen and had spent every year since then learning how to take off his own face.

"Oh," the boy said.

He said it very quietly. Like the answer to a question he had been carrying for a long time.

Izuku put his other glove on.

…

They sat in the corridor. Neither of them suggested moving somewhere with chairs.

The boy talked. Of course he talked. Izuku listened to about a third of it and let the rest run past him, the way you let water run past you when you are trying to hear something further downstream. There had been a fight. There had been a warp quirk, or something like a warp quirk, or something the boy had assumed was a warp quirk because it had come out of a person and had done something to space. There had been a moment of falling.

"…and then I was here," the boy finished. "Wherever here is. Sorry. I really am sorry. I don't want to be a problem."

"You're a problem."

"Right." He looked down at his hands. "Yeah."

"Not your fault," Izuku said. "You're still a problem."

The boy nodded like this was fair. That was the thing that kept catching. He kept accepting things. Someone would hand him a piece of bad news and he would take it, carefully, with both hands, the way you take something breakable from a stranger.

"Can I ask something," the boy said.

"You can ask."

"Are you me?"

Izuku thought about it.

He thought about it properly, because it deserved that, and because he had learned somewhere along the way that questions which sound stupid are usually load-bearing. Was he. There was a version of the answer that was about genetics and a version that was about probability and a version that involved sitting down with a whiteboard, and Byakuran would have loved all three of them and would have gotten none of them right.

"No," he said.

"But we look…"

"I know what we look like."

The boy's mouth closed.

"You're fifteen," Izuku said. "So am I. You go to a school. So do I, on paper, on a set of papers that cost a lot of money and killed a man. You have a mother named Inko. So do I. We have the same face and the same name and probably the same blood type, and I have never once in my life met anyone with my face who was me."

"That's…" The boy stopped. Started again. "That's kind of a weird way to put it."

"It's the only true way to put it."

…

The boy asked about heroes, eventually. Izuku had been waiting for it.

"Is All Might…"

"Alive. Retired. Teaches."

The relief that went over the boy's face was so complete and so immediate that Izuku had to look at the window for a second.

"He's my teacher," the boy said. "Where I'm from. He's, um. He's kind of the reason I'm doing any of this."

"Mm."

"Is he yours?"

"No."

"Oh." A pause. "Who is?"

Izuku considered several answers. The true one was a man in a black suit who had once shot a hole through a wall four centimeters from Izuku's ear to demonstrate a point about posture, and who was, at this exact moment, somewhere in Japan pretending to be a substitute teacher for reasons that nobody had adequately explained.

"Nobody you'd like," he said.

The boy laughed. It was a real laugh, startled out of him, and it was Izuku's laugh, the one he had before, and hearing it come out of someone else's throat was worse than any of the rest of it.

"Sorry," the boy said. "Sorry. That was. Um."

"It's fine."

"Can I ask about Kacchan?"

Izuku did not move.

He was aware, distantly, of the corridor. Of the window. Of the fact that his left hand had gone still against his knee in a way that his left hand did when the rest of him was doing something complicated and had stopped issuing instructions downward.

"No," he said.

The boy blinked. "I just meant…"

"I know what you meant."

"Is he okay?"

"You can ask about anything else," Izuku said.

And that was the end of that.

…

Later, Izuku would try to work out where exactly the conversation turned. He would decide it was the gloves.

The boy noticed them around the forty minute mark. He had been looking at Izuku's hands on and off for a while, the way you look at a thing you have not been given permission to look at, and finally he ran out of whatever it is that stops people from asking.

"Is it your quirk?"

"Yes."

"Does it hurt? To use it, I mean. Some quirks…"

"It doesn't hurt."

"Oh, good." He smiled. He actually smiled. "That's good."

"It doesn't turn off," Izuku said.

The smile stayed for about half a second longer than it should have, the way smiles do when the mouth has not yet received the information the ears have.

"…Sorry?"

"It doesn't turn off. It has never turned off. If I take these off and put my hand on your neck, I will start pulling, and I will not be able to stop pulling, and the only thing that decides whether you are alive in ninety seconds is whether I decide to move my hand." Izuku said all of this in the tone he would have used to read out a train timetable. "So I wear gloves. It's not complicated."

The boy had gone very pale.

"That's…" he said. "How long has it…"

"Always."

"But you were a kid."

"Yes."

"You were a *kid*, you can't just, someone should have…" He stopped. He pressed the heels of his hands into his eyes, briefly, and when he took them away his face had done something Izuku did not have a name for. "I'm sorry. I keep saying that."

"You do."

"Someone should have helped you."

Izuku looked at him.

"Someone did," he said.

…

He did not explain who. He had gotten very good at not explaining who, over the years, and it turned out that skill worked on himself as well.

But the boy kept going. That was the whole problem with the boy. He kept going the way water keeps going, patiently, finding the crack.

"The people here," he said. "Your family. They're…"

"Careful."

"I wasn't going to say anything bad." He looked genuinely wounded that Izuku thought he might. "I was going to say they're loud. I heard them. Before, when I got here, before you came. Someone was shouting about hot dogs."

Something in Izuku's chest did a thing he would have to examine later.

"That's Bluebell," he said.

"Is she nice?"

"She's an S-rank villain who once put a district under thirty centimeters of water because someone insulted her hair."

"Okay," the boy said. "Is she nice, though?"

And Izuku, who had not been outmaneuvered in a conversation in two years, discovered that he did not have an answer that was both honest and safe.

…

"I want to ask you something," the boy said, near the end. "And I think you're going to be angry."

"I doubt it."

"Do you want to stop?"

Izuku waited.

"Being," the boy said, and made a gesture that took in the corridor and the house and the country and possibly the world. "This. Do you want to stop being this."

There it was.

Izuku had known it was coming from about four minutes in, the way you know rain is coming, and he had spent those four minutes deciding whether to lie. It would have been easy. The boy would have believed him. The boy would have believed almost anything said kindly, which was, Izuku thought, the single most dangerous quality a person could have, and also the reason that in this hallway right now there was exactly one of them who had killed anybody.

He decided not to lie. He was not entirely sure why.

"No," he said.

"…No?"

"I have thought about it," Izuku said. "I want you to understand that I have thought about it more than you have. You've had the thought maybe twice, and both times somebody said something nice to you and it went away. I've had it every day for two years. So when I say no, I'm not saying it because I haven't considered your position. I'm saying it because I have, and it's wrong."

"It's not wrong to want people to be safe."

"It's not wrong to want it. It's wrong to think it's available."

"You don't know that."

"I do, actually." Izuku leaned his head back against the wall. "That's the part you're going to have trouble with. I'm not a bad version of you. I'm not what happens when someone gets hurt enough. I looked at the same world you looked at, with the same face and the same mother and probably the same stupid notebooks, and I ran the numbers, and I got a different answer. And my answer works. That's the problem with it. If it didn't work you could argue with me."

The boy did not say anything for a while.

"Where I'm from," he said finally, "you'd be a villain."

"Where you're from, I *am* a villain. Where I'm from, so is everyone I love, and the word stopped meaning anything to me around the time I found out what heroes are for."

"That's not…"

"I'm not asking you to agree." Izuku turned his head. "I'm asking you to notice that I'm not confused."

…

The boy left the way he had arrived, which is to say without any explanation and in the middle of a sentence.

One moment he was sitting against the wall in the corridor that went nowhere, with his knees up and his bag beside him, saying *but what if there was a way to*, and then the sentence had no one in it.

Izuku stayed where he was for eleven minutes.

Then he got up, and went and found Byakuran, who was standing in the kitchen exactly where he had been standing before, still not touching the salt.

"Well?" Byakuran said.

"He's gone."

"Mm. They stopped again. About twelve minutes ago." Byakuran tipped his head, listening to something. "They're arguing about it now. Loudly. I'd rather they didn't."

"Byakuran."

"Mm?"

Izuku opened his mouth.

He was going to say *he had my face*. Or *he asked about Kacchan*. Or *he asked if I wanted to stop and I said no and I meant it and I have been standing in a hallway for eleven minutes finding out how much I meant it.*

"Nothing," he said.

Byakuran looked at him for a moment. Then he picked up the salt, and handed it over, and turned back to the stove, and did not ask again.

That was the thing about Byakuran.

He never made you say it.
